# Between Alert and Action

*Target, 2013. The story is that the alarm went off and nobody looked. Target's own testimony says something narrower and stranger: the activity was detected, logged, surfaced and evaluated. What happened after that is the gap nobody has filled.*

On the evening of 12 December 2013, the US Justice Department telephones Target, the American retailer, about suspicious activity involving payment cards used in its stores. Target opens an investigation that night. Within a week it will tell the public that the card data of some 40 million customers has been stolen from its checkout systems over the previous month. The intruders, it later says, had been inside since 12 November.

## The story everyone tells

Three sentences have carried this case for a decade. The first is a headline from the security journalist Brian Krebs: "Target Hackers Broke in Via HVAC Company", meaning the attackers got in with credentials stolen from a heating-and-refrigeration contractor that had remote access for business reasons. The second is from a Bloomberg Businessweek investigation, relayed by CBS: Target's newly installed security system from the firm FireEye "produced warnings" as the malware was being installed, and "the attack could have been prevented" if Target had reacted. The third is Wikipedia's compression: the team received the warning, had ample time, and did not act.

The first is true and explains a door. The second and third are the interesting ones, because Target's own account does not confirm them and does not deny them.

## What Target said under oath

In February 2014 John Mulligan, Target's chief financial officer, gave prepared testimony to the Senate. He described the scale first: Target's systems logged more than a billion technology events on an average day; tools narrowed those to a few hundred for the security operations centre; reviews produced dozens of cases a day for further assessment. Logging was abundant, filtering was happening, and people were reviewing what surfaced. The company also listed its controls, firewalls, malware detection, intrusion prevention, data-loss prevention, and noted that its systems had been certified compliant with the payment-card industry's security standard two months before the intrusion.

Then the sentence that changes the question. "We now believe that some intruder activity was detected," Mulligan said, and that it was logged, surfaced to the security operations centre, and evaluated by security professionals. With hindsight, he said, Target was asking about the judgments made then and whether different judgments might have produced different outcomes.

Read it carefully. Some activity crossed three boundaries: sensor to record, record to surfaced item, surfaced item to a human. What the testimony does not say is which activity, who evaluated it, with what context, what they decided, or what action followed. And it never identifies that activity as the FireEye alerts. It cannot be used to confirm the Businessweek story, and it cannot be used to clear it.

The competing account is the stronger narrative and the weaker document. The Senate Commerce Committee's staff report says FireEye generated urgent alerts, that Target's team neither reacted nor enabled automatic deletion, and that an alert with the generic label "malware.binary" may have looked like one more false positive. But the report attributes all of that to Businessweek, and says of itself that its explanation is "based on published media reports and expert analyses" and that the complete story might not be known until Target's forensic examination was finished. That examination has not been published.

So the counterfactual escalates as it travels. Mulligan: different judgments "may have led to different outcomes". The Senate staff: Target "could have potentially blocked" the malware's effect. CBS: "the attack could have been prevented". Each is a larger claim than the document beneath it.

## The mechanism: six boxes and two loops

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, renamed this category in its 2025 edition from logging and monitoring to logging and alerting, and its definition explains why: "Without logging and monitoring, attacks and breaches cannot be detected, and without alerting it is very difficult to respond quickly and effectively." A record is evidence. An alert is a request for attention. Neither is a response.

Draw six boxes: activity, event, detection, alert, decision, action. Under them, context and a clock. Each arrow is its own way to fail. Activity can leave no event. An event can omit who did it or to what. A useful detection can drown among low-value ones. An alert can reach the wrong queue. An analyst can reasonably file an ambiguous item with too little context. A correct decision can invoke a stale playbook. A containment step can fail without anyone hearing. Buying a better sensor repairs some of those arrows and none of the later ones.

OWASP names the later ones explicitly: cases where "appropriate alerting thresholds and response escalation processes are not in place or effective", and a detected alert that cannot be processed because its playbook is "incomplete, obsolete, or absent".

Then add two feedback loops the boxes do not have by default. From decision, send the reason back to whoever writes the rules: true positive, false positive, duplicate, insufficient evidence. From action, return proof: host isolated, credential disabled, process stopped, owner reached. Without the first, the rules never learn from judgments. Without the second, a ticket can close while the exposure continues.

Mulligan's testimony places facts side by side without connecting them. Detected, logged, surfaced, evaluated; then, weeks later, an outside phone call. Put a question mark between evaluated and acted. That question mark is the case.

## What Target changed, and the mirror at Equifax

Target later published a list: additional rules and alerts, centralised log feeds, more logging, application whitelisting on the checkout systems, tighter segmentation, reduced vendor access. These are recorded changes, not a recorded result. More rules and more logs improve coverage and also raise the judging load; a central feed preserves evidence but does not choose an owner or make a playbook work. OWASP's prescription reaches the missing interface: establish "monitoring and alerting use cases including playbooks such that suspicious activities are detected and responded to quickly".

Equifax, four years later, shows the same gap in a different place. The device inspecting encrypted traffic into the system that was breached had a security certificate that expired on 31 January 2016. The device stayed in the path; it just could not see. When the certificate was renewed on 29 July 2017, the team spotted a suspicious request almost immediately. By then the intruders had been in since May. The House Oversight report calls it nineteen months without visibility and counts at least 324 expired certificates across the company, 79 of them on devices monitoring critical systems. The widely repeated "nine months" is wrong against both the House report and the government audit office, which relays Equifax's own, different, figure of about ten months.

That was not an alert waiting for judgment. It was a sensor that had silently gone blind, with nothing to tell anyone.

## Is anyone getting better at this?

The best measured record is broader than either company. The incident-response firm Mandiant reports the median "dwell time" in its investigations, the interval from intrusion to detection, falling from 416 days in 2011 to 10 in 2023. A newspaper headline turned that into "orgs are detecting cybercriminals faster than ever". Two qualifications from Mandiant's own reports: in 2023, 54 percent of organisations first learned of the intrusion from outside, and in ransomware cases 76 percent of those outside notifications came from the attacker. A ransom note ends dwell time. And the median rose again, to 14 days in 2025. Detection speed is one interval on the six-box drawing, measured on a shifting set of cases. It does not say whether the action after the alert worked.

## The ledger

- **Target's security system produced warnings during the attack (CBS).** The Senate report relays FireEye alerts from Businessweek. *Holds.*
- **The attack could have been prevented if Target had reacted (CBS).** The Senate report says "could have potentially blocked" the malware's effect; Mulligan says different judgments "may have led to different outcomes". Neither addresses preventing the attack. *Overstated.*
- **The breach was an HVAC-company break-in (Krebs).** True as the entry; it omits the later warnings and the evaluated activity. *Missing.*
- **Target received the FireEye warning and did not act (Wikipedia).** Attributed to Businessweek by the Senate; Mulligan's statement neither confirms nor resolves it. Target's alert and case records would settle it. *Open.*
- **Equifax's certificate had expired for nine months (Wikipedia).** House report: expired January 2016, intrusion May 2017, nineteen months to discovery. *Wrong.*
- **Organisations detect attackers faster than ever (The Register).** Median dwell fell to 10 days, then rose to 14; more than half of detections came from outside. *Overstated.*

## What you can now check

Choose one detection whose failure would matter. Follow a test event all the way through your real system. Do not stop when it appears on a dashboard. Watch which context reaches the alert, which queue receives it, who owns the judgment, which playbook branch opens, and whether the requested action returns evidence that it happened.

Then inspect the silence around that path. What tells you the sensor has lost visibility? What tells you forwarding has stopped, a queue is unstaffed, or an action failed? The thing to test is not an alert rule. It is the closed loop from suspicious activity to verified response. If the test ends with "someone would notice", you have found the same gap Target's testimony leaves open.
