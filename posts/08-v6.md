# The Backup That Was Not a Backup

*Maersk and NotPetya, 2017. The story ends with one server in Ghana saved by a power cut. The record says the survivor was a live copy, not a backup, and that the difference is the whole lesson.*

On 27 June 2017, in offices from Copenhagen to Mumbai, screens at the shipping company A.P. Moller–Maersk go black and then show a ransom note. Within hours the company that moves roughly a fifth of the world's container trade has no working email, no booking system and no way to tell its port terminals which box goes on which ship. Its vessels keep sailing; its people revert to paper and WhatsApp. It will take ten days to rebuild the network and the company will put the cost at 250 to 300 million dollars.

The malware is called NotPetya. It arrived through a tax-filing program that every company doing business in Ukraine has to run, called M.E.Doc. It was made to look like ransomware, the kind of attack that encrypts your files and sells you the key. There was no key.

## The story everyone tells

The version that travels comes from a WIRED oral history, and it ends with a scene. Maersk's identity system, the Active Directory that tells every computer who is allowed to do what, ran on about 150 servers called domain controllers, spread around the world and constantly synchronising with each other. NotPetya wiped them all. Every one, except a single controller in Ghana, which had been knocked offline by a power cut and so never received the malware. Its disk was flown to London, and from it the company was rebuilt. WIRED calls the synchronising controllers a "decentralized backup strategy".

It is a good scene. It is also the wrong lesson, and Maersk's own engineers say so.

## What the people inside say

Gavin Ashton worked on identity and access management at Maersk that summer. His account, written as a participant and not a company statement, confirms the loss of controllers worldwide and the rebuild of the directory in a joint effort with Microsoft and partners. He says the first restored controller ran on a Surface Pro 4, a tablet. He does not mention Ghana or a blackout. Neither do Maersk's published reports. The scene may be exact; the fetched record does not confirm it.

Ashton does say the thing that matters. Maersk's disaster-recovery planning had covered losing a site or a data centre. It had not covered losing every domain controller everywhere at once. And WIRED, in the same paragraph as the Ghana scene, concedes the point: the controllers synchronised, so "in theory" each could replace the others, but that arrangement "had not accounted for every controller being wiped simultaneously".

That is the distinction. A live replica answers one question: can another machine serve the latest state? A backup answers a different one: can we recover a known earlier state after the latest state has become dangerous? Synchronising controllers are the first thing. Whatever survived in Ghana behaved like the second, by accident. The record shows no chosen restore point, no tested recovery image, no retention policy, no planned isolation. Accident supplied distance. Recovery still had to turn the surviving state into a working directory.

## How it got in

The folk version of the entry is "M.E.Doc's update server did it", and the technical record agrees on that narrow point. Cisco's incident responders traced every installation they examined to M.E.Doc's update system; Microsoft's telemetry shows the legitimate updater process executing a malicious command on 27 June.

But "the update server" compresses a staged operation into one bad download. Cisco, given access to M.E.Doc's engineers, logs and code, found that someone had stolen an administrator's credentials, obtained root access and changed the web server's configuration so that update traffic was proxied to another server. They also confirmed modified code inside three M.E.Doc updates, dated April, May and June, that could collect data and download and run arbitrary code. A US indictment, which records allegations and not findings, alleges the same sequence: update files altered on three dates, customer computers downloading them, the actual NotPetya held back until 27 June, and for several hours that day update traffic rerouted to a server in France from which the command to deliver it was sent.

So the compromised update was two things: a trusted foothold planted months earlier, and the delivery path on the day. And the ransom was not a ransom. The indictment alleges the payment screen was a ruse and files could not be recovered even after paying; the security firm ESET examined the disk code independently and found its changes made recovery impossible.

## The mechanism, drawn

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, has a category for this: "Software and data integrity failures relate to code and infrastructure that does not protect against invalid or untrusted code or data being treated as trusted and valid." The important verb is treated. An updater takes bytes from somewhere else and gives them the authority of locally installed software.

Draw four boxes: source code, build artifact, update service, client. At each arrow ask two questions. Did these bytes arrive unchanged? And were these the bytes the authorised publisher meant to release? An encrypted connection can answer the first. It says nothing about the second.

A digital signature is the standard answer to the second: the publisher signs a digest of the release with a private key; the client verifies it with the public key and installs only if it checks out. A changed byte fails. A fake publisher cannot sign. But signatures have a boundary of their own: if an attacker can change the code before it is signed, or steal the key, the signature faithfully authenticates malicious code. The M.E.Doc record shows compromised control points at several boxes at once: stolen credentials, altered server configuration, modified application code. A design that only checks which hostname answered has confused the update service with the artifact. A design that signs whatever the build emits has moved the question upstream, not answered it.

One widely repeated claim, that M.E.Doc used neither HTTPS nor signed binaries, is not established by the primary record. Cisco documents the compromised controls and never says whether clients verified signatures. It stays open.

## The defence is two drawings, not one

OWASP's first instruction is to "use digital signatures or similar mechanisms to verify the software or data is from the expected source and has not been altered", and to review code and configuration changes and control access to the delivery pipeline. Those meet Cisco's findings: the attack record is a chain of compromised decisions, so the defence is a chain of independently enforced ones. Keep signing authority off the update host. Record which reviewed source produced each artifact. Restrict who can request a signature. Give clients a deliberately distributed key, not whatever arrives beside the update.

The record is honest about what it cannot show: none of the documents says whether signatures would have stopped this exact chain, and none shows M.E.Doc adopting a new signing system afterwards.

Then the second drawing, the one the Ghana scene stands in for. Prevention and recovery solve different moments. Once destructive code runs with enough reach, another live copy is not a way back. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says recent offline backups are essential precisely because some malware "merely presents itself as ransomware"; copies should be separate, ideally offsite, and not permanently connected. Microsoft's own forest-recovery guide for Active Directory says to restore the first controller from a backup while the other writable controllers are shut down, so that dangerous state cannot replicate back in. That is a designed version of what the power cut did by chance.

What Maersk did afterwards is on the record only in outline: its annual report lists initiatives across cyber resilience, IT continuity and recovery, without naming an offline-backup architecture or a restoration test. The strongest measured thing in its record is organisational: manual workarounds served customers, vessel control never stopped, and cargo kept moving while the network was rebuilt in ten days. That does not make the interruption small. The 250 to 300 million says otherwise.

## The ledger

- **The synchronising controllers could back each other up, but not survive being wiped together (WIRED).** Ashton confirms plans covered a site, not every controller. *Holds.*
- **A blackout left one controller alive in Ghana (WIRED).** Ashton confirms the rebuild, not the place or the blackout; no Maersk document does either. *Open.*
- **M.E.Doc's update servers caused the initial infections.** Cisco traced every installation to them. *Holds.*
- **The updater lacked HTTPS and signed binaries.** Not stated in any primary record. *Open.*
- **"The NotPetya ransomware attack" (BBC).** The indictment alleges the ransom was a ruse; ESET found recovery impossible by design. *Wrong.*
- **A functioning network after about nine days (Darknet Diaries).** Maersk: "just 10 days". *Holds.*
- **It cost Maersk 350 million dollars (Darknet Diaries).** Maersk's annual report: approximately 250 to 300 million. *Wrong.*

## What you can now check

Choose one updater or deployment agent in a system you run and draw its promotion path. Start where a change is approved; end where a client executes it. Put the build, the artifact store, the signing authority and the delivery service between them. Then mark the first point at which an attacker controlling one box could make unreviewed bytes look approved.

Do the same drawing once more for recovery, but add time. Find the copy that remains usable if destructive code can reach every current peer through the same administrative paths. If the answer is another live replica, you have availability. You have not yet drawn recovery. The lesson from Maersk is not to hope for a disconnected controller in Ghana. It is to decide which state must survive outside the production trust boundary, and prove you can restore it.
