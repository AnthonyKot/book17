A Ranking of Tested Applications

The OWASP Top 10 is the list every security team cites. Its 2025 edition ranks ten classes of weakness. Here is what the ranking actually measures, what it cannot, and what ten famous breaches look like when each is read against its own primary record.

Somewhere in a security explainer published this year, a vendor tells you that the OWASP Top 10 "highlights the most common and impactful vulnerabilities, enabling organizations to focus their security work". The list is real and it is useful. That sentence is not what it does.

The OWASP Top 10 is a ranking, published every few years by a volunteer foundation, of the ten most important classes of weakness in web applications. Auditors ask about it, contracts cite it, and training courses are built on it. The 2025 edition is the eighth. This piece is about how it is made, and about a small experiment: take one famous breach for each of its ten categories, and read the story everyone tells against the documents.

How the list is made

Start with what the list says about itself. Twelve organisations, security-testing firms among them, "kindly donated data for over 2.8 millions applications". The unit of measurement is not the bug but the application: for each weakness type, OWASP calculates "the percentage of applications vulnerable to each CWE from the population tested by an org for a period of time". CWE is the Common Weakness Enumeration, a catalogue of named weakness types; the dataset covered 589 of them, grouped into ten categories after "several months grouping and categorizing" that the authors say "could have continued for additional months. We had to stop at some point."

How often a weakness appears within one application is deliberately thrown away: "Whether an application has four instances of a CWE or 4,000 instances is not part of the calculation." The reason is honest. Human testers list a weakness once; automated scanners list every instance. Counting instances would measure the tester, not the application.

The rank is then a formula with five terms: the maximum incidence of any weakness in the category, how widely it was tested for, two severity averages drawn from the public vulnerability database, and total occurrences. It is not a prevalence half and a severity half. And two of the ten seats do not come from the data at all. Eight categories are ranked from the contributed data; two are "promoted or highlighted by responses from the community survey", because, in OWASP's words, "examining the contributed data is essentially looking into the past" and "there are also important risks that we may never be able to reliably test". The two survey seats in 2025 are supply chain, "overwhelmingly voted a top concern" with "the fewest occurrences in the data", and logging and alerting, which "will always be underrepresented in the data".

What the rank is not

Three things follow, and each is visible in OWASP's own text.

Rank is not prevalence. The introduction gives average per-weakness incidence of around three to four percent for the top categories. The misconfiguration page says "100% of the applications tested were found to have some form of misconfiguration, with an average incidence rate of 3.00%". A category can touch every application while its average incidence is low.

The population is not yours. "The results in the data are largely limited to what the industry can test for in an automated fashion." The data cannot say whether a pipeline network, a compression library's release process or a fleet of kernel sensors was ever in it. And a category hides a range: injection "includes a range of issues from Cross-site Scripting (high frequency/low impact) to SQL Injection (low frequency/high impact)". The category's prevalence is mostly the first; its place in the news is mostly the second. OWASP calls its own stance "data-informed, but not blindly data-driven". So should the reader.

The categories overlap. "It's basically impossible to create ten categories without some level of overlap." The famous breaches show it. The Capital One story everyone tells routes through one weakness class, but the court record confirms only a firewall misconfiguration. MOVEit was injection, but the malicious input arrived through session state. CrowdStrike's crash is filed under exceptional-condition handling, but the fault was an out-of-bounds read the category does not list. A category is a lens: pointed at a real incident, it shows one plane sharply and the others blurred.

So what can a ranking of tested applications say about your application? Which classes of weakness the tools found most often, in the kinds of applications they test, weighted by how badly published bugs of that class have gone. And where practitioners think the tools are blind. It cannot tell you your odds, your order of work, or whether the category you fear is the one that will get you.

Ten stories against ten records

That is why the series this piece belongs to did the other thing. For each of the ten categories it took one incident, set the story that circulates against the primary documents, row by row, and set the attack against a real defence with a record. Each row got one of five verdicts: holds, overstated, wrong, missing something a defender needed, or open because no public document settles it.

Across the ten cases the ledgers hold 75 rows. Twenty-two hold. Twenty-five are overstated. Ten are missing. Eleven are open. Seven are wrong.

Read as a distribution: the folk stories are mostly right in outline and mostly wrong in the detail that would have helped. The wrong rows are rarely a wrong story. They are almost always one wrong word. "Encrypted" for hashed, at Adobe and LinkedIn. "Ransomware" for a wiper that could never decrypt, at Maersk. "Inactive" for a VPN account that accepted a login, at Colonial Pipeline. "Nine months" for nineteen, for the expired certificate at Equifax. A reader who trusts the headline will not be misled about what happened. They will be misled about why.

The missing rows share a shape. The headline names one object; the record shows its twin. Capital One's misconfigured firewall sits beside regulators' findings about controls and governance. The xz backdoor's version number hides that the trigger lived only in the release tarballs, never in the source tree. Adobe's properly hashed password system sits beside the reversible backup nobody deleted. MOVEit's first patched flaw sits before four more of the same class patched in five weeks. CrowdStrike's triggering update sits beside a five-month-old sensor defect. Reviewing only the object in the headline misses the twin.

The open rows are gaps at different stages: how Capital One's attacker got in, where Colonial's password came from, who wrote Apple's duplicated line, and what Target did between evaluating an alert and acting. Different documents would close each: trial exhibits, a forensic report, a version history, a security centre's case records.

And the defences divide five and five. Five cases have a measured observation: exposed databases counted, builds reproduced bit for bit, hijacking attempts sampled, cargo kept moving, detection times tracked. Five have a design, adoption or code change with no before-and-after outcome tied to it. A ranking of weaknesses exists. A ranking of what fixed them does not, and it could not be built from the public record.

The ledger for the list itself

  • The list highlights "the most common and impactful vulnerabilities" (a vendor explainer, 2025). OWASP's rank uses five terms including maximum per-weakness incidence, promotes two categories from a survey, and says exposure and impact "are specific to each organization". Overstated.

What you can now check

Take the list and find the category your own system's folk story lives in: the breach your team cites when it argues for a control. Then ask which object in your system has an operational twin. The backup beside the replacement. The release artifact beside the source tree. The second default beside the first. The step after the alert. The list's data is largely constrained by what can be tested automatically, and two of its categories come from a survey. It tells you where to begin looking. The record shows why to look beside the named thing.

The Door Colonial Could Not See

In May 2021 a ransomware attack shut Colonial Pipeline, 5,500 miles of fuel pipeline in the United States, for most of a week. The story of how it began settled within a month: one leaked password, a VPN account with no second factor, and a company that switched off its own pipeline to contain the damage. That version is true, and it is the least useful one. When the company's chief executive and its lead outside investigator testified to Congress a month later, what they described was not a password problem but a door: a remote-access path the company believed was switched off, that its penetration tests never found, and that accepted a username and a password anyway. By the end of this piece you will know how to look for that door on a network you run.

The headline

On 4 June 2021 Bloomberg ran the story that fixed the folk version: "Hackers Breached Colonial Pipeline Using Compromised Password". Its first sentence said the hack "was the result of a single compromised password". CNN carried it the same day, adding that the password belonged to "a disused virtual private networking account" without multi-factor authentication, and that it had been "part of a batch of leaked passwords found on the dark web, according to Bloomberg's interview with Carmakal". Wikipedia compresses it further: "a compromised password for an inactive virtual private network (VPN) account, which did not have multi-factor authentication enabled", in an attack that "afflicted computerized equipment managing the pipeline".

Four days later the Senate Homeland Security committee opened its hearing by repeating it: "Recent news reports indicate that hackers accessed the Colonial system through a compromised password of a virtual private network (VPN) account," its chairman said. The witness was Joseph Blount, Colonial's chief executive. The next day he testified again, to the House Homeland Security committee, beside Charles Carmakal of Mandiant, the incident-response firm hired on the morning the ransom note was found. Both transcripts are published by the Government Publishing Office. Everything below about how the attacker got in comes from them.

The morning

Blount's written statement gives the timeline. Colonial "identified the ransomware attack just before 5:00 AM Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) on Friday, May 7th, when one of our employees identified the ransom note on a system in the IT network". The employee told the operations supervisor at the control centre, "who put in the stop work order to halt operations throughout the pipeline". The shutdown began at about 5:55; by 6:10 "all 5,500 miles of pipelines had been shut down". The reason, in Blount's words, was "the imperative to isolate and contain the attack to help ensure the malware did not spread to the Operational Technology (OT) network, which controls our pipeline operations, if it had not already". At that hour "we did not know the point of origination of the attack nor the scope of it".

This is the first place the folk story bends. The ransomware was on the IT network, the office side. The joint CISA and FBI advisory of 11 May says DarkSide ransomware was deployed "against the pipeline company's information technology (IT) network" and that "at this time, there is no indication that the entity's operational technology (OT) networks have been directly affected". The pipeline stopped because a person with stop-work authority stopped it. Wikipedia's "computerized equipment managing the pipeline" invites a stronger reading than the documents support.

The FBI named DarkSide on 10 May; CISA describes it as ransomware-as-a-service, developers taking a share from the "affiliates" who deploy it, and no document here identifies a person behind it. Blount paid: "approximately 75 bitcoins" on 8 May by the Department of Justice's count, $4.4 million by the Senate's, a decision he said was his. The pipeline began returning to service "on Wednesday evening, May 12". On 7 June the DOJ announced it had seized 63.7 of those bitcoins, then worth about $2.3 million, from an address "for which the FBI has the 'private key'". It does not say how.

The account

Now the part the headline was about. Blount told the Senate: "we believe the attacker exploited the legacy VPN profile that was not intended to be in use". Asked whether Colonial had required multi-factor authentication before the attack, he answered: "in the case of this particular legacy VPN, it did only have single-factor authentication. It was a complicated password, so I want to be clear on that. It was not a 'Colonial 123' type password." And then: "in our normal operation, we use an RSA token allowance in order to create authentication difficulties for remote access." Colonial had a second factor. This profile sat outside it.

Senator Hassan put it back to him as "you did not have two-step authentication in place" and "a pretty old VPN". His answer is the sentence this piece is built on: "I did reference earlier that the VPN was a legacy VPN ... That we could not see and it did not show up in any pen testing, that is unfortunate."

Carmakal, the next day, was more specific. His prepared statement: "The earliest evidence of compromise that we have identified to date occurred on April 29, 2021. On that date, the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile and an employee's username and password. The legacy VPN profile did not require a one-time passcode to be provided." When the chairman called the profile a vulnerability, Carmakal corrected him: "let me, you know, clarify it as a misconfiguration ... what actually occurred was there was a legacy VPN profile that was in place that wasn't believed to be active, and that enabled an attacker to leverage both the user and the password to login."

Asked why there was no second factor on it: "it was not required for the specific VPN profile that was used for this specific account. It is because the account and the VPN profile wasn't believed to actually be enabled." Who had legitimate access to the password? "One person, as far as we know." Where else had it been used? "We do not know the exact source of the website ... we did find that it was one of the passwords that was stolen from another website. But we don't know exactly where it came from." How the attacker got the username, Mandiant "did not figure out". Blount's statement says Colonial was "still trying to determine how the attackers gained the needed credentials". No public document names the employee, and this piece does not either.

Set the two accounts against the headline. A compromised password: yes. A dark-web batch: the testimony is "stolen from another website" and "we don't know exactly where it came from". An inactive account: the record's words are "not intended to be in use", "wasn't believed to be active", "wasn't believed to actually be enabled", and it accepted a login. A company without multi-factor authentication: a company whose normal remote access used a token, with one profile that did not. The password opened the door. The record's subject is the door.

What the door is

OWASP, the open project whose Top 10 is a widely used list of application-security risks, files this under A07:2025 Authentication Failures and defines it in one sentence: "When an attacker is able to trick a system into recognizing an invalid or incorrect user as legitimate, this vulnerability is present." Its list of weaknesses includes the one the headline used, an application that "Has missing or ineffective multi-factor authentication." It also includes a quieter one that matches the testimony: an application that "Allows use of weak or ineffective fallbacks if multi-factor authentication is not available." OWASP's list is about web applications, and a VPN appliance is not one. The class of weakness fits anyway.

Start with what a password proves: that whoever typed it knows a string, not where they learned it. If the same string was used on a site that was later breached, it is in a list somewhere; OWASP's name for the result is a system that "Permits automated attacks such as credential stuffing, where the attacker has a breached list of valid usernames and passwords." A complicated password that has leaked does not need to be guessed. A second factor changes what a login proves, because a one-time code from a token or an app proves possession of the token at that moment, and a leaked password does not come with one. The profile Mandiant found asked for no code. What the attacker would have done had it demanded one, the record does not show.

Now the picture to hold in your head. Draw the perimeter as a table, not a wall. Each row is a way in: a VPN profile, a remote-desktop gateway, an API token path, a service account, a vendor portal. Each row has a column for what that path demands: password only, password plus code, password plus key. The security of the perimeter is not the average of that column. It is the weakest row an attacker can reach with a valid username. A VPN appliance is exactly such a table; "profile" is the appliance's word for a row. Colonial's normal rows demanded an RSA token. One row, the legacy profile, demanded a password.

And here is why it is hard to see. The row was legacy, was believed not to be enabled, and did not show up in penetration tests. The record does not say why the tests missed it or when it was created. It still accepted a login. Belief about a configuration is not a configuration. OWASP's phrase, "weak or ineffective fallbacks", is exact: the strong path existed, and a weaker path was still accepted.

What holds, and what wears down

Colonial's fix is in Carmakal's testimony: "The legacy VPN profile has been completely removed. So, a user, whether an attacker or an employee, would not be able to attempt to login to the system without requiring multifactor authentication." Blount's statement adds "additional layers of protection across our enterprise" and two named outside specialists brought in beside Mandiant. That is the whole public record of what Colonial adopted. It contains no measurement, and it cannot say whether the attacker would have found another row. OWASP's first prevention is to "implement and enforce use of multi-factor authentication to prevent automated credential stuffing, brute force, and stolen credential reuse attacks"; CISA and the FBI, four days after the ransom note, put "Require multi-factor authentication for remote access to OT and IT networks" at the top of their list. Colonial's normal remote access already did that. The legacy profile did not.

The best-documented record of a second factor at scale is Google's, and it needs careful reading. In July 2018 Krebs on Security reported that Google "has not had any of its 85,000+ employees successfully phished on their work-related accounts since early 2017", when it began requiring physical security keys. The spokesperson's own words were narrower: "We have had no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys at Google." That is a qualified statement to a journalist; no Google publication supporting it is in the record.

The published measurement came in 2019, when researchers from Google, NYU and UCSD evaluated over 350,000 real hijacking attempts against Google accounts. For security keys the paper reports 100% of bot attacks and 100% of phishing attacks prevented, with confidence intervals of plus or minus 25 and 28 points, and gives no figure at all for targeted attacks. Google's blog post on the study says "zero users that exclusively use security keys fell victim to targeted phishing during our investigation"; on-device prompts prevented 90% of targeted attacks and SMS codes 76%. The authors' conclusion is that for at-risk users "security keys offer the best immediate solution".

Why keys rather than codes? A one-time code can be relayed: a phishing page asks for the password and the code and forwards both to the real site. Google's 2016 security-key paper says one-time passcodes "as a second factor are still vulnerable to relatively common attacks such as phishing". A security key instead signs a challenge with a key bound to the site's origin. The W3C's WebAuthn standard states the rule: a credential "can only be accessed by origins belonging to that Relying Party. This scoping is enforced jointly by conforming User Agents and authenticators." A phishing site at the wrong origin cannot obtain a signature for the right one.

Then the limit, and it is the one to remember alongside Colonial. Krebs's 2018 piece stated the folk theory of two-factor: "even if thieves manage to phish or steal your password, they still cannot log in to your account unless they also hack or possess that second factor." In September 2022 Uber published a post-incident statement. A contractor's corporate password had likely been "purchased" on the dark web after malware infected a personal device. "The attacker then repeatedly tried to log in to the contractor's Uber account. Each time, the contractor received a two-factor login approval request, which initially blocked access. Eventually, however, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker successfully logged in." The second factor was present, enforced and held by the right person. It failed because it was a question put to a human, over and over, until the answer changed. A push prompt proves possession of a phone; it does not prove that the person holding it is the one typing the password. A security key that tests for user presence while signing the challenge from the browser making the request offers no separate approval button for someone else's login. Nothing in the Colonial record suggests this would have happened there. Nothing rules it out.

The regulator's answer is worth one paragraph, because it shows what a rule can and cannot do. Before May 2021, the GAO says, TSA's pipeline efforts included "voluntary security guidelines and security reviews". Colonial had one such review scheduled for the end of July; Blount told the Senate it "probably would not have resulted in finding that legacy VPN. Again, they do not actually go into the system. It is a questionnaire format type thing." TSA's two directives of 26 May and 19 July 2021 then required incident reporting within 12 hours, a designated coordinator, a self-assessment, mitigation measures, a contingency plan and an annual third-party architecture review. The July directive's original text is unpublished security-sensitive information; its 2024 renewal, which says it continues measures "first issued by TSA on July 26, 2021", requires "Multi-factor authentication, or other logical and physical security controls that supplement password authentication to provide risk mitigation commensurate to multi-factor authentication". A directive can require a factor, with a compensating-controls clause. It cannot find a profile believed disabled. Only an inventory can.

What to check tomorrow

The check, the one thing to carry from this to a system you run, is not a password audit. Build the table. Ask the VPN appliance, the identity provider, the gateway, whatever accepts logins from outside, for every profile, group, policy or connection path that will accept a login, and write down what each one demands. Then test the rows documented as disabled, by trying them. Colonial's profile "did not show up in any pen testing"; the record does not say why, and for your purposes it does not need to. The row you are looking for was created before your current second factor, is no longer understood by anyone who still works there, and is believed off without evidence of a refused login.

Blount said Colonial "could not see" its profile. The table is how you see yours. Every row that answers to a password alone is a door, whether or not anyone remembers building it.

The Backup That Was Not a Backup

Maersk and NotPetya, 2017. The story ends with one server in Ghana saved by a power cut. The record says the survivor was a live copy, not a backup, and that the difference is the whole lesson.

On 27 June 2017, in offices from Copenhagen to Mumbai, screens at the shipping company A.P. Moller–Maersk go black and then show a ransom note. Within hours the company that moves roughly a fifth of the world's container trade has no working email, no booking system and no way to tell its port terminals which box goes on which ship. Its vessels keep sailing; its people revert to paper and WhatsApp. It will take ten days to rebuild the network and the company will put the cost at 250 to 300 million dollars.

The malware is called NotPetya. It arrived through a tax-filing program that every company doing business in Ukraine has to run, called M.E.Doc. It was made to look like ransomware, the kind of attack that encrypts your files and sells you the key. There was no key.

The story everyone tells

The version that travels comes from a WIRED oral history, and it ends with a scene. Maersk's identity system, the Active Directory that tells every computer who is allowed to do what, ran on about 150 servers called domain controllers, spread around the world and constantly synchronising with each other. NotPetya wiped them all. Every one, except a single controller in Ghana, which had been knocked offline by a power cut and so never received the malware. Its disk was flown to London, and from it the company was rebuilt. WIRED calls the synchronising controllers a "decentralized backup strategy".

It is a good scene. It is also the wrong lesson, and Maersk's own engineers say so.

What the people inside say

Gavin Ashton worked on identity and access management at Maersk that summer. His account, written as a participant and not a company statement, confirms the loss of controllers worldwide and the rebuild of the directory in a joint effort with Microsoft and partners. He says the first restored controller ran on a Surface Pro 4, a tablet. He does not mention Ghana or a blackout. Neither do Maersk's published reports. The scene may be exact; the fetched record does not confirm it.

Ashton does say the thing that matters. Maersk's disaster-recovery planning had covered losing a site or a data centre. It had not covered losing every domain controller everywhere at once. And WIRED, in the same paragraph as the Ghana scene, concedes the point: the controllers synchronised, so "in theory" each could replace the others, but that arrangement "had not accounted for every controller being wiped simultaneously".

That is the distinction. A live replica answers one question: can another machine serve the latest state? A backup answers a different one: can we recover a known earlier state after the latest state has become dangerous? Synchronising controllers are the first thing. Whatever survived in Ghana behaved like the second, by accident. The record shows no chosen restore point, no tested recovery image, no retention policy, no planned isolation. Accident supplied distance. Recovery still had to turn the surviving state into a working directory.

How it got in

The folk version of the entry is "M.E.Doc's update server did it", and the technical record agrees on that narrow point. Cisco's incident responders traced every installation they examined to M.E.Doc's update system; Microsoft's telemetry shows the legitimate updater process executing a malicious command on 27 June.

But "the update server" compresses a staged operation into one bad download. Cisco, given access to M.E.Doc's engineers, logs and code, found that someone had stolen an administrator's credentials, obtained root access and changed the web server's configuration so that update traffic was proxied to another server. They also confirmed modified code inside three M.E.Doc updates, dated April, May and June, that could collect data and download and run arbitrary code. A US indictment, which records allegations and not findings, alleges the same sequence: update files altered on three dates, customer computers downloading them, the actual NotPetya held back until 27 June, and for several hours that day update traffic rerouted to a server in France from which the command to deliver it was sent.

So the compromised update was two things: a trusted foothold planted months earlier, and the delivery path on the day. And the ransom was not a ransom. The indictment alleges the payment screen was a ruse and files could not be recovered even after paying; the security firm ESET examined the disk code independently and found its changes made recovery impossible.

The mechanism, drawn

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, has a category for this: "Software and data integrity failures relate to code and infrastructure that does not protect against invalid or untrusted code or data being treated as trusted and valid." The important verb is treated. An updater takes bytes from somewhere else and gives them the authority of locally installed software.

Draw four boxes: source code, build artifact, update service, client. At each arrow ask two questions. Did these bytes arrive unchanged? And were these the bytes the authorised publisher meant to release? An encrypted connection can answer the first. It says nothing about the second.

A digital signature is the standard answer to the second: the publisher signs a digest of the release with a private key; the client verifies it with the public key and installs only if it checks out. A changed byte fails. A fake publisher cannot sign. But signatures have a boundary of their own: if an attacker can change the code before it is signed, or steal the key, the signature faithfully authenticates malicious code. The M.E.Doc record shows compromised control points at several boxes at once: stolen credentials, altered server configuration, modified application code. A design that only checks which hostname answered has confused the update service with the artifact. A design that signs whatever the build emits has moved the question upstream, not answered it.

One widely repeated claim, that M.E.Doc used neither HTTPS nor signed binaries, is not established by the primary record. Cisco documents the compromised controls and never says whether clients verified signatures. It stays open.

The defence is two drawings, not one

OWASP's first instruction is to "use digital signatures or similar mechanisms to verify the software or data is from the expected source and has not been altered", and to review code and configuration changes and control access to the delivery pipeline. Those meet Cisco's findings: the attack record is a chain of compromised decisions, so the defence is a chain of independently enforced ones. Keep signing authority off the update host. Record which reviewed source produced each artifact. Restrict who can request a signature. Give clients a deliberately distributed key, not whatever arrives beside the update.

The record is honest about what it cannot show: none of the documents says whether signatures would have stopped this exact chain, and none shows M.E.Doc adopting a new signing system afterwards.

Then the second drawing, the one the Ghana scene stands in for. Prevention and recovery solve different moments. Once destructive code runs with enough reach, another live copy is not a way back. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says recent offline backups are essential precisely because some malware "merely presents itself as ransomware"; copies should be separate, ideally offsite, and not permanently connected. Microsoft's own forest-recovery guide for Active Directory says to restore the first controller from a backup while the other writable controllers are shut down, so that dangerous state cannot replicate back in. That is a designed version of what the power cut did by chance.

What Maersk did afterwards is on the record only in outline: its annual report lists initiatives across cyber resilience, IT continuity and recovery, without naming an offline-backup architecture or a restoration test. The strongest measured thing in its record is organisational: manual workarounds served customers, vessel control never stopped, and cargo kept moving while the network was rebuilt in ten days. That does not make the interruption small. The 250 to 300 million says otherwise.

The ledger

  • The synchronising controllers could back each other up, but not survive being wiped together (WIRED). Ashton confirms plans covered a site, not every controller. Holds.
  • A blackout left one controller alive in Ghana (WIRED). Ashton confirms the rebuild, not the place or the blackout; no Maersk document does either. Open.
  • M.E.Doc's update servers caused the initial infections. Cisco traced every installation to them. Holds.
  • The updater lacked HTTPS and signed binaries. Not stated in any primary record. Open.
  • "The NotPetya ransomware attack" (BBC). The indictment alleges the ransom was a ruse; ESET found recovery impossible by design. Wrong.
  • A functioning network after about nine days (Darknet Diaries). Maersk: "just 10 days". Holds.
  • It cost Maersk 350 million dollars (Darknet Diaries). Maersk's annual report: approximately 250 to 300 million. Wrong.

What you can now check

Choose one updater or deployment agent in a system you run and draw its promotion path. Start where a change is approved; end where a client executes it. Put the build, the artifact store, the signing authority and the delivery service between them. Then mark the first point at which an attacker controlling one box could make unreviewed bytes look approved.

Do the same drawing once more for recovery, but add time. Find the copy that remains usable if destructive code can reach every current peer through the same administrative paths. If the answer is another live replica, you have availability. You have not yet drawn recovery. The lesson from Maersk is not to hope for a disconnected controller in Ghana. It is to decide which state must survive outside the production trust boundary, and prove you can restore it.

The Backup That Outlived the Fix

Adobe, 2013. The story says the passwords were never properly protected. The regulators say the proper system had been running for three years. What the attackers took was the copy nobody had deleted.

On 17 September 2013, somebody inside Adobe's network notices an attacker trying to decrypt credit-card numbers in a customer order database. That is how the company finds out. The attacker has been in since 30 August, entering through a public-facing web server and moving from it to other servers. Sixteen days later, on 3 October, Adobe's chief security officer writes that attackers had "accessed Adobe customer IDs and encrypted passwords on our systems".

The passwords are what the world remembers. The database that leaked, and soon circulated freely, held about 150 million accounts. Because of how it had been stored, anyone who read it could see which users shared a password, and a column of plain-text "password hints" sat beside the scrambled values. Security researchers treated the dump as a crossword: the hints were the clues, and the grid told you which clues had the same answer. Within weeks the hundred most common passwords had been worked out, along with the accounts that used them.

The story everyone tells

If you have heard of this breach, you have heard some version of this. Have I Been Pwned, the site people use to check whether their email has leaked, describes 153 million accounts, "each containing an internal ID, username, email, encrypted password and a password hint in plain text", and adds: "The password cryptography was poorly done and many were quickly resolved back to plain text". Wikipedia, citing a password-manager company, says Adobe "failed to use best practices for securing the passwords and has not salted them".

Every sentence there describes the data that leaked. None describes the system that was supposed to have replaced it. That system is in the record, and it changes what the lesson is.

What the regulators found

Three privacy regulators investigated, in Ireland, Canada and Australia, and published reports. Adobe's European operation is in Ireland, and the Irish investigators asked the obvious question: why were passwords stored "in an encrypted manner rather that hashed and salted"? The answer they recorded is the hinge of the story. Adobe "was actually hashing and salting passwords within a new system for a number of years prior to the discovery of the security breach". It had "decided to also keep the database in the old system as a backup measure in case of issues with the new system".

The Australian report dates it. Adobe "introduced a new system in April 2010 as a more secure means of authenticating users than the encrypted passwords stored in the backup system". Adobe told the Commissioner that for more than a year its authentication system had "cryptographically hashed customer passwords using the SHA-256 algorithm, including salting the passwords and iterating the hash more than 1,000 times". Those are the words a security engineer wants to hear: hashed, salted, iterated.

And then, from the same report: "The attacker took a copy of a backup database containing the personal information of customers." The Irish report says the same: "Attackers identified and removed data from a backup server that stored the compromised data." The fix had shipped in 2010. The data it replaced was still on a server in 2013, and it was the data the attackers copied.

The backup was not stale. The Australian count is precise: 1,787,100 Australian users "whose current password data was involved", where current means "a password that was valid against Adobe's production authentication system". Another 218,750 had obsolete password data in the same database. For most of the people counted, the password sitting in reversible form on the backup still opened their account.

Why the dump was a crossword

Two words carry the technical finding, and both are worth understanding even if you never write code, because the same mistake appears in systems you use.

The first is encryption. A password store has one job: to let a server check your password without being able to produce it. Encryption cannot do that job, because encryption is reversible by design. Whoever holds the key can read every password, and the server has to hold the key to check anything. The right tool is a hash, a one-way function. The server stores the hash of your password, computes the hash again when you log in, and compares. Nobody, the server included, can walk back from the stored value to the password except by guessing.

Adobe's old system encrypted, with an algorithm called Triple DES. The second word is the mode it used. Triple DES works on fixed-size blocks of input, and in the mode Adobe chose, every block is scrambled independently under the same key. So the same input always produces the same output. The Australian report says exactly this without the jargon: "common passwords shared by different users had the same ciphertext representation."

Draw the table and the problem is visible without any cryptography:

user        stored value (encrypted)    hint (plain text)
alice@…     2fca9b003de39278            "my dog"
bob@…       2fca9b003de39278            "the usual"
carol@…     2fca9b003de39278            "rex plus year"
dave@…      e2a311d0ff1c5a17            (none)

That is the shape, not real data. Three people with the same password have the same stored value. Anyone reading the table can group users by that value without knowing the key, then read every hint in the group. One careless hint anywhere in the group gives away the password for all of them. The Canadian report describes it in a sentence: "the plain text hints sometimes contained the password itself (or a very obvious hint)", and "the encryption format used by Adobe scrambled identical passwords in the same way", so it "would be relatively easy to obtain access to several different accounts where the same password was used".

Note what this did not need. No report says the encryption key was recovered. The crossword was solved with the clues, not the cipher. And the hints were not universal: the Irish report puts them at "a small percentage of users". A small percentage of 150 million is enough.

The fix, and why it was already built

The proper store differs in two ways. A salt is a random value stored per user and mixed into the hash, so that equal passwords no longer produce equal stored values. The US standards body puts the purpose in one clause: "to prevent multiple subscriber accounts from having the same hashed password". A work factor makes each guess slow, "to make each password guess more expensive for an attacker who has obtained a hashed password file".

Adobe's 2010 system had both properties in its description. The OWASP Top 10, the list of the ten most common classes of web-application weakness that this case belongs to under "Cryptographic Failures", gives the same instruction in one line: "Store passwords using strong adaptive and salted hashing functions with a work factor (delay factor), such as Argon2, yescrypt, scrypt or PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512."

So the engineering was not the failure. Something more ordinary was. Encryption feels like protection, and for data you must read back, such as card numbers, it is. A team that has encrypted one table encrypts the next with the same library, and a store that decrypts correctly passes every test a store can pass. The hints were a product feature. And once the new system worked, the old database was kept "in case of issues". The Canadian investigators' list of concerns reads like a description of that decision: "password management, outdated software, redundant systems containing personal information ... retention of data for longer than necessary". Some of those systems, they noted, "had been inherited as a result of absorbing other companies".

The sentence written for this case

The Australian Commissioner found that Adobe "breached NPP 4 by failing to take reasonable steps to protect all of the personal information it held", and then wrote a sentence that applies far beyond Adobe. The obligation "will not be satisfied if an organisation has adequate security measures in place to protect personal information stored in one area of its systems" but "does not implement these measures in relation to all of the personal information that it holds".

That is the rule. Security in one place does not count if the same data sits unprotected in another. A migration is finished when the old store is gone, not when the new one works. The Irish finding says it in a regulator's register: the transition "could have been achieved more effectively and expeditiously than was the case". OWASP says it in plainer words: "Don't store sensitive data unnecessarily. Discard it as soon as possible."

What Adobe did afterward is on the record. Australia lists "decommissioning the affected server and abolishing the use of password hints". Canada lists "a reduction in the retention period for customers' payment data" and enhancements it could not describe because they were commercially sensitive. The hint feature is now forbidden outright by the US standard: a verifier "SHALL NOT permit the subscriber to store a hint ... that is accessible to an unauthenticated claimant". What no report contains is a check that no other copy of the reversible database existed. That question stays open.

The mirror: LinkedIn

A year earlier, LinkedIn had fallen a different way. On 6 June 2012 it "became aware" of "the theft of approximately 6.5 million passwords". Those were not encrypted; they were hashed. But a later paper identifies them as "unsalted SHA-1 password hashes", and adds that LinkedIn "was not using key-stretching tools such as PBKDF2 or Argon2 at the time of the breach", so "the passwords were significantly easier to crack". LinkedIn's own statement of 12 June said it had "completed a long-planned transition" to a system "that both hashes and salts the passwords". Four years later the same 2012 data resurfaced, now at "over 177.5 million" records.

Set the two side by side. Adobe stored passwords reversibly, in a mode that leaked equality, beside plain-text hints, in a backup that should not have existed. LinkedIn stored them irreversibly but fast and unsalted, so equal passwords still matched and every guess was cheap. Neither company lacked the knowledge. Adobe had built the right system and LinkedIn had planned the right transition. Both were caught holding the old store.

The ledger

The book this comes from keeps a ledger for each case: the claim as it circulates, the document that checks it, and a verdict. Here is Adobe's.

  • "Adobe had not salted the passwords." The Irish report records a hashing-and-salting system running for years before the breach; the Australian report dates it to April 2010. The claim describes the leaked backup and omits the production system. Missing the main fact.
  • "Each breached account contained a password hint in plain text." The Irish report: hints belonged to "a small percentage of users". Overstated.
  • "Many passwords were quickly resolved back to plain text." True, but by the crossword of hints and repeated passwords, not by breaking the encryption. No report says the key was recovered. Missing the mechanism.
  • "The breach happened in October 2013." Access ran from 30 August to 17 September; October is the announcement. Overstated.
  • "Every affected customer was forced to change their password." Adobe reset passwords only for users whose current password data was in the database; 218,750 Australians had obsolete data. Overstated.
  • LinkedIn's passwords were "encrypted" and "decrypted" (WIRED, 2012). They were hashed, and hashes are guessed, not decrypted. Wrong.

What you can now check

Find one migration in a system you run, or depend on, that everyone calls finished. A password store moved to a proper hash. A secrets system replaced. A card-number table tokenised. Then ask one question of it: where is the old copy? Not the old code. The old data. A backup taken before the cut-over, a replica never torn down, a nightly export that still runs, a dump on a share "in case of issues with the new system".

If you can name the copy and the date it was destroyed, the migration is finished. If you can only name the new system, you have Adobe's finding: more than three years after the replacement arrived, a retained database still held current passwords in reversible form, and that database is what was taken. Then look for the hint column, or its modern equivalent: any field stored in the clear that helps a reader guess the protected one. The clue beside the ciphertext, not the cipher, is what turned a dump into a crossword.

One Line Too Many, One Field Too Few

Apple's goto fail, 2014, and CrowdStrike's Channel File 291, 2024. Two bugs that failed in opposite directions, and why neither fix is the lesson.

Two headlines, ten years apart. In February 2014 a duplicated line of code, goto fail;, in the part of Apple's software that secures connections between its devices and websites (TLS) let anyone who could intercept a connection impersonate a server without holding the certificate's private key. In July 2024 a CrowdStrike content update crashed the Windows hosts that received it; Microsoft put the count at 8.5 million devices. The headlines skip the detail that matters: the two bugs failed in opposite directions. Apple's never raised an error. It returned success from a label called fail. CrowdStrike's raised an error nothing could catch; the crash text, in Windows' own words, reads "This cannot be protected by try-except". Each was fixed by a small change. Neither change is the lesson.

The break

Apple shipped iOS 7.0.6 and 6.1.6 on 21 February 2014 and OS X 10.9.2 four days later. Each security note carried the same two sentences. Impact: "An attacker with a privileged network position may capture or modify data in sessions protected by SSL/TLS". Description: "Secure Transport failed to validate the authenticity of the connection. This issue was addressed by restoring missing validation steps". That is everything Apple has ever said about CVE-2014-1266.

Adam Langley, then working on TLS for Google's Chrome, published the next day the analysis everyone has quoted since, and explained why "the signature verification will never fail". The file is public. In the release Apple tagged Security-55471, sslKeyExchange.c reads:

    if ((err = ReadyHash(&SSLHashSHA1, &hashCtx)) != 0)
        goto fail;
    if ((err = SSLHashSHA1.update(&hashCtx, &clientRandom)) != 0)
        goto fail;
    if ((err = SSLHashSHA1.update(&hashCtx, &serverRandom)) != 0)
        goto fail;
    if ((err = SSLHashSHA1.update(&hashCtx, &signedParams)) != 0)
        goto fail;
        goto fail;
    if ((err = SSLHashSHA1.final(&hashCtx, &hashOut)) != 0)
        goto fail;

    err = sslRawVerify(ctx, ctx->peerPubKey, dataToSign, dataToSignLen,
                       signature, signatureLen);
    ...
fail:
    SSLFreeBuffer(&signedHashes);
    SSLFreeBuffer(&hashCtx);
    return err;

The second goto fail; is indented as if it belonged to the if above it. It does not. C binds only the first statement to an unbraced if, so the jump always runs. err still holds the zero the successful hash update left in it, and the function returns "no error" without reaching sslRawVerify, the one call that checks the signature.

That signature is the server's, on the ServerKeyExchange message that DHE and ECDHE suites use to hand over the connection's ephemeral key. Langley's consequence in one line: "It's possible to send a correct certificate chain to the client, but sign the handshake with the wrong private key, or not sign it at all". The CVE says the same more formally: the code "does not check the signature in a TLS Server Key Exchange message".

The reach was everything built on SecureTransport, which on those platforms meant most software; Chrome and Firefox escaped because they carry their own TLS stack. Certificate pinning would not have helped. The chain was genuine; the broken link was between the chain and the handshake.

The fix is public too. Between Security-55471 and Security-55471.14 the entire change to the file is the deleted duplicate line. How the line got there, Apple has never said. David Wheeler, whose essay on the bug is the most-cited attempt at lessons, writes: "I have not found any data on why the line was duplicated".

Ten years later, the other failure. At 04:09 UTC on Friday 19 July 2024 CrowdStrike released a content update for its Windows sensor and reverted it at 05:27. Windows hosts running sensor 7.11 or later that were online in those 78 minutes and received it crashed. By evening the chief executive had said the sentence every incident now needs: "This was not a cyberattack". The next day Microsoft published its estimate: "8.5 million Windows devices, or less than one percent of all Windows machines".

CrowdStrike's preliminary review of 24 July taught the public a vocabulary. Sensor Content ships with the sensor and includes Template Types, "expressed in code". Rapid Response Content is configuration pushed from the cloud, "not code or a kernel driver", delivered as Template Instances through numbered Channel Files. Sensor 7.11, released 28 February 2024, added a Template Type for inter-process communication; four instances shipped in Channel File 291 between March and April, and all "performed as expected".

On 19 July two more instances went out. "Due to a bug in the Content Validator, one of the two Template Instances passed validation despite containing problematic content data". On the sensor that produced "an out-of-bounds memory read triggering an exception. This unexpected exception could not be gracefully handled, resulting in a Windows operating system crash (BSOD)".

The root-cause analysis of 6 August said what "problematic" meant. "The new IPC Template Type defined 21 input parameter fields, but the integration code that invoked the Content Interpreter with Channel File 291's Template Instances supplied only 20 input values". That mismatch had been live since February. Nobody noticed because every test and every earlier instance used a wildcard in the 21st field, so the sensor never looked at it. One July instance "introduced a non-wildcard matching criterion for the 21st input parameter", and "at the next IPC notification from the operating system" the interpreter went looking for a 21st value in a 20-value array. The RCA calls it "the confluence of these issues": the validator believed the definition, the interpreter believed the code, and no test had asked for field 21.

By 29 July about 99% of Windows sensors were back online, by CrowdStrike's count. What the outage cost is being argued in court, not settled in a post-mortem.

The mechanism

OWASP's Top 10 for 2025 has a new category for this, Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions: "Mishandling exceptional conditions in software happens when programs fail to prevent, detect, and respond to unusual and unpredictable situations, which leads to crashes, unexpected behavior, and sometimes vulnerabilities." Among the weaknesses it maps is CWE-636, Not Failing Securely, also called failing open. Read both incidents against that sentence and the pattern is not "a bad line" or "a bad update". In both, a check ran, and it was not the check that mattered.

Draw Apple's function as three boxes. One: a chain of steps, each written if ((err = step()) != 0) goto fail;. Two: the verify call, the only step that can say a signature is bad. Three: the label, which frees memory and returns err. The idiom holds as long as the label is reached only by a failed step and the last step before it wrote err. The duplicate line broke the first condition. Control reached the label from a step that had succeeded, and err faithfully reported the success.

The label lied before the bug. After sslRawVerify there is no separate success exit; success also falls through to fail:. The label never meant "failure". It meant "clean up and return whatever err is". In that design a stray jump is not a crash or a rejection but an early return with the current status, and the current status was zero. That is failing open in C: an absence of error standing in for a positive result.

Nobody saw it because the tools did not. Langley compiled a six-line version with -Wall: "neither GCC 4.8.2 or Clang 3.3 from Xcode make a peep about the dead code". Clang could warn with -Wunreachable-code, which was not in -Wall. Wheeler found that gcc accepted the same flag and did nothing, silently. Indentation shows what the writer meant; the compiler reads braces. When they disagree, the eye sides with the indentation, and the eye reviews the code.

Now draw the CrowdStrike sensor in three boxes. One: a definitions file that says this Template Type has 21 fields. Two: sensor code that builds an array of 20 pointers from a named-pipe event and hands it to the interpreter. Three: a rule from the cloud that says "compare field 21 against this pattern". The interpreter asks for index 20 of a 20-element array and gets whatever lies past the end; in the published crash dump, a value that "does not point to valid memory". The code checks that pointer for NULL, which it is not, and reads through it. A check ran. It was the wrong check. The question was "is 20 in range", not "is this null".

The code runs in csagent.sys, a file-system filter driver loaded early in boot, so the bad read is not an exception a program can catch. It is a bugcheck. The RCA reproduces the debugger's summary: "PAGE_FAULT_IN_NONPAGED_AREA (50) Invalid system memory was referenced. This cannot be protected by try-except". The machine stops. Nobody designed that outcome. It is what the kernel does with an unhandled fault, and the unit it contains is the whole host.

OWASP's page has a line that fits both drawings: "Any time an application is unsure of its next instruction, an exceptional condition has been mishandled." Apple's code was unsure whether it had verified anything and answered yes. CrowdStrike's was unsure whether field 21 existed and answered by reading it. Neither is a web application, and an out-of-bounds read is a memory-safety fault the category does not list. What puts both here is the handling, not the fault.

The defense

OWASP's advice is to "'catch' every possible system error directly at the place where they occur and then handle it" and, mid-transaction, to "roll back every part of the transaction and start again (also known as failing closed)". Apple's code failed open at the one boundary where failing closed is not optional: authentication. The fail-closed form of the idiom is small. Start err as a failure, so a stray jump returns "bad". Or give the verify call the only path to "ok", so no earlier success can be mistaken for it. Apple did neither in the fix; the diff deletes the line and leaves the structure. Whether a test or a review rule was added later, Apple has not said. The defense record on Apple's side is two sentences and a one-line diff.

What outsiders said would have caught it is opinion, and the opinions disagree. Langley: "A test case could have caught this, but it's difficult because it's so deep into the handshake. One needs to write a completely separate TLS stack"; code review "can be effective". Wheeler: negative tests and clang's flag. Both are plausible. Neither is a record of a defense that worked. Langley's test server, real certificates with a wrong signature, was built after disclosure; it found vulnerable clients and prevented nothing.

CrowdStrike's fault was contained by the kernel at the size of the host. Microsoft's explanation a week later states the rule: "All code operating at kernel level requires extensive validation because it cannot fail and restart like a normal user application". And the price: "Kernel drivers provide the above properties at the cost of resilience". For a security sensor, stopping is more defensible than silently continuing; a sensor that stops watching is the open version. The chief executive's first statement met that concern head on: "There is no impact to any protection if the Falcon sensor is installed".

But what failed was one telemetry rule in one channel file, and the containment unit was the machine. Every fix in the record is narrower than that unit. A runtime bounds check on the interpreter's input array landed on 25 July and reached Windows sensors 7.11 and later by 9 August, along with sensor code that now supplies 21 inputs. A compile-time check that the field counts match went to production on 27 July, and the validator now allows only a wildcard in field 21. The RCA does not say what the interpreter does with content it rejects, so I will not call the fix fail-open or fail-closed. The direction is plain: the checks moved to the content boundary, so bad content is stopped before the host is.

The other half of the fix is what a customer can pin. Before the incident customers chose the sensor version, N, N-1 or N-2. Content was not the sensor; it arrived from the cloud outside those controls and reached every eligible online host in the window. Finding 6 of the RCA adds canary rings and, for the first time, lets customers "choose where and when Rapid Response Content updates are deployed". Finding 3: all twelve automated test cases used a wildcard in the 21st field, so none exercised the mismatch. Finding 5: every new Template Instance is now tested before deployment.

Did it work? The RCA says the scenario "is now incapable of recurring", a vendor's claim about its own fix. Two independent reviews were commissioned; no findings had been published when I checked. What the record does settle is the shape of the decision. Apple's boundary was open at authentication, and closed was one line away. CrowdStrike's was closed at the size of a machine when the failing unit was a rule. Both answers were wrong somewhere. The job is to choose the boundary, and its size, on purpose.

What the outage cost is alleged, not found. Delta Air Lines sued CrowdStrike on 25 October 2024, alleging over $500 million in losses and roughly 7,000 cancelled flights over five days, and that "If CrowdStrike had tested the Faulty Update on even one computer before deployment, the computer would have crashed". The RCA is consistent with that, but the RCA is a vendor document and the complaint is a complaint. In May 2025 the court cut the fraud counts and let negligence and computer-trespass claims proceed, per press reports of the order. No court has found a figure or a fact.

What you can now see

Find one place in a system you run where an error path and a success path share an exit. Ask what happens the first time that configuration names a field the code has never supplied. The Firewall Could Read the Bank

Capital One, 2019. The server-side request forgery everyone cites is not in the court record. The permission is.

In 2019 the US bank Capital One announced that an intruder had taken data on roughly a hundred million of its customers. The version that spread within days was a server-side request forgery story, SSRF for short: a misconfigured web application firewall tricked into fetching cloud credentials from the metadata service, then a hundred million records out the door. That version comes from Brian Krebs, four days after the arrest, citing "a source with direct knowledge of the breach investigation". No court document contains it. The FBI's sworn complaint describes the mechanism in one sentence: "A firewall misconfiguration permitted commands to reach and be executed by that server, which enabled access to folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space". The words SSRF, metadata and AWS appear nowhere in its twelve pages.

What the complaint does contain is more useful. The firewall's role listed more than 700 buckets, and, "according to Capital One, the *****-WAF-Role account does not, in the ordinary course of business, invoke the List Buckets Command". So here is the claim to argue with: the bug that mattered was not the forwarding rule. It was a policy that let a firewall's identity enumerate and read an account. Whatever route the requests took, that policy is what turned a proxy into a bank. If you think that is hindsight, the rest of this post is the record you would need to argue against.

The mechanism, in four boxes

The file that exposed everything, posted to a GitHub gist in April 2019, was not the data. Per the complaint, it held a server's IP address, "code for three commands, as well as a list of more than 700 folders or buckets of data". The first command "obtained security credentials for an account known as ***-WAF-Role". The second, the List Buckets Command, used that role "to list the names of folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space". The third, the Sync Command, used it "to extract or copy data from those folders or buckets in Capital One's storage space for which the ***-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". Capital One re-ran the three commands and "confirmed that the commands did, in fact, function".

Draw four boxes. An attacker on the internet. A firewall or proxy instance that forwards requests. The instance metadata service, which in Amazon's own words "runs on a special 'link local' IP address of 169.254.169.254 that means only software running on the instance can access it" and "makes the AWS credentials available for any IAM role that is attached to the instance". And the storage buckets.

The metadata service's whole security model is one assumption: a request that reaches that address came from a program on the instance. Anything on the instance that forwards requests to a caller-chosen destination breaks the assumption without breaking anything else. The application-level form of the bug is two lines:

# generic application-level SSRF: the server fetches a caller-chosen URL
target = request.param("url")
return http.get(target)   # the server's identity, the caller's destination

The configuration form is a forwarding rule with no allow-list of upstream hosts. Same bug, no code. Point either at the metadata service's credential path and what comes back, in the reconstruction everyone repeats, is the instance role's temporary key, secret and session token. Nothing was injected. Nothing crashed. No log line said "denied".

Be precise about which part is reconstruction and which is record. Krebs's source said the credentials came from the metadata service, and Amazon told Krebs the cause was "a misconfiguration of a web application firewall and not the underlying infrastructure". No court document confirms the metadata step, and the trial exhibits that would settle it are not public. That row of the ledger below stays open. What is record: the credential, once obtained, was used from somewhere else. The complaint places the List Buckets calls at "IP addresses that I believe to be TOR exit nodes" and the Sync calls at an address belonging to a VPN provider. A credential is portable. From the moment it left the instance, every call was an ordinary API call by a legitimate principal.

That is why OWASP files this under Broken Access Control, the first category on its Top 10, and not under injection. Its definition: "Access control enforces policy such that users cannot act outside of their intended permissions." The first common failure it lists is "Violation of the principle of least privilege, commonly known as deny by default, where access should only be granted for particular capabilities, roles, or users, but is available to anyone." The 2025 list rolled SSRF into this category, and this case is the argument for doing so: the forged request supplies the route, the over-broad role supplies the damage. If you want to contest that, start with the complaint's own adjective for the March calls: "unusual". Not failed. Not denied. Unusual. A permitted call by a permitted identity is the hardest thing in a log to see, because nothing failed.

The defense, as a spec

Two defenses are on the record. One has a published design and no published outcome. The other is provable from the complaint alone.

What Amazon shipped. On 19 November 2019, four months after the arrest, Amazon published version 2 of the EC2 instance metadata service. The post does not mention Capital One. It names its threats in the title: "open firewalls, reverse proxies, and SSRF vulnerabilities". The spec, in the post's own words:

  • A caller must open a session with an HTTP PUT and receive a token, then send that token as a header on every read. Amazon's recipe:
TOKEN=`curl -X PUT "http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token" \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 21600"`
curl http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/profile \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN"
  • Against open WAFs: "our analysis of third-party WAF products and open WAF misconfigurations found that the vast majority do not permit HTTP PUT requests".
  • Against open reverse proxies: IMDSv2 "will also not issue session tokens to any caller with an X-Forwarded-For header", the header proxies add.
  • Against an SSRF that can set arbitrary headers: a PUT followed by a secret token "is always strictly more effective than requiring only a static header".
  • Against an instance misconfigured as a router or NAT: the token response is sent with an IP TTL of 1, so it "will, by default, not be able to travel outside the instance".

What it did not cover, from the same post: "Both IMDSv1 and IMDSv2 will be available and enabled by default". Version 1, the one that answers a plain GET, "remains available for customers who have tools and scripts using v1". Amazon shipped a CloudWatch metric counting v1 calls per instance and an IAM condition key, ec2:RoleDelivery, so that a bucket policy can refuse credentials that arrived through v1. That is the whole published record: a design, a rationale, and no outcome. No count of accounts that turned v1 off, no count of refused requests, then or since as far as I can find. Until one exists, the defense's record is its design.

What would have worked without Amazon. OWASP's first prevention line: "Except for public resources, deny by default." A web application firewall inspects traffic and passes or drops it. Nothing in that job requires listing every bucket in an account or reading credit-card applications. The complaint's own qualifier is the spec: the Sync Command copied from buckets "for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". Scope the role to the firewall's own logs and configuration and the three commands return nothing worth posting. Remove list permission and they do not even return the inventory. IMDSv2 protects the credential. Least privilege caps what the credential is worth. Only the second was available in March 2019, and it was available to Capital One alone.

Why it was not in place is the regulator's finding, not the FBI's, and it is the part the folk story skips. The OCC's consent order of August 2020 carries an $80 million penalty and never mentions a firewall or the incident. Its findings, which "the Bank neither admits nor denies", are that "in or around 2015, the Bank failed to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to migrating its information technology operations to the cloud operating environment", and that it then failed at "appropriate design and implementation of certain network security controls, adequate data loss prevention controls, and effective dispositioning of alerts". Internal audit "failed to identify numerous control weaknesses and gaps".

Set that beside the complaint's dates. A role calling outside its ordinary course of business on 22 March. A stranger's e-mail to the bank's disclosure address on 17 July: "There appears to be some leaked s3 data of yours in someone's github / gist". Whether any alert fired on the March calls is not on the public record. Whether the bank's fixes worked is not either: the order says the bank "has begun addressing the identified corrective action", and the bank's own statement says the configuration was fixed. That is all anyone outside has been told.

Monday

Pick one internet-facing machine you run that forwards requests: a WAF, a reverse proxy, a bastion, a gateway you configured yourself. Then:

  1. Find the identity attached to it. Instance role, service account, workload identity.
  2. Open the policy. Not the wiki, not your memory: the policy. Write down every action it is allowed and on what: list, read, write, delete, create.
  3. Beside each line, write the machine's job in five words.
  4. Any line with no job beside it is the finding. Look at list permissions first. In the complaint, a role that "does not, in the ordinary course of business" list buckets returned more than 700 names from one call. A stolen key with list permission is a map of the account.
  5. If the answer at step 2 is "everything", stop there. You have found the bug before the SSRF that would reach it. Fix the role before you touch the proxy.

A bad result looks like this: the policy lets the machine list every bucket in the account, or carries a wildcard on a service the machine has no reason to touch, and nobody can say when that permission was last used.

Then a second check, for anyone on EC2. Look at the metadata service setting on that instance. If it still answers a plain GET, that is version 1. Turn on the v1-call metric Amazon shipped, watch it until it reads zero, then require version 2. If you cannot get it to zero, you have found the script that still depends on it, and that script is next Monday's job.

Neither check needs an attacker. Both are visible in an afternoon with the console open.

Between Alert and Action

Target, 2013. The story is that the alarm went off and nobody looked. Target's own testimony says something narrower and stranger: the activity was detected, logged, surfaced and evaluated. What happened after that is the gap nobody has filled.

On the evening of 12 December 2013, the US Justice Department telephones Target, the American retailer, about suspicious activity involving payment cards used in its stores. Target opens an investigation that night. Within a week it will tell the public that the card data of some 40 million customers has been stolen from its checkout systems over the previous month. The intruders, it later says, had been inside since 12 November.

The story everyone tells

Three sentences have carried this case for a decade. The first is a headline from the security journalist Brian Krebs: "Target Hackers Broke in Via HVAC Company", meaning the attackers got in with credentials stolen from a heating-and-refrigeration contractor that had remote access for business reasons. The second is from a Bloomberg Businessweek investigation, relayed by CBS: Target's newly installed security system from the firm FireEye "produced warnings" as the malware was being installed, and "the attack could have been prevented" if Target had reacted. The third is Wikipedia's compression: the team received the warning, had ample time, and did not act.

The first is true and explains a door. The second and third are the interesting ones, because Target's own account does not confirm them and does not deny them.

What Target said under oath

In February 2014 John Mulligan, Target's chief financial officer, gave prepared testimony to the Senate. He described the scale first: Target's systems logged more than a billion technology events on an average day; tools narrowed those to a few hundred for the security operations centre; reviews produced dozens of cases a day for further assessment. Logging was abundant, filtering was happening, and people were reviewing what surfaced. The company also listed its controls, firewalls, malware detection, intrusion prevention, data-loss prevention, and noted that its systems had been certified compliant with the payment-card industry's security standard two months before the intrusion.

Then the sentence that changes the question. "We now believe that some intruder activity was detected," Mulligan said, and that it was logged, surfaced to the security operations centre, and evaluated by security professionals. With hindsight, he said, Target was asking about the judgments made then and whether different judgments might have produced different outcomes.

Read it carefully. Some activity crossed three boundaries: sensor to record, record to surfaced item, surfaced item to a human. What the testimony does not say is which activity, who evaluated it, with what context, what they decided, or what action followed. And it never identifies that activity as the FireEye alerts. It cannot be used to confirm the Businessweek story, and it cannot be used to clear it.

The competing account is the stronger narrative and the weaker document. The Senate Commerce Committee's staff report says FireEye generated urgent alerts, that Target's team neither reacted nor enabled automatic deletion, and that an alert with the generic label "malware.binary" may have looked like one more false positive. But the report attributes all of that to Businessweek, and says of itself that its explanation is "based on published media reports and expert analyses" and that the complete story might not be known until Target's forensic examination was finished. That examination has not been published.

So the counterfactual escalates as it travels. Mulligan: different judgments "may have led to different outcomes". The Senate staff: Target "could have potentially blocked" the malware's effect. CBS: "the attack could have been prevented". Each is a larger claim than the document beneath it.

The mechanism: six boxes and two loops

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, renamed this category in its 2025 edition from logging and monitoring to logging and alerting, and its definition explains why: "Without logging and monitoring, attacks and breaches cannot be detected, and without alerting it is very difficult to respond quickly and effectively." A record is evidence. An alert is a request for attention. Neither is a response.

Draw six boxes: activity, event, detection, alert, decision, action. Under them, context and a clock. Each arrow is its own way to fail. Activity can leave no event. An event can omit who did it or to what. A useful detection can drown among low-value ones. An alert can reach the wrong queue. An analyst can reasonably file an ambiguous item with too little context. A correct decision can invoke a stale playbook. A containment step can fail without anyone hearing. Buying a better sensor repairs some of those arrows and none of the later ones.

OWASP names the later ones explicitly: cases where "appropriate alerting thresholds and response escalation processes are not in place or effective", and a detected alert that cannot be processed because its playbook is "incomplete, obsolete, or absent".

Then add two feedback loops the boxes do not have by default. From decision, send the reason back to whoever writes the rules: true positive, false positive, duplicate, insufficient evidence. From action, return proof: host isolated, credential disabled, process stopped, owner reached. Without the first, the rules never learn from judgments. Without the second, a ticket can close while the exposure continues.

Mulligan's testimony places facts side by side without connecting them. Detected, logged, surfaced, evaluated; then, weeks later, an outside phone call. Put a question mark between evaluated and acted. That question mark is the case.

What Target changed, and the mirror at Equifax

Target later published a list: additional rules and alerts, centralised log feeds, more logging, application whitelisting on the checkout systems, tighter segmentation, reduced vendor access. These are recorded changes, not a recorded result. More rules and more logs improve coverage and also raise the judging load; a central feed preserves evidence but does not choose an owner or make a playbook work. OWASP's prescription reaches the missing interface: establish "monitoring and alerting use cases including playbooks such that suspicious activities are detected and responded to quickly".

Equifax, four years later, shows the same gap in a different place. The device inspecting encrypted traffic into the system that was breached had a security certificate that expired on 31 January 2016. The device stayed in the path; it just could not see. When the certificate was renewed on 29 July 2017, the team spotted a suspicious request almost immediately. By then the intruders had been in since May. The House Oversight report calls it nineteen months without visibility and counts at least 324 expired certificates across the company, 79 of them on devices monitoring critical systems. The widely repeated "nine months" is wrong against both the House report and the government audit office, which relays Equifax's own, different, figure of about ten months.

That was not an alert waiting for judgment. It was a sensor that had silently gone blind, with nothing to tell anyone.

Is anyone getting better at this?

The best measured record is broader than either company. The incident-response firm Mandiant reports the median "dwell time" in its investigations, the interval from intrusion to detection, falling from 416 days in 2011 to 10 in 2023. A newspaper headline turned that into "orgs are detecting cybercriminals faster than ever". Two qualifications from Mandiant's own reports: in 2023, 54 percent of organisations first learned of the intrusion from outside, and in ransomware cases 76 percent of those outside notifications came from the attacker. A ransom note ends dwell time. And the median rose again, to 14 days in 2025. Detection speed is one interval on the six-box drawing, measured on a shifting set of cases. It does not say whether the action after the alert worked.

The ledger

  • Target's security system produced warnings during the attack (CBS). The Senate report relays FireEye alerts from Businessweek. Holds.
  • The attack could have been prevented if Target had reacted (CBS). The Senate report says "could have potentially blocked" the malware's effect; Mulligan says different judgments "may have led to different outcomes". Neither addresses preventing the attack. Overstated.
  • The breach was an HVAC-company break-in (Krebs). True as the entry; it omits the later warnings and the evaluated activity. Missing.
  • Target received the FireEye warning and did not act (Wikipedia). Attributed to Businessweek by the Senate; Mulligan's statement neither confirms nor resolves it. Target's alert and case records would settle it. Open.
  • Equifax's certificate had expired for nine months (Wikipedia). House report: expired January 2016, intrusion May 2017, nineteen months to discovery. Wrong.
  • Organisations detect attackers faster than ever (The Register). Median dwell fell to 10 days, then rose to 14; more than half of detections came from outside. Overstated.

What you can now check

Choose one detection whose failure would matter. Follow a test event all the way through your real system. Do not stop when it appears on a dashboard. Watch which context reaches the alert, which queue receives it, who owns the judgment, which playbook branch opens, and whether the requested action returns evidence that it happened.

Then inspect the silence around that path. What tells you the sensor has lost visibility? What tells you forwarding has stopped, a queue is unstaffed, or an action failed? The thing to test is not an alert rule. It is the closed loop from suspicious activity to verified response. If the test ends with "someone would notice", you have found the same gap Target's testimony leaves open.

Three Defaults, Not One

The MongoDB ransom wave, 2017. The headline says the database shipped open and version 3.6 closed it. The vendor's own bug tracker says the fix was three years old, and the wave fell where no default reached.

On 27 December 2016 a Dutch researcher opened one more MongoDB server that asked no one for a password. Where its data had been was a single database named WARNING, holding one document with a ransom note. Within two weeks the trackers' count of such databases passed 27,000. The headline version has held since: MongoDB shipped open to the internet by default, and the ransom wave forced the fix in version 3.6.

The vendor's own bug tracker says otherwise. The ticket that made the official packages listen only on localhost was closed in April 2014, nearly three years before the first ransom note. What 3.6 changed was where the default lived. That is the argument of this post: there were three defaults, not one, each living in a different place, and the wave fell on the installs none of them reached. A default is measured by what it reaches, not by when it is announced.

The break

The folk story has four tellers, and each sentence is nearly true. Brian Krebs, 10 January 2017: "If installed on a server with the default settings, for example, MongoDB allows anyone to browse the databases, download them, or even write over them and delete them". The Register, the day before: "up until recently, the software's default configuration is insecure". Wikipedia: "Because of MongoDB's default security configuration, which allows any user full access to the database, data from tens of thousands of MongoDB installations has been stolen". And The Next Web, on the day 3.6 was announced: MongoDB "will no longer come with an unsafe configuration out of the box". One default, insecure, fixed by the wave.

The first sighting was Victor Gevers's, reported by Bleeping Computer. Gevers had spent years finding open servers and telling their owners. On 27 December he found a MongoDB server "left open to external connections and without a password on the admin account". "No password" was the week's shorthand; the condition under it was a server with access control off, which asks no one who they are. The ransom note read "SEND 0.2 BTC TO THIS ADDRESS … AND CONTACT THIS EMAIL WITH YOUR IP OF YOUR SERVER TO RECOVER YOUR DATABASE !". Gevers was precise about the class: "This is not ransomware. Database does not get encrypted. It only gets replaced." That owner had a backup from just before, and restored it.

The count began the next week and never had one denominator. On 4 January Shodan's John Matherly put it at "just over 1,800" hit, with at least eleven owners paying. On 6 January Niall Merrigan, tracking with Gevers, tweeted 10,500. On 9 January The Register had it going "from 12,000 earlier today to 27,633, over the course of about 12 hours", with fifteen distinct attackers, one of them on 15,482 instances. Krebs on 10 January: "at least 29,000 … now erased". The population those counts were drawn from was 99,000 by Gevers's estimate, "more than 52,000" by Shodan's, and about 42,000 by the arithmetic of Bleeping's "about 25%".

What those numbers counted is in Krebs's description of the method. A Shodan query for MongoDB returns a list of database names. Gevers ran it and saw "far too many" named "readme," "readnow," "encrypted" and "readplease", each holding one document with a demand. Merrigan's check: wait a few minutes, re-run the query, and the same addresses "now have a different database title and a new ransom note". Groups were "re-hacking the same servers and rewriting each other ransom notes", so victims who paid sometimes paid the wrong group. In 84 cases Gevers could find no "trace of data exfiltration". The count was of ransom-note names seen through a scanner, each host under whichever note was written last. "Erased" holds. "Held for ransom" mostly meant deleted.

On 6 January the vendor spoke. Andreas Nilsson, MongoDB's director of product security: "These attacks are preventable with the extensive security protections built into MongoDB. You need to use these features correctly". The operative line: "The most popular installer for MongoDB (RPM) limits network access to localhost by default. Use this configuration too if you're installing via another means." Victims were told to "assume that the attacker has a copy of all data".

The wave came back. On 4 September 2017 Bleeping reported three new groups had "hijacked over 26,000 servers" in a week, 22,449 of them by one group. Gevers's open questions that day are this post's: "Is this a lack of knowledge? Did they mess up the [MongoDB] security settings without knowing it? Are they running on older version without safe defaults and other vulnerabilities?" Four days later the vendor's Davi Ottenheimer wrote that the new wave "does not indicate a new risk, just new targets".

The mechanism

Nothing in this story is a bug. Every line of code did what it said. OWASP's Top 10 files it under Security Misconfiguration, defined as "when a system, application, or cloud service is set up incorrectly from a security perspective, creating vulnerabilities", and two lines from its list of symptoms are this whole incident: "Unnecessary features are enabled or installed (e.g., unnecessary ports, services, pages, accounts, testing frameworks, or privileges)." and "For upgraded systems, the latest security features are disabled or not configured securely."

The whiteboard has two switches. A server process listens on an address and a port. Bound to 0.0.0.0 it answers on every interface the machine has, including the public one; bound to 127.0.0.1 it answers only to programs on the same machine. MongoDB's port is 27017. The second switch is access control, which asks a connecting client who it is. MongoDB's setting for it, security.authorization, reads "Default: disabled" in the manual, and disabled means "A user can access any database and perform any action". With the first switch open and the second off, whoever finds the port has the database. The attack in the record is only that. Merrigan's summary, via Krebs: "Use Shodan to scan for open MongoDB databases, connect using anonymous access, and then list all available databases."

Now the first switch, which the record shows had three defaults.

The binary's. Before April 2017 the server's listener read, for an empty bind setting:

if (*ips == '\0') {
    out.push_back(SockAddr("0.0.0.0", port));  // IPv4 all

and the option's help text said "comma separated list of ip addresses to listen on - all local ips by default". Nothing set meant everywhere.

The package's. In November 2011 Roman Shtylman filed SERVER-4216 against the Debian package: its config file "Does not have a 'bind_ip 127.0.0.1' option set". His ask: "The default should be to lockdown as much as possible and only expose if the user requests it". It was closed on 8 April 2014 with fix version 2.6.0, the day 2.6 shipped. From then on the RPM and DEB packages wrote a config line that said localhost; the binary underneath still said everywhere. The 3.6 release notes put it exactly: "From MongoDB versions 2.6 to 3.4, only the binaries from the official MongoDB RPM … and DEB … packages would bind to localhost by default". The Register's "up until recently" was wrong in both directions: the package default was nearly three years old, and the binary's had not moved.

3.6's. The same line of code with a different constant: SockAddr("127.0.0.1", port); // IPv4 localhost. That is the whole of the binding change.

Who fell through to the first default? Anyone on a tarball, anyone on a version older than 2.6, and anyone on an image built from either. Matherly's 2015 census: 2.4.14 "seems to be the last version that still listened to 0.0.0.0 by default", and the exposed hosts sat in clouds, where "cloud images don't get updated as often".

And a fourth population the folk story leaves out. In December 2015 the single most common exposed version was 3.0.7, whose packages already defaulted to localhost. Matherly's reading: "a lot of people are changing the default configuration of MongoDB to something less secure and aren't enabling any firewall", possibly "upgrading their instances but using their existing, insecure configuration files". That is OWASP's "upgraded systems" line, thirteen months before the wave. A default that lives in a package's config file is a property of one install path, not of the product. Every other path inherits the binary's answer, and a config file copied from a tutorial carries its own.

Why it is hard to see: the safe setting looks like a broken one. A database bound to localhost refuses an application server on any other host, and the fix ticket's own user summary prints the override, bindIp: 0.0.0.0, as the way to "make MongoDB servers accept connections from remote and local sources", with a request to review the Security Checklist first. The override is two lines. The checklist is a page.

The second switch was never moved. Eliot Horowitz, MongoDB's CTO, on 3.6: "If you explicitly turn it on, but don't turn on authentication, we can't help you at that point". The manual's security.authorization still reads "Default: disabled". The new default does not close the door. It moves the door one step further from the tutorial.

The defense

Read the vendor's statement next to the change it shipped, in order. On 6 January 2017 the advice was that the RPM installer already binds to localhost: "Use this configuration too if you're installing via another means". On 7 March, nine weeks later, Spencer Jackson of MongoDB filed SERVER-28229, "Bind to localhost by default": "MongoDB binaries should bind to localhost by default. This will allow small deployments and testing environments to be used from localhost, while not being accessible from the internet". Three parts: bind to 127.0.0.1 unless told otherwise, print a startup warning when no bind address was given, add a --bind_ip_all flag for the old behaviour. Jackson's commit landed on 28 April 2017, fix version 3.5.7, a development release.

The warning it added is the best single line in the defense record, because it names the trade-off:

** ATTENTION: The server is bound to localhost.
**          Remote systems will be unable to connect to this server.
**          Start the server with --bind_ip <address> to specify which IP addresses it
**          should serve responses from, or with --bind_ip_all to bind to all interfaces.

The official Docker image's maintainers saw it at once and opened an issue that "3.5.7 now binds to localhost-only by default". Ottenheimer's September post laid out all three defaults in the vendor's own words: localhost binding "since release 2.6.0 … in our most popular deployment package formats, RPM and deb"; then "Beginning with development release version 3.5.7, localhost-only binding is implemented directly in the MongoDB server"; and "a warning to our download center" for "non-packaged distributions".

The announcement came on 8 November 2017: "With MongoDB 3.6, this default behavior is implemented directly in the server itself, so all MongoDB packages across all platforms refuse all external connections to the database unless otherwise explicitly configured by the administrator". That was the announcement, not the release. The Next Web the same day: "The final release is expected at some point in December". The source tree's r3.6.0 tag is dated 1 December, and on 5 December Horowitz told the announce list that 3.6 was "now generally available for production deployments".

The sequence is the real post-mortem. The statement in January said users must set it. The ticket in March said the product would. The announcement in November said it did. The release in December shipped it. Seven months passed between commit and general availability, and the September wave, 26,000 in a week, fell inside them.

Did it work? Take each measurement with its method. Matherly's 2015 census pulled every MongoDB banner from Shodan and summed the reported database sizes: "nearly 30,000 instances" without authorization and 595.2 TB in July 2015; "at least 35,000" and 684.8 TB in December. In May 2020 his after-figure: exposed MongoDB data fell "from 24 TB to 12.5 TB" between 2018 and 2020, "the only database among the 3 I looked at where the amount of exposed data has actually decreased". He also wrote that the 2018 level "was already drastically lower than the others due to a series of ransomware attacks", and that "Those attacks are still on-going". So exposed data fell, with two caveats the record itself supplies: the 2020 post does not say it measured the way 2015 did, and Shodan's own reading credits the wipes for part of the fall.

The host count is another matter. The only after-series is ZDNet's, in July 2020: "From the 60,000 MongoDB servers left exposed online in early 2017, the needle has barely moved to 48,000". It names no source. Exposed data fell. Whether exposed hosts fell is contested, and this post does not claim it.

What the record does show is the reach of a default. In February 2019 another 3,000 were ransomed, and in 2020 a single campaign took 22,900; ZDNet's explanation was "incorrect MongoDB configuration tutorials", honest mistakes, and "server images that come packed with a misconfigured MongoDB system out of the box". A changed default reaches new installs as they are created and old ones as they are rebuilt, and nothing in between. Horowitz's sentence was conditional, "If you don't explicitly turn it on", and on its own terms it holds. The Next Web's gloss on it, that the ransoms "won't be an issue any more, as of MongoDB 3.6", is the claim the later waves test.

The one defense that could look at the installed base was the instrument. The same query that found the victims is the one a defender runs first: Shodan by port or product name, or Shadowserver's report for your own address space. The vendor pointed at alerts to detect internet exposure in both its January and September posts. No published figure says how many operators ran either, and the vendor has published no count of installs that kept the 3.6 default. The measured defense here is a default, and a default is measured by what it reached.

The ledger

The folk story, claim by claim, against the documents. HOLDS: the record agrees. OVERSTATED: true in outline, wrong in a way that matters. MISSING: the record shows something a defender needed that the retelling leaves out. OPEN: no document settles it.

  1. The attacks hit servers "left accessible via the Internet and without a password on the administrator account"; the data was "exported … and replaced" (Bleeping Computer, January 2017). The vendor's two posts say the same: "dropped databases and/or collections and replaced them"; "no password on their Administrator account". Two conditions and a drop-and-replace. HOLDS.
  2. MongoDB "will no longer come with an unsafe configuration out of the box" as of 3.6 (The Next Web). SERVER-4216 closed 8 April 2014 with 2.6.0; the 3.6 notes: "From MongoDB versions 2.6 to 3.4, only the binaries from the official MongoDB RPM … and DEB … packages would bind to localhost by default". True of the tarball; for the packages most people installed from, the safe bind was nearly three years old. 3.6 changed where the default lived. OVERSTATED.
  3. "If installed on a server with the default settings … MongoDB allows anyone to browse the databases, download them, or even write over them and delete them" (Krebs); "default security configuration, which allows any user full access" (Wikipedia). True of the tarball, the pre-2.6 packages and images built from them, and true of authentication on every version; not true of the bind on an RPM or DEB install after April 2014. One of the two default settings was never changed. OVERSTATED.
  4. "About 78% of all these hosts were running known vulnerable versions" (Gevers via Bleeping). No published version breakdown of the January 2017 victims exists. The nearest census, Matherly's of December 2015, shows the most common exposed version was 3.0.7, whose packages already defaulted to localhost. That neither confirms nor refutes 78%. A version facet of the January 2017 scan would settle it. OPEN.
  5. "MongoDB 3.6 comes hardened against database ransomware by default" (The Next Web headline, 8 November 2017). Specified 7 March, committed 28 April in development release 3.5.7, announced 8 November, generally available 5 December. The September wave took 26,000 more while the change sat in a development release. The gap between commit and reach is the defense story, and the folk date hides it. MISSING.
  6. "Body count hits 27,000 in hours" (The Register); "at least 29,000 … now erased" (Krebs). A count of ransom-note names seen by a scanner, each host under whichever note was last written, over a denominator that varied by a factor of two in the same week. "Erased" holds; "held for ransom" mostly meant deleted. OVERSTATED.
  7. Ottenheimer "blamed the attacks –and rightfully so– on database owners" (ZDNet, 2020). The blame is quoted fairly. The same post, six paragraphs on, is the vendor's own account of changing the product's default in 3.5.7, which ZDNet leaves out, and with it the fact that the vendor's answer to the wave was a change, not only a statement. OVERSTATED.
  8. "If you don't explicitly turn it on, that entire method of doing ransomware goes away" (Horowitz, to The Next Web). On its stated condition. A 3.6 binary left at its default is not reachable from the internet; the sentence claims nothing about installs that turn networking on, and he said so in the next breath. HOLDS.
  9. "Thankfully, that won't be an issue any more, as of MongoDB 3.6" (The Next Web, glossing Horowitz). 3,000 more ransomed in February 2019; 22,900 in one 2020 campaign; Matherly in 2020: the attacks "are still on-going". True for a fresh install that keeps its default; not for the installed base, the images, or anyone who set the override. OVERSTATED.
  10. "From the 60,000 MongoDB servers left exposed online in early 2017, the needle has barely moved to 48,000" (ZDNet, 2020). Unsourced. Shodan's series is of exposed data, which fell, partly because the wipes deleted it. Whether exposed hosts fell is not on the record. A same-method host count for 2017 and 2020 would close this row. OPEN.

Two HOLDS, five OVERSTATED, one MISSING, two OPEN, no WRONG. The retellings are not wrong. They collapse three defaults into one and date the fix to the press release.

What you can now see

Take one datastore you run, not only MongoDB, and find out what address it is listening on. Ask the running process, not the config file: the config says what someone meant, the socket says what is true. Then ask where that answer came from. It comes from one of three layers: the binary's built-in default, a config line the package wrote, or a line a person supplied, by flag, by template or from a tutorial.

If the answer is "every interface" and you cannot name the person and the reason, you have found the finding, before the firewall and before authentication. The 2017 record is exactly that line, written three ways: absent from an old tarball, overridden on a 3.0 host that had shipped safe, and baked into a cloud image nobody rebuilt. Then check the second switch. The 3.6 default moved one. The other is still where it was.

The Tarball Was Not the Tree

The xz backdoor, 2024. The story everyone tells is about a patient stranger. The record is about a file that was never in git.

A failed login takes 0.299 seconds. Then a library update lands, and the same failed login, to a user that does not exist, takes 0.807 seconds. Andres Freund is chasing "a few odd symptoms around liblzma (part of the xz package) on Debian sid installations over the last weeks (logins with ssh taking a lot of CPU, valgrind errors)". Half a second, on a machine that is his own, and he is careful about his standing before he says anything else: "I am not a security researcher, nor a reverse engineer".

On 29 March 2024, at 08:51 Pacific time, he posts to the oss-security list under the subject "backdoor in upstream xz/liblzma leading to ssh server compromise". By the end of the day Red Hat has assigned CVE-2024-3094 and NVD has published it with the highest severity score it gives, 10.0, and the weakness class "embedded malicious code".

The story everyone tells

WIRED tells it best, and its version is the one that travels. An attacker who "can spend two years politely and enthusiastically volunteering to help" a compression library that nearly every Linux distribution carries. "It's unlikely that Jia Tan is a real person, or even one person working alone." Wikipedia adds the emails of June 2022, "a pressure campaign against the original maintainer", and the suspicion of "sock puppetry". The backdoor, in this telling, was "deliberately included into the software in February 2024", and a lone engineer caught it because of a strange delay.

Hold that version. Almost all of it survives contact with the record. What the record adds is the part a defender needed, and it is not about the stranger.

What the record can date

The commits and the list archive let us check the chronology, which is all they can check. The first patch from the address that would later sign the releases was authored on 28 January 2022 and committed by the maintainer, Lasse Collin, on 7 February.

In June 2022, on the xz-devel list, a poster named Jigar Kumar wrote that "Submitting patches here has no purpose these days. The current maintainer lost interest or doesn't care to maintain anymore". Collin answered the next day. "I haven't lost interest but my ability to care has been fairly limited mostly due to longterm mental health issues but also due to some other things". He had "worked off-list a bit with Jia Tan on XZ Utils and perhaps he will have a bigger role in the future", and "this is an unpaid hobby project". Kumar pressed again a week later. A second poster, Dennis Ens, added that "its important to be aware of your own limits". On 29 June Collin wrote that Jia Tan "has been helping a lot off-list and is practically a co-maintainer already". The emails and the handover sit in one thread. Collin does not say the emails drove it, and no document says who Kumar or Ens were.

Then git. Jia Tan's first commit as committer, not only author, is dated 30 December 2022. Every release tag from 5.4.2, in March 2023, to 5.6.1, in March 2024, names Jia Tan as tagger and carries a PGP signature block: nine tags. Among commits dated 2023 the repository records 313 authored by Jia Tan against 164 by Collin. Collin did not leave. His last commits before the 5.6.0 tag are dated two days earlier.

Collin's own commit-by-commit review, published two months after the disclosure, marks a commit of 22 January 2024 adding two RISC-V test files as "the first commit preparing for the backdoor". Three days earlier, four commits had moved the project's published URLs to a subdomain that, Collin later said, was hosted on GitHub and was the only part of the domain Jia Tan controlled. On 15 February a one-line commit added a file called build-to-host.m4 to the repository's .gitignore. Keep that name. On 23 February came "Tests: Add a few test files", five binaries including the two that carried the payload; the review calls them "Backdoor files". The 5.6.0 tag followed the next day.

Two days after that, a commit titled "Build: Fix Linux Landlock feature test in Autotools and CMake builds" placed a line consisting of a single "." inside the C snippet that CMake compiles to detect the Landlock sandbox. A bare dot is not C. The detection always failed and the sandbox was never enabled. Collin's fix, on 30 March, is titled "CMake: Fix sabotaged Landlock sandbox check. It never enabled it".

In early March the backdoor was producing crashes and Valgrind errors on some systems. Freund lists three commits of 5 and 9 March presented as fixes, then a fourth, "Tests: Update two test files", that replaced the payload. The 5.6.1 tag is dated 9 March. "Given the activity over several weeks", Freund wrote, "the committer is either directly involved or there was some quite severe compromise of their system. Unfortunately the latter looks like the less likely explanation".

So far the folk story holds. Two years and three weeks from first patch to payload; git dates the interval and says nothing about intent. A January-to-March operation rather than "February", which matters only a little. Now the part that matters a lot.

The turn

Freund's post says where the backdoor lived, and it is two places. The second is the repository: two files under tests/files/ held the payload in obfuscated form, and they "were not even used for any 'tests' in 5.6.0". The first is not the repository at all. "One portion of the backdoor is solely in the distributed tarballs". The 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 release archives carried a modified copy of build-to-host.m4, the file that had been added to .gitignore in February. The modification was "not in the upstream source of build-to-host, nor is build-to-host used by xz in git". Red Hat put it in one clause: "the Git distribution lacks the M4 macro". Collin, later: the trigger "was never included in the Git repository".

Read that again against the story you were holding. The code everyone could review, in git, never contained the trigger. The release archive that distributions built from did. WIRED's 2,700-word account contains neither the word "tarball" nor the word "release". Wikipedia's lede says the backdoor affected "both version 5.6.0 and 5.6.1", as if a version were a thing you could hold. The unit that carried the backdoor was an artefact, not a version, and the attack lived in the gap between the tree people read and the tarball machines compiled.

Here is how that gap works, in five boxes. Box one is the git tree, which reviewers read. Box two is the release tarball, which distributions download. For a project built with Autotools the two are not the same. The tarball adds generated files: a configure script tens of thousands of lines long, and a directory of .m4 macro files that a tool copies in from the GNU tool chain. They are treated as noise, because they are the output of a tool. build-to-host.m4 is one of them, and the copy in the 5.6.x tarballs was not what the tool produces.

Box three is configure running. The modified macro, in Freund's words, "injects an obfuscated script to be executed at the end of configure", which, "if some preconditions match, modifies $builddir/src/liblzma/Makefile". It goes on only on x86-64 Linux, only with gcc and GNU ld, and only inside a Debian or RPM package build. Freund read the last condition as "likely aimed at making it harder to reproduce the issue for investigators". Box four is the Makefile, and the record gives the hook itself:

am__test = bad-3-corrupt_lzma2.xz
...
am__test_dir=$(top_srcdir)/tests/files/$(am__test)
...
sed rpath $(am__test_dir) | $(am__dist_setup) >/dev/null 2>&1

That expands to a pipeline: read a "corrupt" test file, fix its bytes, decompress it, pipe the result to a shell. The shell script extracts a prebuilt object from the second test file and links it into liblzma. NVD's description says exactly this: the build "extracts a prebuilt object file from a disguised test file existing in the source code, which is then used to modify specific functions in the liblzma code".

Box five is the running process. The object replaces the resolvers that glibc calls at load time to choose a CRC implementation, and from there, when the host process is sshd, redirects the RSA_public_decrypt entry in the program's linkage table to its own code. Freund's trace shows that code running during a public-key login, "in a pre-authentication context".

Which raises the question the headlines skipped: why is a compression library inside sshd at all? "openssh does not directly use liblzma", Freund wrote. "However debian and several other distributions patch openssh to support systemd notification, and libsystemd does depend on lzma". A distribution patch, not upstream OpenSSH, put the library into the address space of a daemon that faces the internet, before any login is checked. That is the second thing the folk story leaves out, and the third is the dot in the Landlock check: a separate disabling of a defense in the same fortnight, which the CVE does not describe and neither Ars Technica nor Wikipedia mentions.

Was anyone exposed? "It's not really affecting anyone in the real world", one analyst told Ars Technica. Debian's advisory says the compromised packages "were part of the Debian testing, unstable and experimental distributions" from a version uploaded on 1 February; no stable release was known to be affected. Red Hat confirmed two affected builds in the Fedora 40 beta. openSUSE said Tumbleweed and MicroOS had carried 5.6.0 "between March 7 and March 28" and told users with SSH exposed to the internet to install fresh, "as it's unknown if the backdoor has been exploited". No stable release; weeks of exposure on rolling and beta systems.

OWASP's 2025 list has a category for this, Software Supply Chain Failures, defined as "breakdowns or other compromises in the process of building, distributing, or updating software". The word to underline is building. Nothing here is a vulnerability in the compression code. It is a change to the process that turns reviewed source into a shipped library.

What was in place, and what would have worked

The tarball's signature was valid. Collin's statement: the 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 tarballs "were created and signed by Jia Tan". OWASP's prevention list says to "Prefer signed packages to reduce the chance of including a modified, malicious component". A valid signature shows that a particular key signed a particular file. It does not show who operated the key, who built the file, or whether the file matches any source a reviewer has read. Nothing in the collected record shows anyone comparing the tarball to the tag before Freund did. The one check that fired was a human noticing half a second, and his own post describes how contingent that was: the slowdown did not appear at first outside systemd and depended on which environment variables were set.

Reproducible builds, the practice of making a package build bit-for-bit identically from the same inputs, is the defense most often named. The record says: not as the practice stood. Within a day of the disclosure, Debian's Vagrant Cascadian rebuilt eleven security updates and the reverting xz package and "was able to get to build bit-for-bit identical with what is in the Debian archive". That checked Debian's builders. It did not check the tarball, and it could not: a build of the 5.6.1 tarball reproduces perfectly, backdoor included, because the tarball is the declared input. Julien Malka's 2025 essay is titled "How NixOS and reproducible builds could have detected the xz backdoor", and its own footnote concedes the limit: "In the case of the xz backdoor, NixOS executables did not contain the backdoor and as such without any modification we would not have discovered the backdoor. So yes, the title is a little bit catchy". What his method actually does is build from the maintainer's tarball and from the GitHub source archive and diff the results. Stripped of the title, it is a comparison of two sources. That is the check that would have worked.

The record of that check being adopted is thin but not empty. On 3 April 2024 Arch's kpcyrd wrote that "a distribution that claims to build from source should operate on VCS snapshots instead of tarballs with 25k lines of pre-generated shell-script", and that "Some packages have been actively converted to VCS snapshots by Arch Linux staff in response to the xz incident". On 13 April Simon Josefsson announced that libntlm 1.8 would ship, beside the usual tarball, a signed "minimal source-only tarball" made with git archive, because "tarballs with files that are not included in the git archive offer an opportunity to disguise malicious backdoors". One distribution, one upstream. Nobody has published a count of how many followed, and I will not invent one.

The better-documented defense is the removal of the edge. By 17 April OpenSSH had implemented systemd readiness notification without libsystemd, for its 9.8 release, and systemd had changed libsystemd to load its compression libraries on demand rather than link them. After both changes a backdoored liblzma is still a backdoored liblzma, but it is no longer in sshd's process before a login is checked. That is the least-privilege answer to a supply-chain problem: shrink what a compromised dependency can reach.

And the threat model, stated as a model. OWASP's list says you are likely vulnerable if your supply chain systems "do not have any separation of duty. No single person should be able to write code and promote it all the way to production without oversight from another human being." In June 2022 xz had one maintainer, who called the work "an unpaid hobby project". Within the month he called a second account "practically a co-maintainer already"; nine months later that account was tagging and signing releases. Nothing in the record says he was wrong to at the time. His own review flags nothing before January 2024. A dependency with a single unfunded maintainer is a system with one administrator, and the credential that matters is that administrator's trust. OWASP's advice to watch for components that are "unmaintained" is aimed at abandonment. xz was the opposite case: a project that suddenly had more maintainer than it could review.

What you are left holding

Before this, you thought of a dependency as its source: the tree, the tag, the code a reviewer could read. After it, you know there are two things, and your build takes the other one. The tree is what people read. The tarball is what machines compile, and for an Autotools project it carries files that exist nowhere in git, produced by a tool, treated as noise, signed by whoever holds the release key.

The check is short. Pick one dependency your build takes as a release tarball; a C library with a configure script is the classic case. Download the tarball your pipeline uses. Run git archive on the matching tag from the project's repository and diff the two trees. You will get a list of files that exist only in the tarball: configure, Makefile.in, a directory of .m4 files. In March 2024, build-to-host.m4 would have been on that list as a file with no counterpart in git.

You do not have to read those files. You have to decide who is allowed to produce them. Either your build regenerates them from the tree and discards the tarball's copies, or you build from the tag and skip the tarball. Both close the gap. Neither needs a scanner, an inventory tool or a budget. What neither shows is the payload, which was committed to git as test data; a history diff shows binary fixtures being added and replaced, not what they mean. That is the other half of the lesson, and it has no short check.

What "Turn Off GPS" Left Out

Strava, 2018. A fitness app's map drew the outlines of military bases. The story says the company gave them away and the Pentagon banned the trackers. The documents say the map worked exactly as designed, and that the design was the problem.

In January 2018 a student of international security in Australia opens a map published by Strava, the app that runners and cyclists use to record their workouts, and looks at Syria. Most of the country is dark. A few places glow: neat rectangles and loops in the desert, drawn by the running routes of people who exercise there every day. They are the outlines of military bases. The same map shows forward operating bases in Afghanistan and the perimeter of the Royal Navy's submarine base on the Clyde.

Strava had published the map two months earlier, in November 2017, as an engineering achievement: two years of activity from its users worldwide, 700 million workouts, 1.4 trillion GPS points, 16 billion kilometres. The company called it the Global Heatmap. It was meant to show athletes where other people run and ride.

The story everyone tells

The Guardian's headline on 28 January 2018 fixed the first version: "Fitness tracking app Strava gives away location of secret US army bases". Seven months later, Ars Technica fixed the second: "Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones". And a data-science textbook by four well-known authors later supplied the absolution: "Strava did nothing wrong; they displayed anonymized tracks for opted-in users who hadn't realized this compromised military security."

Each is nearly right. The map did show the bases. The Pentagon did issue a policy. Strava's processing did work as designed. What the three sentences leave out is why a correctly working map drew a base, and what the Pentagon's policy actually says.

The map worked

Strava's engineer described the pipeline in detail. The heatmap "contained only public activities and respected all privacy settings". Filters removed implausible running speeds and cars mislabelled as bike rides. Nothing in the record describes a processing defect. Every activity on the map was one its user had classed as public.

The company's chief executive said the same thing from the inside, the day after the story broke. Military members and aid workers "may have shared their location in areas without other activity density and, in doing so, inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations". The academic paper that studied the incident names the condition: "the sparsity of background noise in some regions".

That is the mechanism, and it is a design property, not a bug. A heatmap is an aggregate. In a city, one person's run disappears into thousands of others. In a desert, five people's daily run is the only thing on the map, and it draws the fence line. The same output that is harmless at one density is an outline at another.

Two design choices made it worse. The 2017 map drew each workout as a continuous path rather than a scatter of dots, at twice the previous resolution, so sparse traces read as roads. And to keep quiet trails visible alongside busy cities, the colouring used relative contrast: a pixel's brightness depended on how it ranked against other pixels, not on how many people had passed. The engineer stated the cost himself: the map "is not absolutely quantitative". Dense areas got cover from the crowd. Sparse areas got maximum contrast and no crowd.

One more word in the textbook's absolution is wrong. Strava's users had not opted in. The company's own letter describes "the ability to opt out of heatmaps altogether", and two US senators wrote to Strava that month saying consumers "were confused by Strava's opt-out provisions or unaware of what information was tracked". Opt-out, not opt-in.

Why no scanner would have found it

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, has a category for this called Insecure Design, defined as "missing or ineffective control design". Its page draws the line that matters here: "A secure design can still have implementation defects leading to vulnerabilities that may be exploited. An insecure design cannot be fixed by a perfect implementation as needed security controls were never created to defend against specific attacks."

The tools that find ordinary bugs look for patterns in code and probe running systems with bad input. Strava's code had no such pattern; every privacy setting was honoured. The dangerous behaviour lived one level up, in the decision to render public records at uniform contrast across regions of wildly different density. Testing that needs a different question. Assume every input is authorised and every branch correct. Then name the reader, the sensitive asset, and what that reader can observe. Compare a dense city with an isolated installation. Ask when a cohort becomes a person, when a route becomes a routine, and when historical detail becomes operational knowledge. That is threat modelling, and OWASP names its absence as a cause: "the lack of business risk profiling inherent in the software or system being developed".

What Strava changed, and what the Pentagon actually said

Strava's fix was not a patch. It was a threshold. Roads and trails with very little activity "would not show any heat until several different athletes uploaded activities in that area". Opt-outs would take effect at a monthly refresh. Street-level zoom was restricted to logged-in users. Note what the threshold does that an opt-out cannot. A per-user privacy zone hides that user's own rows, but as Strava's own update says, "Other athletes recording on Strava within an athlete's Privacy Zone may create heat on the map through that zone". A control that protects one person's data does not protect the place. The threshold protects the place.

The Pentagon's policy is more careful than its headline. The Deputy Secretary of Defense's memorandum of 3 August 2018 prohibits geolocation features on government and personal devices "while in locations designated as operational areas". Everywhere else, it rejects a single rule: component heads "will consider the inherent risks" and "should apply a tiered structure for categorizing location and operations sensitivity". And even in operational areas it allows exceptions, on one condition for personal devices: a commander may authorise them after "a threat-based comprehensive Operations Security (OPSEC) survey".

That survey is the interesting part, because it already existed. A year before the map, in July 2017, the US Government Accountability Office had reported that the Defense Department's own 2016 guidance warned that aggregated device data "could inform adversaries about capabilities, deployments, or work patterns", and that the OPSEC surveys required by the Department's directive were not being conducted for those risks. The Department had the process. It had not run it. The 2018 memorandum made running it the condition for an exception.

What neither the company nor the Department published is a measurement: whether sensitive sites stopped appearing, or whether personnel followed the directive. Publishing a policy is not the same as measuring its effect.

The ledger

  • "Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones." The memorandum applies in designated operational areas, allows exceptions after an OPSEC survey, and gives every other location risk-based rules. Overstated.
  • "Strava gives away location of secret US army bases." The academic study and the CEO's letter both confirm the exposure and its cause, sparse background activity. Holds.
  • The heatmap was anonymised. The study: "the identities of specific athletes were not directly leaked in the heatmap". Holds. (The same study found a separate route to identities, by uploading spoofed GPS data, which needed another interaction with Strava.)
  • The users had opted in. Strava's letter and the senators' inquiry both describe an opt-out. Wrong.

What you can now check

Find one feature in a system you run that publishes an aggregate: a heatmap, a leaderboard, a usage summary, a "popular near you". Assume the code is correct and every row in it was shared on purpose. Then ask what a hostile reader learns from the places where the aggregate is nearly empty, because that is where a handful of ordinary actions stops being a crowd and becomes an outline.

If your aggregate has no minimum below which it shows nothing, you have Strava's design. Strava's fix was a threshold of several athletes before any heat appears. The Department's was a survey before any exception. Neither is a scanner finding. Both required someone to ask what the empty map would reveal.

One Function, Three Patches

MOVEit, 2023. The story is one zero-day in a file-transfer product, patched in a day. The patch diff shows a function that did escape its input, for three uses out of four, and two more patches inside fifteen days.

On the evening of 28 May 2023, a customer calls Progress Software's support line about unusual activity on a MOVEit Transfer server. MOVEit is a product companies use to move files between each other securely; hospitals, pension funds, payroll processors and government agencies run it on internet-facing servers. By the next day Progress has a team on it. On 30 May it takes its own cloud version offline. On 31 May it publishes a warning and, the same day, patches.

It was already late. The incident-response firm Mandiant later placed the earliest evidence of exploitation on 27 May, and saw data leaving some servers within minutes of the attackers' tool arriving. About 2,500 MOVEit servers were reachable from the internet that week. By the end of the year, one tally would list more than 2,700 affected organisations and roughly 95 million people-records, most of them exposed through a third party rather than directly. An insurer that had never used MOVEit reported 2.5 million policyholders exposed because a vendor had.

The story everyone tells

The version that circulated is short and mostly right. A criminal group called Cl0p found a previously unknown flaw, a "zero-day", in MOVEit Transfer and used it to steal data from thousands of organisations. The flaw was a SQL injection: a way of slipping database commands into a field where the application expected ordinary text. Progress patched it on 31 May, and a spokesperson told a reporter the patch "fixed the issue".

The label is right. What it hides is the useful part: the route in was not a form field, the vulnerable code did escape its input, and the one patch became three.

What the patch diff shows

The security firm Rapid7 did what a defender can do with any patch: decompile the before and after, and compare. Two things had changed.

The first was a removed function. Before the patch, MOVEit would read key-value pairs from a special request header and copy them into the user's session. A later page loaded data from that session, and four calls later the value reached a database lookup. A value could reach the query through session state, a path a reviewer testing the visible input fields would never try. Rapid7 noted that naive injection attempts along the obvious routes failed, "because in every case the application escaped the arguments". The path that worked ran through the session.

The second was the rebuilt lookup, a function that finds users by email address. This is the whiteboard drawing of the whole case. The old function built one SQL string with the address in four places: three pattern matches and one equality test. In front of the three pattern slots, an escaping function rewrote the dangerous characters. In front of the equality test, nothing. Abridged from Rapid7's decompilation:

"... WHERE InstID={0} AND Deleted=0 AND ("
"Email='{2}' OR "                              // raw
BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "{1},%") + " OR "     // escaped
BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "%,{1}") + " OR "     // escaped
BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "%,{1},%") +          // escaped
") ORDER BY LoginName",
InstID, EscapeLikeForSQL(EmailAddress), EmailAddress

Follow the subscripts. The three pattern branches consume slot 1, the escaped value. The equality branch spells out slot 2, the raw one. The developer did treat the address as hostile. The treatment covered one kind of use and not the other, inside the same statement.

Why escaping is a promise and a parameter is a boundary

The OWASP Top 10, the standard list of web-application weakness classes, defines injection as a flaw "that allows untrusted user input to be sent to an interpreter (e.g. a browser, database, the command line) and causes the interpreter to execute parts of that input as commands". A database is an interpreter. Quotation marks and keywords have structural meaning to it. If an application makes one string out of its own SQL and a caller's text, the database has to decide which characters are instructions and which are data.

There are two ways to handle that. Escaping rewrites the meaningful characters in the input for one exact context: inside these quotes, in this clause, for this database engine. Change the surrounding quotes, use the value twice, or forget one occurrence, and the promise has to be proved again. A parameterised query changes the interface instead. The application sends a statement with placeholders, and supplies the values separately; the database driver presents them as data and never as more statement text. OWASP's wording: "The preferred option is to use a safe API, which avoids using the interpreter entirely, provides a parameterized interface, or migrates to Object Relational Mapping Tools (ORMs)."

The patched function is that change, visible in code. Abridged again:

clauses = { "Email=" + ParameterName("Email"),
            BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("FirstEmail")),
            BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("MiddleEmail")),
            BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("LastEmail")) };
where.WithParameter("Email", EmailAddress);
where.WithParameter("FirstEmail",  escaped + ",%");
where.WithParameter("MiddleEmail", "%," + escaped + ",%");
where.WithParameter("LastEmail",   "%," + escaped);
DoReadQuery(where.GetQuery(), where.Parameters, ...);

The last line is the boundary. Before the patch, the database call received one completed string. After it, the call receives a query and a separate collection of values. Four named bindings make four data flows visible; a reviewer can count them against four placeholders. One change closed the sink; the removal of the header function closed the route that reached it.

One patch becomes three

Then the story most retellings stop telling. On 9 June, Progress said a third-party code review had found further SQL-injection vulnerabilities, distinct from the first, and patched them. On 15 June it patched another, after someone posted it publicly. The July service pack added two more. Across five weeks, five SQL-injection identifiers were patched in the same product. Only the first is recorded as exploited.

Rapid7's verdict on the first patch is the sentence to keep: the 9 June patch addressed "several parts of an exploit chain that were not fully mitigated by the first patch". The spokesperson's "fixed the issue" was true of one function. The code around it took two more patches and a service pack.

This is not an argument against review. It is the evidence for what review is for. In March 2024 the US cybersecurity agency and the FBI issued an alert on SQL injection, prompted by "a widely publicized campaign" against a file-transfer product they did not name. It notes that MySQL introduced prepared statements in 2004, and that SQL injection had been called "unforgivable" since at least 2007. Its proposal is about defaults inside a software company: libraries that make the safe route the default, checks at pull-request time, and formal code review commissioned by executives, "because a simple review can reveal the prevalence of the class". Finding two more rounds of the same flaw after the emergency patch is what that looks like in practice. Repairing one observed path and eliminating a defect class are different jobs.

What the record does not say is why a product shipped in 2023 with this function built this way. Progress's filings report about 118 class actions and three government investigations; a 2025 court order let some claims proceed, on the plaintiffs' allegations, which the order says are not findings. The public record does not establish how long the function existed or what the company should have known. The code answers how. The institutional why is not on the record.

The ledger

  • A zero-day let Cl0p raid MOVEit servers and steal data. Progress's advisory, the CVE record, Mandiant's timeline and Rapid7's diff all agree. Holds.
  • Progress patched on 31 May, but instances were already under attack. Earliest exploitation 27 May; support call 28 May; cloud shut down 30 May; patch 31 May. Holds.
  • Progress disclosed "critical SQL injection flaw CVE-2023-34362" on 31 May. True, and it omits the four further SQL-injection identifiers patched in the following five weeks, which a defender needed to know. Missing.
  • More than 60 million individuals were affected. The tallies count people-records across notifications and say overlap cannot be removed. Whether the deduplicated number exceeds 60 million is not established. Open.
  • The 31 May patch "fixed the issue". It closed one function. Rapid7: parts of the exploit chain "were not fully mitigated by the first patch". Overstated.

What you can now check

This week, open one function in a system you run that takes a value from outside and searches the database with it. A lookup by email address is the obvious candidate. Do not start at the input validator. Start at the final database call. Does it receive one completed SQL string, or a statement and a separate collection of parameters?

If it receives one string, follow a single value backward through every appearance in that statement. A value used in one equality test and three pattern tests has four contexts, even though it came from one field. Mark each occurrence raw, escaped or bound. The dangerous finding is not a missing escape. It is an interface that lets the answer differ at each occurrence.

Then follow the value one step farther back than its name suggests. Session variables, cached objects and internal headers can still hold caller-controlled data. Trust is a property of the route a value travelled, not of the variable holding it now. The useful end state fits in one line: the query text contains placeholders, and the values cross the driver boundary separately. If your ordinary database API makes that shape awkward, the API is part of the finding.
