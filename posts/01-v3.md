# The Firewall Could Read the Bank

*Capital One, 2019. The server-side request forgery everyone cites is not in the court record. The permission is.*

You have heard this one as an SSRF story: a misconfigured web application firewall tricked into fetching cloud credentials from the metadata service, then a hundred million records out the door. That version comes from Brian Krebs, four days after the arrest, citing "a source with direct knowledge of the breach investigation". No court document contains it. The FBI's sworn complaint describes the mechanism in one sentence: "A firewall misconfiguration permitted commands to reach and be executed by that server, which enabled access to folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space". The words SSRF, metadata and AWS appear nowhere in its twelve pages.

What the complaint does contain is more useful. The firewall's role listed more than 700 buckets, and, "according to Capital One, the *****-WAF-Role account does not, in the ordinary course of business, invoke the List Buckets Command". So here is the claim to argue with: the bug that mattered was not the forwarding rule. It was a policy that let a firewall's identity enumerate and read an account. Whatever route the requests took, that policy is what turned a proxy into a bank. If you think that is hindsight, the rest of this post is the record you would need to argue against.

## The mechanism, in four boxes

The file that exposed everything, posted to a GitHub gist in April 2019, was not the data. Per the complaint, it held a server's IP address, "code for three commands, as well as a list of more than 700 folders or buckets of data". The first command "obtained security credentials for an account known as *****-WAF-Role". The second, the List Buckets Command, used that role "to list the names of folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space". The third, the Sync Command, used it "to extract or copy data from those folders or buckets in Capital One's storage space for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". Capital One re-ran the three commands and "confirmed that the commands did, in fact, function".

Draw four boxes. An attacker on the internet. A firewall or proxy instance that forwards requests. The instance metadata service, which in Amazon's own words "runs on a special 'link local' IP address of 169.254.169.254 that means only software running on the instance can access it" and "makes the AWS credentials available for any IAM role that is attached to the instance". And the storage buckets.

The metadata service's whole security model is one assumption: a request that reaches that address came from a program on the instance. Anything on the instance that forwards requests to a caller-chosen destination breaks the assumption without breaking anything else. The application-level form of the bug is two lines:

```
# generic application-level SSRF: the server fetches a caller-chosen URL
target = request.param("url")
return http.get(target)   # the server's identity, the caller's destination
```

The configuration form is a forwarding rule with no allow-list of upstream hosts. Same bug, no code. Point either at the metadata service's credential path and what comes back, in the reconstruction everyone repeats, is the instance role's temporary key, secret and session token. Nothing was injected. Nothing crashed. No log line said "denied".

Be precise about which part is reconstruction and which is record. Krebs's source said the credentials came from the metadata service, and Amazon told Krebs the cause was "a misconfiguration of a web application firewall and not the underlying infrastructure". No court document confirms the metadata step, and the trial exhibits that would settle it are not public. That row of the ledger below stays open. What is record: the credential, once obtained, was used from somewhere else. The complaint places the List Buckets calls at "IP addresses that I believe to be TOR exit nodes" and the Sync calls at an address belonging to a VPN provider. A credential is portable. From the moment it left the instance, every call was an ordinary API call by a legitimate principal.

That is why OWASP files this under Broken Access Control, the first category on its Top 10, and not under injection. Its definition: "Access control enforces policy such that users cannot act outside of their intended permissions." The first common failure it lists is "Violation of the principle of least privilege, commonly known as deny by default, where access should only be granted for particular capabilities, roles, or users, but is available to anyone." The 2025 list rolled SSRF into this category, and this case is the argument for doing so: the forged request supplies the route, the over-broad role supplies the damage. If you want to contest that, start with the complaint's own adjective for the March calls: "unusual". Not failed. Not denied. Unusual. A permitted call by a permitted identity is the hardest thing in a log to see, because nothing failed.

## The defense, as a spec

Two defenses are on the record. One has a published design and no published outcome. The other is provable from the complaint alone.

**What Amazon shipped.** On 19 November 2019, four months after the arrest, Amazon published version 2 of the EC2 instance metadata service. The post does not mention Capital One. It names its threats in the title: "open firewalls, reverse proxies, and SSRF vulnerabilities". The spec, in the post's own words:

- A caller must open a session with an HTTP PUT and receive a token, then send that token as a header on every read. Amazon's recipe:

```
TOKEN=`curl -X PUT "http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token" \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 21600"`
curl http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/profile \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN"
```

- Against open WAFs: "our analysis of third-party WAF products and open WAF misconfigurations found that the vast majority do not permit HTTP PUT requests".
- Against open reverse proxies: IMDSv2 "will also not issue session tokens to any caller with an X-Forwarded-For header", the header proxies add.
- Against an SSRF that can set arbitrary headers: a PUT followed by a secret token "is always strictly more effective than requiring only a static header".
- Against an instance misconfigured as a router or NAT: the token response is sent with an IP TTL of 1, so it "will, by default, not be able to travel outside the instance".

What it did not cover, from the same post: "Both IMDSv1 and IMDSv2 will be available and enabled by default". Version 1, the one that answers a plain GET, "remains available for customers who have tools and scripts using v1". Amazon shipped a CloudWatch metric counting v1 calls per instance and an IAM condition key, `ec2:RoleDelivery`, so that a bucket policy can refuse credentials that arrived through v1. That is the whole published record: a design, a rationale, and no outcome. No count of accounts that turned v1 off, no count of refused requests, then or since as far as I can find. Until one exists, the defense's record is its design.

**What would have worked without Amazon.** OWASP's first prevention line: "Except for public resources, deny by default." A web application firewall inspects traffic and passes or drops it. Nothing in that job requires listing every bucket in an account or reading credit-card applications. The complaint's own qualifier is the spec: the Sync Command copied from buckets "for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". Scope the role to the firewall's own logs and configuration and the three commands return nothing worth posting. Remove list permission and they do not even return the inventory. IMDSv2 protects the credential. Least privilege caps what the credential is worth. Only the second was available in March 2019, and it was available to Capital One alone.

**Why it was not in place** is the regulator's finding, not the FBI's, and it is the part the folk story skips. The OCC's consent order of August 2020 carries an $80 million penalty and never mentions a firewall or the incident. Its findings, which "the Bank neither admits nor denies", are that "in or around 2015, the Bank failed to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to migrating its information technology operations to the cloud operating environment", and that it then failed at "appropriate design and implementation of certain network security controls, adequate data loss prevention controls, and effective dispositioning of alerts". Internal audit "failed to identify numerous control weaknesses and gaps".

Set that beside the complaint's dates. A role calling outside its ordinary course of business on 22 March. A stranger's e-mail to the bank's disclosure address on 17 July: "There appears to be some leaked s3 data of yours in someone's github / gist". Whether any alert fired on the March calls is not on the public record. Whether the bank's fixes worked is not either: the order says the bank "has begun addressing the identified corrective action", and the bank's own statement says the configuration was fixed. That is all anyone outside has been told.

## The ledger

Every famous breach has a folk version, never false and never the whole story. Here is this one's, claim by claim, against the documents. HOLDS means the record agrees. OVERSTATED means true in outline and wrong in a way that matters. MISSING means the record shows something the folk version leaves out that a defender needed. OPEN means no public document settles it.

1. **"A 'Server Side Request Forgery' (SSRF) attack" tricked the WAF into relaying requests to "the 'metadata' service"** (Krebs, 2 August 2019, unnamed source). Complaint: "a firewall misconfiguration permitted commands to reach and be executed by that server". No court document says SSRF or metadata. Amazon's IMDSv2 post names open WAFs and SSRF as its threats without naming a customer. Consistent with every document, asserted by none; the trial exhibits would settle it. **OPEN.**
2. **The WAF role "was allowed to list all of the files in any buckets of data, and to read the contents of each of those files"** (Krebs). Complaint: List Buckets returned bucket names account-wide, more than 700 of them; Sync copied only from buckets "for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". List-all is what the call does. Read-all is not what the complaint says, and how many of the 700 were readable is not on the record. **OVERSTATED.**
3. **"The intrusion was caused by a misconfiguration of a web application firewall and not the underlying infrastructure"** (Amazon, to Krebs); "the configuration vulnerability" (Capital One). The OCC's findings are about risk assessment before the 2015 migration, network controls, data-loss prevention, alert disposition, audit and board accountability, and never mention the incident. One misconfiguration is the proximate cause on every account. The controls that would have caught or capped it are the regulator's subject, and nobody's headline. **MISSING.**
4. **"An 'insider' attack in which the accused took advantage of access surreptitiously obtained from her former employer"** (Krebs, reporting the speculation). Complaint: a systems engineer at the cloud provider in 2015–16. Ninth Circuit: the intrusions began "over two years after her employment at Amazon ended". The credentials came from Capital One's own server. Krebs himself: "the methods she deployed have been well understood for years". **OVERSTATED.**
5. **"Some of its leaked data was being stored out in the open on … Github"** (Krebs, 30 July 2019). The gist held a server IP, three commands and 700-plus bucket names. The recipe and the inventory, not the records. That is why Capital One could confirm in two days: it re-ran the commands. **OVERSTATED.**
6. **"Impacted more than 100 million U.S. Customers"** (Department of Justice, verdict release). Capital One: the largest category was people "as of the time they applied for one of our credit card products from 2005 through early 2019". Applicants, mostly, from a file going back to 2005. The number is right and the noun is wrong. **OVERSTATED.**
7. **"About 140,000 Social Security numbers … 80,000 bank account numbers"; 106 million people** (Wikipedia; Capital One). The sworn complaint said approximately 120,000 and 77,000. Capital One said about 140,000 and 80,000, then added about 4,700 more in 2021. The Ninth Circuit says 98 million Americans. As an order of magnitude, **HOLDS.**
8. **"No evidence the data was shared"; "time served and five years of probation"** (Wikipedia). Department of Justice: "no evidence that THOMPSON sold or disseminated". The Ninth Circuit vacated the 2022 sentence in March 2025 as "substantively unreasonable" against a Guidelines range of 168 to 210 months. On remand in October 2025 the court entered time served again, with supervised release credited for "the 3 years of probation already served", GPS monitoring, 250 hours of community service and $40,745,000 in restitution. **HOLDS**, except that the second sentence is supervised release, not probation.

Left out on purpose: the defendant's messages, her resume, the Slack channel. They convict. They do not change what you would do on Monday.
