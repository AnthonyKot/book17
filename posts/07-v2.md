# The Door Colonial Could Not See

Everyone knows how the Colonial Pipeline attack began: one leaked password, a VPN account with no second factor, and 5,500 miles of fuel pipeline shut for most of a week in May 2021. That version is true, and it is the least useful one. When the company's chief executive and its lead outside investigator testified to Congress a month later, what they described was not a password problem but a door: a remote-access path the company believed was switched off, that its penetration tests never found, and that accepted a username and a password anyway. By the end of this piece you will know how to look for that door on a network you run.

## The headline

On 4 June 2021 Bloomberg ran the story that fixed the folk version: "Hackers Breached Colonial Pipeline Using Compromised Password". Its first sentence said the hack "was the result of a single compromised password". CNN carried it the same day, adding that the password belonged to "a disused virtual private networking account" without multi-factor authentication, and that it had been "part of a batch of leaked passwords found on the dark web, according to Bloomberg's interview with Carmakal". Wikipedia compresses it further: "a compromised password for an inactive virtual private network (VPN) account, which did not have multi-factor authentication enabled", in an attack that "afflicted computerized equipment managing the pipeline".

Four days after Bloomberg, the Senate Homeland Security committee opened its hearing with the same summary. "Recent news reports indicate that hackers accessed the Colonial system through a compromised password of a virtual private network (VPN) account," the chairman said. The witness was Joseph Blount, Colonial's chief executive. The next day he testified again, to the House Homeland Security committee, beside Charles Carmakal of Mandiant, the incident-response firm hired on the morning the ransom note was found. Both transcripts are published by the Government Publishing Office. Everything below about how the attacker got in comes from them.

## The morning

Blount's written statement gives the timeline. Colonial "identified the ransomware attack just before 5:00 AM Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) on Friday, May 7th, when one of our employees identified the ransom note on a system in the IT network". The employee told the operations supervisor at the control centre, "who put in the stop work order to halt operations throughout the pipeline". The shutdown began at about 5:55; by 6:10 "all 5,500 miles of pipelines had been shut down". The reason, in Blount's words, was "the imperative to isolate and contain the attack to help ensure the malware did not spread to the Operational Technology (OT) network, which controls our pipeline operations, if it had not already". At that hour "we did not know the point of origination of the attack nor the scope of it".

This is the first place the folk story bends. The ransomware was on the IT network, the office side. The joint CISA and FBI advisory of 11 May says DarkSide ransomware was deployed "against the pipeline company's information technology (IT) network" and that "at this time, there is no indication that the entity's operational technology (OT) networks have been directly affected". The pipeline stopped because a person with stop-work authority stopped it, not yet knowing how far the attack reached. Wikipedia's "computerized equipment managing the pipeline" invites a stronger reading than the documents support.

The FBI named DarkSide on 10 May. CISA describes it as ransomware-as-a-service: the developers take a share from the "affiliates" who deploy it. No document in this record identifies a person behind it. Blount paid. The Department of Justice puts the 8 May payment at "approximately 75 bitcoins"; in the Senate the figure was $4.4 million, and Blount said the decision was his: "I chose the option to make the ransom payment in order to get all the tools necessary." The pipeline began returning to service "on Wednesday evening, May 12". On 7 June the DOJ announced it had seized 63.7 of those bitcoins, then worth about $2.3 million, from an address "for which the FBI has the 'private key'". The release does not say how the FBI came to hold it.

## The account

Now the part the headline was about. Blount told the Senate: "we believe the attacker exploited the legacy VPN profile that was not intended to be in use". Asked whether Colonial had required multi-factor authentication before the attack, he answered: "in the case of this particular legacy VPN, it did only have single-factor authentication. It was a complicated password, so I want to be clear on that. It was not a 'Colonial 123' type password." And then: "in our normal operation, we use an RSA token allowance in order to create authentication difficulties for remote access." Colonial had a second factor. This profile sat outside it.

Senator Hassan put it back to him as "you did not have two-step authentication in place" and "a pretty old VPN". His answer is the sentence this piece is built on: "I did reference earlier that the VPN was a legacy VPN ... That we could not see and it did not show up in any pen testing, that is unfortunate."

Carmakal, the next day, was more specific. His prepared statement: "The earliest evidence of compromise that we have identified to date occurred on April 29, 2021. On that date, the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile and an employee's username and password. The legacy VPN profile did not require a one-time passcode to be provided." Mandiant had never worked for Colonial before that week. When the chairman called the profile a vulnerability, Carmakal corrected him: "let me, you know, clarify it as a misconfiguration ... what actually occurred was there was a legacy VPN profile that was in place that wasn't believed to be active, and that enabled an attacker to leverage both the user and the password to login."

Asked why there was no second factor on it: "it was not required for the specific VPN profile that was used for this specific account. It is because the account and the VPN profile wasn't believed to actually be enabled." Who had legitimate access to the password? "One person, as far as we know." Where else had it been used? "We do not know the exact source of the website ... we did find that it was one of the passwords that was stolen from another website. But we don't know exactly where it came from." How the attacker got the username, Mandiant "did not figure out". Blount's statement says Colonial was "still trying to determine how the attackers gained the needed credentials". No public document names the employee, and this piece does not either.

Set the two accounts against the headline. A compromised password: yes. A dark-web batch: the testimony is "stolen from another website" and "we don't know exactly where it came from". An inactive account: the record's words are "not intended to be in use", "wasn't believed to be active", "wasn't believed to actually be enabled", and it accepted a login. A company without multi-factor authentication: a company whose normal remote access used a token, with one profile that did not. The password opened the door. The record's subject is the door.

## What the door is

OWASP, the open project whose Top 10 is a widely used list of application-security risks, files this under A07:2025 Authentication Failures and defines it in one sentence: "When an attacker is able to trick a system into recognizing an invalid or incorrect user as legitimate, this vulnerability is present." Its list of weaknesses includes the one the headline used, an application that "Has missing or ineffective multi-factor authentication." It also includes a quieter one that matches the testimony: an application that "Allows use of weak or ineffective fallbacks if multi-factor authentication is not available." A VPN appliance is not a web application, and the OWASP list is built from tested web applications. The class of weakness fits anyway.

Start with what a password proves. It proves that whoever typed it knows a string, not where they learned it. If the same string was used on a site that was later breached, it is in a list somewhere. OWASP names the result: a system that "Permits automated attacks such as credential stuffing, where the attacker has a breached list of valid usernames and passwords." Carmakal described one password stolen from another website and used against one VPN. A complicated password that has leaked does not need to be guessed.

A second factor changes what a login proves. A one-time code from a token or an app proves possession of the token at that moment, and a leaked password does not come with one. The profile Mandiant found asked for no code. It accepted a username and a password. What the attacker would have done had it demanded a code, the record does not show.

Now the picture to hold in your head. Draw the perimeter as a table, not a wall. Each row is a way in: a VPN profile, a remote-desktop gateway, an API token path, a service account, a vendor portal. Each row has a column for what that path demands: password only, password plus code, password plus key. The security of the perimeter is not the average of that column. It is the weakest row an attacker can reach with a valid username. A VPN appliance is exactly such a table; "profile" is the appliance's word for a row. Colonial's normal rows demanded an RSA token. One row, the legacy profile, demanded a password.

And here is why it is hard to see. The row was legacy, was believed not to be enabled, and did not show up in penetration tests. The record does not say why the tests missed it or when it was created. It still accepted a login. Belief about a configuration is not a configuration. OWASP's phrase, "weak or ineffective fallbacks", is exact: the strong path existed, and a weaker path was still accepted.

## What holds, and what wears down

Colonial's fix is in Carmakal's testimony: "The legacy VPN profile has been completely removed. So, a user, whether an attacker or an employee, would not be able to attempt to login to the system without requiring multifactor authentication." Blount's statement adds "additional layers of protection across our enterprise" and two named outside specialists brought in beside Mandiant. That is the whole public record of what Colonial adopted. It contains no measurement, and it cannot say whether the attacker would have found another row. OWASP's first prevention is to "implement and enforce use of multi-factor authentication to prevent automated credential stuffing, brute force, and stolen credential reuse attacks"; CISA and the FBI, four days after the ransom note, put "Require multi-factor authentication for remote access to OT and IT networks" at the top of their list. Colonial's normal remote access already did that. The legacy profile did not.

The best-documented record of a second factor at scale is Google's, and it needs careful reading. In July 2018 Krebs on Security reported that Google "has not had any of its 85,000+ employees successfully phished on their work-related accounts since early 2017", when it began requiring physical security keys. The spokesperson's own words were narrower: "We have had no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys at Google." That is a qualified statement to a journalist. There is no Google publication behind it.

The published measurement came in 2019, when researchers from Google, NYU and UCSD evaluated over 350,000 real hijacking attempts against Google accounts. For security keys the paper reports 100% of bot attacks and 100% of phishing attacks prevented, with confidence intervals of plus or minus 25 and 28 points, and gives no figure at all for targeted attacks. Google's blog post on the study says "zero users that exclusively use security keys fell victim to targeted phishing during our investigation"; on-device prompts prevented 90% of targeted attacks and SMS codes 76%. The authors' conclusion is that for at-risk users "security keys offer the best immediate solution". They also measured friction: of users whose sign-in first failed at a two-factor step, 88.2% got in within the same session, against 89.5% of those who first failed at a password. The authors called that similar.

Why keys rather than codes? A one-time code can be relayed: a phishing page asks for the password and the code and forwards both to the real site. Google's 2016 security-key paper says one-time passcodes "as a second factor are still vulnerable to relatively common attacks such as phishing". A security key instead signs a challenge with a key bound to the site's origin; in the paper's words, if the key does not agree that the credential "is associated with the web origin that requested the signature, it rejects the request". The W3C's WebAuthn standard makes the rule general: a credential "can only be accessed by origins belonging to that Relying Party. This scoping is enforced jointly by conforming User Agents and authenticators." A phishing site at the wrong origin cannot obtain a signature for the right one.

Then the limit, and it is the one to remember alongside Colonial. Krebs's 2018 piece stated the folk theory of two-factor: "even if thieves manage to phish or steal your password, they still cannot log in to your account unless they also hack or possess that second factor." In September 2022 Uber published a post-incident statement. A contractor's corporate password had likely been "purchased" on the dark web after malware infected a personal device. "The attacker then repeatedly tried to log in to the contractor's Uber account. Each time, the contractor received a two-factor login approval request, which initially blocked access. Eventually, however, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker successfully logged in." The second factor was present, enforced and held by the right person. It failed because it was a question put to a human, over and over, until the answer changed. A push prompt proves possession of a phone; it does not prove that the person holding it is the one typing the password. A security key that tests for user presence while signing the challenge from the browser making the request offers no separate approval button for someone else's login. Nothing in the Colonial record suggests this would have happened there. Nothing rules it out.

The regulator's answer is worth one paragraph, because it shows what a rule can and cannot do. Before May 2021, the GAO says, TSA's pipeline programme consisted of "voluntary security guidelines and security reviews". Colonial had one such review scheduled for the end of July; Blount told the Senate it "probably would not have resulted in finding that legacy VPN. Again, they do not actually go into the system. It is a questionnaire format type thing." TSA's first directive, issued 26 May 2021, required incident reports to CISA within 12 hours, a named coordinator and a self-assessment. Its second, issued 19 July, required mitigation measures, a contingency plan and an annual third-party architecture review. That directive's 2021 text is unpublished security-sensitive information; its 2024 renewal, which says it continues measures "first issued by TSA on July 26, 2021", requires "Multi-factor authentication, or other logical and physical security controls that supplement password authentication to provide risk mitigation commensurate to multi-factor authentication". A directive can require a factor, with a compensating-controls clause. It cannot find a profile believed disabled. Only an inventory can.
