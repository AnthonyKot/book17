# Three Defaults, Not One

*The MongoDB ransom wave, 2017. The headline says the database shipped open and version 3.6 closed it. The vendor's own bug tracker says the fix was three years old, and the wave fell where no default reached.*

On 27 December 2016 a Dutch researcher opened one more MongoDB server that asked no one for a password. Where its data had been was a single database named WARNING, holding one document with a ransom note. Within two weeks the trackers' count of such databases passed 27,000. The headline version has held since: MongoDB shipped open to the internet by default, and the ransom wave forced the fix in version 3.6.

The vendor's own bug tracker says otherwise. The ticket that made the official packages listen only on localhost was closed in April 2014, nearly three years before the first ransom note. What 3.6 changed was where the default lived. That is the argument of this post: there were three defaults, not one, each living in a different place, and the wave fell on the installs none of them reached. A default is measured by what it reaches, not by when it is announced.

## The break

The folk story has four tellers, and each sentence is nearly true. Brian Krebs, 10 January 2017: "If installed on a server with the default settings, for example, MongoDB allows anyone to browse the databases, download them, or even write over them and delete them". The Register, the day before: "up until recently, the software's default configuration is insecure". Wikipedia: "Because of MongoDB's default security configuration, which allows any user full access to the database, data from tens of thousands of MongoDB installations has been stolen". And The Next Web, on the day 3.6 was announced: MongoDB "will no longer come with an unsafe configuration out of the box". One default, insecure, fixed by the wave.

The first sighting was Victor Gevers's, reported by Bleeping Computer. Gevers had spent years finding open servers and telling their owners. On 27 December he found a MongoDB server "left open to external connections and without a password on the admin account". "No password" was the week's shorthand; the condition under it was a server with access control off, which asks no one who they are. The ransom note read "SEND 0.2 BTC TO THIS ADDRESS … AND CONTACT THIS EMAIL WITH YOUR IP OF YOUR SERVER TO RECOVER YOUR DATABASE !". Gevers was precise about the class: "This is not ransomware. Database does not get encrypted. It only gets replaced." That owner had a backup from just before, and restored it.

The count began the next week and never had one denominator. On 4 January Shodan's John Matherly put it at "just over 1,800" hit, with at least eleven owners paying. On 6 January Niall Merrigan, tracking with Gevers, tweeted 10,500. On 9 January The Register had it going "from 12,000 earlier today to 27,633, over the course of about 12 hours", with fifteen distinct attackers, one of them on 15,482 instances. Krebs on 10 January: "at least 29,000 … now erased". The population those counts were drawn from was 99,000 by Gevers's estimate, "more than 52,000" by Shodan's, and about 42,000 by the arithmetic of Bleeping's "about 25%".

What those numbers counted is in Krebs's description of the method. A Shodan query for MongoDB returns a list of database names. Gevers ran it and saw "far too many" named "readme," "readnow," "encrypted" and "readplease", each holding one document with a demand. Merrigan's check: wait a few minutes, re-run the query, and the same addresses "now have a different database title and a new ransom note". Groups were "re-hacking the same servers and rewriting each other ransom notes", so victims who paid sometimes paid the wrong group. In 84 cases Gevers could find no "trace of data exfiltration". The count was of ransom-note names seen through a scanner, each host under whichever note was written last. "Erased" holds. "Held for ransom" mostly meant deleted.

On 6 January the vendor spoke. Andreas Nilsson, MongoDB's director of product security: "These attacks are preventable with the extensive security protections built into MongoDB. You need to use these features correctly". The operative line: "The most popular installer for MongoDB (RPM) limits network access to localhost by default. Use this configuration too if you're installing via another means." Victims were told to "assume that the attacker has a copy of all data".

The wave came back. On 4 September 2017 Bleeping reported three new groups had "hijacked over 26,000 servers" in a week, 22,449 of them by one group. Gevers's open questions that day are this post's: "Is this a lack of knowledge? Did they mess up the [MongoDB] security settings without knowing it? Are they running on older version without safe defaults and other vulnerabilities?" Four days later the vendor's Davi Ottenheimer wrote that the new wave "does not indicate a new risk, just new targets".

## The mechanism

Nothing in this story is a bug. Every line of code did what it said. OWASP's Top 10 files it under Security Misconfiguration, defined as "when a system, application, or cloud service is set up incorrectly from a security perspective, creating vulnerabilities", and two lines from its list of symptoms are this whole incident: "Unnecessary features are enabled or installed (e.g., unnecessary ports, services, pages, accounts, testing frameworks, or privileges)." and "For upgraded systems, the latest security features are disabled or not configured securely."

The whiteboard has two switches. A server process listens on an address and a port. Bound to 0.0.0.0 it answers on every interface the machine has, including the public one; bound to 127.0.0.1 it answers only to programs on the same machine. MongoDB's port is 27017. The second switch is access control, which asks a connecting client who it is. MongoDB's setting for it, `security.authorization`, reads "Default: disabled" in the manual, and disabled means "A user can access any database and perform any action". With the first switch open and the second off, whoever finds the port has the database. The attack in the record is only that. Merrigan's summary, via Krebs: "Use Shodan to scan for open MongoDB databases, connect using anonymous access, and then list all available databases."

Now the first switch, which the record shows had three defaults.

**The binary's.** Before April 2017 the server's listener read, for an empty bind setting:

```cpp
if (*ips == '\0') {
    out.push_back(SockAddr("0.0.0.0", port));  // IPv4 all
```

and the option's help text said "comma separated list of ip addresses to listen on - all local ips by default". Nothing set meant everywhere.

**The package's.** In November 2011 Roman Shtylman filed SERVER-4216 against the Debian package: its config file "Does not have a 'bind_ip 127.0.0.1' option set". His ask: "The default should be to lockdown as much as possible and only expose if the user requests it". It was closed on 8 April 2014 with fix version 2.6.0, the day 2.6 shipped. From then on the RPM and DEB packages wrote a config line that said localhost; the binary underneath still said everywhere. The 3.6 release notes put it exactly: "From MongoDB versions 2.6 to 3.4, only the binaries from the official MongoDB RPM … and DEB … packages would bind to localhost by default". The Register's "up until recently" was wrong in both directions: the package default was nearly three years old, and the binary's had not moved.

**3.6's.** The same line of code with a different constant: `SockAddr("127.0.0.1", port);  // IPv4 localhost`. That is the whole of the binding change.

Who fell through to the first default? Anyone on a tarball, anyone on a version older than 2.6, and anyone on an image built from either. Matherly's 2015 census: 2.4.14 "seems to be the last version that still listened to 0.0.0.0 by default", and the exposed hosts sat in clouds, where "cloud images don't get updated as often".

And a fourth population the folk story leaves out. In December 2015 the single most common exposed version was 3.0.7, whose packages already defaulted to localhost. Matherly's reading: "a lot of people are changing the default configuration of MongoDB to something less secure and aren't enabling any firewall", possibly "upgrading their instances but using their existing, insecure configuration files". That is OWASP's "upgraded systems" line, thirteen months before the wave. A default that lives in a package's config file is a property of one install path, not of the product. Every other path inherits the binary's answer, and a config file copied from a tutorial carries its own.

Why it is hard to see: the safe setting looks like a broken one. A database bound to localhost refuses an application server on any other host, and the fix ticket's own user summary prints the override, `bindIp: 0.0.0.0`, as the way to "make MongoDB servers accept connections from remote and local sources", with a request to review the Security Checklist first. The override is two lines. The checklist is a page.

The second switch was never moved. Eliot Horowitz, MongoDB's CTO, on 3.6: "If you explicitly turn it on, but don't turn on authentication, we can't help you at that point". The manual's `security.authorization` still reads "Default: disabled". The new default does not close the door. It moves the door one step further from the tutorial.

## The defense

Read the vendor's statement next to the change it shipped, in order. On 6 January 2017 the advice was that the RPM installer already binds to localhost: "Use this configuration too if you're installing via another means". On 7 March, nine weeks later, Spencer Jackson of MongoDB filed SERVER-28229, "Bind to localhost by default": "MongoDB binaries should bind to localhost by default. This will allow small deployments and testing environments to be used from localhost, while not being accessible from the internet". Three parts: bind to 127.0.0.1 unless told otherwise, print a startup warning when no bind address was given, add a `--bind_ip_all` flag for the old behaviour. Jackson's commit landed on 28 April 2017, fix version 3.5.7, a development release.

The warning it added is the best single line in the defense record, because it names the trade-off:

```
** ATTENTION: The server is bound to localhost.
**          Remote systems will be unable to connect to this server.
**          Start the server with --bind_ip <address> to specify which IP addresses it
**          should serve responses from, or with --bind_ip_all to bind to all interfaces.
```

The official Docker image's maintainers saw it at once and opened an issue that "3.5.7 now binds to localhost-only by default". Ottenheimer's September post laid out all three defaults in the vendor's own words: localhost binding "since release 2.6.0 … in our most popular deployment package formats, RPM and deb"; then "Beginning with development release version 3.5.7, localhost-only binding is implemented directly in the MongoDB server"; and "a warning to our download center" for "non-packaged distributions".

The announcement came on 8 November 2017: "With MongoDB 3.6, this default behavior is implemented directly in the server itself, so all MongoDB packages across all platforms refuse all external connections to the database unless otherwise explicitly configured by the administrator". That was the announcement, not the release. The Next Web the same day: "The final release is expected at some point in December". The source tree's r3.6.0 tag is dated 1 December, and on 5 December Horowitz told the announce list that 3.6 was "now generally available for production deployments".

The sequence is the real post-mortem. The statement in January said users must set it. The ticket in March said the product would. The announcement in November said it did. The release in December shipped it. Seven months passed between commit and general availability, and the September wave, 26,000 in a week, fell inside them.

Did it work? Take each measurement with its method. Matherly's 2015 census pulled every MongoDB banner from Shodan and summed the reported database sizes: "nearly 30,000 instances" without authorization and 595.2 TB in July 2015; "at least 35,000" and 684.8 TB in December. In May 2020 his after-figure: exposed MongoDB data fell "from 24 TB to 12.5 TB" between 2018 and 2020, "the only database among the 3 I looked at where the amount of exposed data has actually decreased". He also wrote that the 2018 level "was already drastically lower than the others due to a series of ransomware attacks", and that "Those attacks are still on-going". So exposed data fell, with two caveats the record itself supplies: the 2020 post does not say it measured the way 2015 did, and Shodan's own reading credits the wipes for part of the fall.

The host count is another matter. The only after-series is ZDNet's, in July 2020: "From the 60,000 MongoDB servers left exposed online in early 2017, the needle has barely moved to 48,000". It names no source. Exposed data fell. Whether exposed hosts fell is contested, and this post does not claim it.

What the record does show is the reach of a default. In February 2019 another 3,000 were ransomed, and in 2020 a single campaign took 22,900; ZDNet's explanation was "incorrect MongoDB configuration tutorials", honest mistakes, and "server images that come packed with a misconfigured MongoDB system out of the box". A changed default reaches new installs as they are created and old ones as they are rebuilt, and nothing in between. Horowitz's sentence was conditional, "If you don't explicitly turn it on", and on its own terms it holds. The Next Web's gloss on it, that the ransoms "won't be an issue any more, as of MongoDB 3.6", is the claim the later waves test.

The one defense that could look at the installed base was the instrument. The same query that found the victims is the one a defender runs first: Shodan by port or product name, or Shadowserver's report for your own address space. The vendor pointed at alerts to detect internet exposure in both its January and September posts. No published figure says how many operators ran either, and the vendor has published no count of installs that kept the 3.6 default. The measured defense here is a default, and a default is measured by what it reached.
