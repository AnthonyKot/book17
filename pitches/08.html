Chapter 08 — pitches (standard mode: the user picks; nothing is drafted yet)

Sources for every claim below are indexed in resources/sources/08/SOURCES.md; the quoted phrases exist in the saved excerpts. §6 contract: a legitimate M.E.Doc update mechanism as NotPetya's delivery channel; Maersk's recovery from one surviving domain controller; signed updates, offline backups, and the cost of ten days. Caveats carried into every pitch: the indictment states allegations, not findings; NotPetya is outside OWASP's web-application scope; the Ghana location and blackout rest on Wired's interviews rather than a fetched Maersk document; and no fetched primary record settles whether M.E.Doc updates were signed, so that detail remains OPEN.

A. The backup that was not a backup — CHOSEN

Anchor: the DOJ indictment's alleged sequence (three M.E.Doc Update Files modified before the June 27 delivery) and Cisco's incident-response finding that the actor used stolen credentials to manipulate the update server, set against Gavin Ashton's first-person Maersk account: disaster recovery covered loss of “a site or datacentre,” but nothing had covered “We have lost everything, everywhere, all at once,” and the first restored domain controller ran on a Surface Pro 4. Folk claim tested: Wired calls the synchronized controllers a “decentralized backup strategy” and the Ghana survivor “all thanks to a power outage”; the survival story may HOLD, but calling live, mutually synchronizing replicas backups is OVERSTATED, and the fetched first-party Maersk record does not confirm Ghana. Defense: OWASP says, “Use digital signatures or similar mechanisms to verify the software or data is from the expected source and has not been altered,” while NCSC requires offline copies kept separate and Microsoft says forest recovery starts by restoring at least one controller from “an available backup”; the record shows Maersk instead recovered its directory from a surviving live controller, used manual workarounds, and says it rebuilt the network “in just 10 days while keeping our customers’ cargo moving.” Takeaway: replication keeps a service available through an ordinary failure but also faithfully carries a destructive state; a recovery copy has to leave the production trust boundary on purpose.

B. The update server was only half the mechanism

Anchor: paragraphs 37–38 of the DOJ indictment allege that modified M.E.Doc Update Files installed command capability before June 27, then temporary rerouting delivered NotPetya; Cisco's closer record says a backdoor had been inserted into three update versions and that “all installations came through the M.E.Doc update system.” Folk claim tested: BleepingComputer says “the M.E.Doc update servers were responsible for the initial NotPetya infections” and that the mechanism “didn't use HTTPS or cryptographically-signed binaries”; the first claim HOLDS, but the second is OPEN in the primary record, and “the server did it” is MISSING the already-installed backdoor and the later command path. Defense: OWASP's signature line is the direct answer to an altered unsigned artifact, but its further requirements for review, segregation and access control meet more of this record—Cisco found stolen administrator credentials, modified code and server configuration—while Maersk's surviving-controller recovery and NCSC's separate offline copies cover the failure after prevention. Takeaway: a signature proves a bounded statement about an artifact and signer, not that the build system, signing authority, update service and code-review path deserve to share one trust boundary.

C. Nine days, ten days, two different claims

Anchor: Maersk's Q2 presentation says the malware entered through Ukrainian tax-filing software, manual workarounds served customers, and vessel control was maintained; its annual report puts the loss at “USD 250-300m,” while its later white paper says it rebuilt the network “in just 10 days while keeping our customers’ cargo moving.” Folk claim tested: Darknet Diaries says, “After about nine days of working on it 24/7, they were able to have a functioning network again. This ultimately cost Maersk 350 million dollars”; the day count is a different recovery threshold, and the cost is WRONG against Maersk's reported range. Defense: OWASP's signed-update line addresses entry, while NCSC's offline-backup and rebuild planning address impact; the Ghana controller is evidence of what isolation can preserve only if attributed to Wired, and Ashton's account shows Maersk's plans had not covered simultaneous loss of every controller. Takeaway: “recovered” needs an object and a threshold—directory, functioning network, customer channel, or full application estate—because a ten-day number otherwise turns continuity work into a magic restore button.

D. Ransomware was the cover story

Anchor: paragraph 33 of the DOJ indictment alleges that NotPetya was designed to masquerade as ransomware and that “the purported ransomware purpose of NotPetya was a ruse”; ESET independently found recovery impossible, and Ashton writes that “there was nobody to pay, it was designed purely with destruction in mind.” Folk claim tested: BBC's opening calls it the “NotPetya ransomware attack,” while Wikipedia says, “It is said to have been the most destructive cyberattack ever”; the first is WRONG as a statement of function, and the superlative is OPEN because neither page supplies a reproducible comparison. Defense: OWASP's signature check is prevention before execution, NCSC says offline copies are essential when malware is merely presented as ransomware, and Maersk's reports show what actually continued—manual customer workarounds and vessel control—before the network rebuild reached ten days. Takeaway: when the recovery promise is intentionally absent, ransom is scenery; integrity engineering needs both a gate before trusted code runs and a recovery state the running code cannot rewrite.

Recommendation

Pick A. It carries the whole §6 contract in one argument and offers the chapter's cleanest whiteboard distinction: synchronized replicas share the failure domain; an offline, tested backup does not. Its ledger can honestly mark “decentralized backup strategy” OVERSTATED, the update-channel shorthand MISSING the staged backdoor and rerouting, the ransomware label WRONG, the Darknet Diaries cost WRONG, and the Ghana specifics OPEN outside Wired's reporting. B would make the A08 mechanism sharper but cost the Maersk recovery its human and operational weight, and its title question cannot be closed until a primary source settles signing; fold its update/backdoor sequence and OPEN signing row into A. C would give the crispest numerical correction but cost mechanism depth and risk becoming a chapter about recovery metrics; fold its nine-versus-ten-days and USD 350m-versus-250–300m rows into A. D would give the simplest WRONG verdict but cost the category-specific trust-boundary argument and resemble a generic ransomware chapter; fold the ruse into A's break and ledger.
