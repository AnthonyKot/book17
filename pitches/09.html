Chapter 09 — pitches (standard mode: the user picks; nothing is drafted yet)

Sources and saved excerpts for every pitch are indexed in resources/sources/09/SOURCES.md. §6 contract: Target’s alerts existed, told through the Senate Commerce timeline; Equifax’s expired inspection certificate is the mirror; the defense is the M-Trends dwell-time trend and what actually moved it. Caveats carried into every pitch: the Senate report says it is “based on media reports and expert analyses” and its FireEye account comes from Bloomberg Businessweek; Target’s and Equifax’s remediation statements record adoption, not measured success; M-Trends measures Mandiant Consulting investigations, not all breaches or a controlled intervention; the FTC complaint alleges, while the stipulated order requires; and Target is an enterprise/payment-network case viewed through a web-application category.

A. Evaluated, not ignored — CHOSEN

Anchor: John Mulligan’s prepared Senate statement on 26 March 2014 says intruder activity was “detected by our computer security systems, logged and surfaced to the SOC and evaluated by our security professionals.” Beside it, the Senate staff report’s timeline says FireEye “triggered urgent alerts” and that the team “neither reacted to the alarms” — a stronger sentence the report attributes to Businessweek. Folk claim tested: CBS said “the attack could have been prevented if Target had reacted to warnings from its own cyber security system.” Krebs’s headline made the remembered cause “Target Hackers Broke in Via HVAC Company”; the first is OVERSTATED because the Senate record says only Target “could have potentially blocked” the malware and Target says the surfaced activity was evaluated, while the second is MISSING the alert-to-judgment path after entry. Defense: OWASP says to establish “effective monitoring and alerting use cases including playbooks”; Target later reported “additional rules, alerts, centralized log feeds and enabled additional logging capabilities.” No saved Target document measures their result, while M-Trends 2024 supplies the broader outcome record — 46% of its cases were found internally and 54% externally, with attacker notification materially affecting ransomware cases. Takeaway: an alert is neither a detection failure nor a response; the defendable unit is the whole route from telemetry, through a judgment with context, to an action someone can verify.

B. The certificate that did not cause the breach

Anchor: the House Equifax report says the inspection certificate expired on 31 January 2016, leaving Equifax without traffic visibility for “nineteen months” and recording that “at least 324” SSL certificates had expired; it also says inspection resumed on 29 July 2017 and the team detected a suspicious request “Almost immediately”. Folk claim tested: Wikipedia says, “The certificate had been expired for nine months”; that is WRONG against the House document’s date and nineteen-month discovery clock, while GAO’s different formulation — “about 10 months before the breach occurred” — explains how the reference points may have been collapsed, and the certificate did not create the Struts entry flaw. Defense: OWASP’s response-playbook line is set against the control that visibly worked as soon as its dependency was restored, then the FTC order’s required asset inventory, intrusion protection across legacy assets, and periodic safeguard testing; GAO says Equifax reported continuous-monitoring changes but adds, “We did not independently assess” them. Takeaway: monitor the monitor — a green product icon means little if certificate lifecycle, traffic decryption, and control ownership can silently remove the sensor’s sight.

C. Ten days is not a defender score

Anchor: Mandiant’s 2024 infographic takes global median dwell time in its investigations from 416 days in 2011 to 10 in 2023, but its executive report says 70% of ransomware cases were learned of externally and 76% of those external notices came from the adversary; the 2026 report then records a rise from 11 days to 14, “driven largely by long-term espionage and DPRK IT worker operations.” Folk claim tested: The Register’s headline says, “Mandiant: Orgs are detecting cybercriminals faster than ever”; the long fall HOLDS, but reading it as a defender-only improvement is OVERSTATED because the incident mix, attackers announcing ransomware, and stealth-focused cases move the same median. Defense: OWASP asks for playbooks that produce quick response; M-Trends names red-team measurement and tabletop exercises in 2024, then in 2026 recommends centralized long-term logs and warns that standard 90-day retention misses some long-lived cases — a documented practice and outcome series, not proof that any one control caused the decline. Takeaway: track compromise-to-internal-detection, compromise-to-containment, and who supplied the first signal; one global median mixes all three stories and can improve when an attacker becomes louder.

D. According to Businessweek

Anchor: the Senate Commerce report begins by calling itself an account “based on media reports and expert analyses” and says the complete story may await Target’s forensic examination; at the crucial alert step it attributes details to “a Bloomberg Businessweek report” and uses the qualifiers “reportedly” and “It is possible”, while Mulligan’s same-day statement confirms detection, logging, surfacing, evaluation, and later Justice Department notification without adopting the report’s “neither reacted” wording. Folk claim tested: Wikipedia turns that qualified chain into a settled sentence — Target “had ample time to disrupt the theft” but “did not act to prevent” it. Defense: OWASP’s required escalation playbook meets a thin record — Target announced new rules, alerts and centralized feeds, but no fetched document shows whether the post-breach decision path worked; M-Trends therefore supplies only a population-level mirror, not validation of Target’s fix. Takeaway: the chapter can tell what the tools saw and what Target later acknowledged, while keeping the identity of each alert recipient, the decision made, and the counterfactual prevention claim OPEN instead of laundering a famous retelling through a government cover.

Recommendation

Pick A — Evaluated, not ignored. It satisfies the whole §6 contract while producing the chapter’s cleanest honest divergence: definite prevention is OVERSTATED by the Senate’s “could have potentially blocked” formulation, the HVAC-company story is MISSING the operational path after entry, and why the judgments did not stop the breach remains OPEN; Equifax then supplies a concrete mirror in which a control was present but blind, and M-Trends supplies the measured defense trend with its confounders. B folds into A as the mirror section and at least two ledger rows (nine versus nineteen months; one certificate versus at least 324), but choosing it would make Equifax the break and breach §6’s exclusive Target job. C folds in as the defense section and a ledger row on “faster than ever” but choosing it would turn the chapter into a metrics essay and reduce the promised Senate timeline to an example. D folds into A as the source-status caveat and an OPEN row on who decided what after the alerts; choosing it would spend the chapter’s main energy auditing the Senate report and leave less room to put the alert-to-action mechanism in the reader’s hands.
