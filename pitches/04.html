Chapter 04 — pitches (standard mode: the user picks; nothing is drafted yet)

Sources and exact quotation text are in resources/sources/04/. §6 contract: show that encryption is not hashing through Adobe's 2013 dump, its ECB pattern leak and password-hint crossword; set that against slow salted hashing, with LinkedIn 2012's unsalted SHA-1 as the mirror.

Caveats carried into every pitch: the regulators confirm the same-password/same-output failure but do not name 3DES or ECB; that identification rests on the saved CC BY paper because the original Stricture analysis could not be found. Adobe's replacement hash and LinkedIn's post-breach hash are not specified, their work factors are not public, and the Canadian report says many remediation details cannot be disclosed, so none of these records proves a measured reduction in password recovery.

A. The backup that defeated the migration

Anchor: the Irish Data Protection Commissioner's Case Study 16 — the attackers “identified and removed data from a backup server”, even though Adobe said it “was actually hashing and salting passwords within a new system for a number of years”; it kept the encrypted old database “as a backup measure in case of issues with the new system.” Folk claim tested: Wikipedia's retelling says Adobe “has not salted them”, which describes the stolen old store but misses the already-running replacement and turns a failed retirement into simple ignorance. Defense set against the attack: OWASP says, “Store passwords using strong adaptive and salted hashing functions with a work factor (delay factor),” while the Australian regulator records the concrete completion step — “decommissioning the affected server and abolishing the use of password hints” — and LinkedIn supplies the required mirror with its own “long-planned transition” from hashing alone to hashing and salting. Takeaway: a cryptographic migration is not a defense until the reversible fallback, replicas and backups stop being another way to retrieve the secret.

B. The crossword was not decryption

Anchor: Canada's PIPEDA finding explains the mechanism without publishing credentials: Adobe's format “scrambled identical passwords in the same way,” so a plain-text hint for one account could expose other accounts with the same ciphertext; the Stajano paper names the construction as “3DES” with each block treated independently “in ECB mode.” Folk claim tested: Have I Been Pwned says 153 million accounts were breached “with each containing” a plain-text hint and that many passwords were “resolved back to plain text”; the Irish regulator instead says hints belonged to “a small percentage of users”, and the documented method is inference from repeated ciphertext and clues; none of these records says the encryption key was recovered. Defense set against the attack: OWASP's adaptive salted hash removes reversibility; NIST says a salt prevents multiple accounts from having “the same hashed password”, while a cost factor makes “each password guess more expensive”; Australia's report records that Adobe abolished hints. Takeaway: the famous crossword did not defeat 3DES — ECB exposed equality, the hints supplied candidate answers, salting separates equal passwords, and stretching makes each remaining guess costlier.

C. Hashing is not encryption — and not enough

Anchor: LinkedIn's 12 June 2012 statement says its old database “hashed passwords” and its replacement “both hashes and salts the passwords”; Harsha et al. identify the stolen values as “unsalted SHA-1 password hashes” and add that LinkedIn was not using “key-stretching tools such as PBKDF2 or Argon2”. Folk claim tested: WIRED called them “hashed (encrypted) passwords” that had been “decrypted and posted online”, collapsing a one-way check into reversible encryption and password guessing into decryption — precisely the distinctions the Adobe record makes visible. Defense set against the attack: OWASP requires all three properties — “adaptive and salted hashing functions with a work factor” — and NIST says the work factor exists to make the cost of a guessing attack “high or prohibitive”; Adobe's ECB store lacked the first distinction, while LinkedIn's fast, unsalted SHA-1 lacked the other two. Takeaway: encryption, hashing, salting and stretching are not synonyms or a maturity ladder; they do different jobs, and a password verifier needs the last three together.

Recommendation

Pick A. It gives the chapter the detail the headline hid and the cleanest honest MISSING row: “has not salted them” omits a migration that had run for years but left the reversible old store online as a backup; it also provides the best documented defense, because the regulator records decommissioning that server rather than merely recommending a hash. B folds into A as the mechanism and two sharper ledger rows: “each” account had a hint is WRONG against the Irish “a small percentage of users”, while “resolved back to plain text” is MISSING the documented inference route from equal ciphertext and clues. C becomes A's defense mirror and another ledger row: WIRED's “hashed (encrypted)” and “decrypted” are WRONG terms, followed by LinkedIn's documented salted transition and the paper's missing-stretching qualification. Choosing B instead would cost the chapter its least familiar record-side discovery and risk retelling the famous crossword without explaining why the vulnerable database still existed. Choosing C would make LinkedIn, not Adobe, the narrative anchor; it would also lean more heavily on a later paper because LinkedIn's own statement names neither SHA-1 nor a work factor. In A, both alternatives survive as ledger rows and full mechanism/defense sections while the Adobe regulator record remains the spine.
