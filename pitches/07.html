Chapter 07 — pitches (standard mode: the user picks; nothing is drafted yet)

Sources for every pitch are indexed in resources/sources/07/SOURCES.md; the quoted phrases below are from the excerpts saved there. §6 contract: one legacy account from the Senate testimony; what a second factor would have changed; Google's published result on hardware keys for staff; MFA fatigue (Uber 2022) as the limit.

A. Not intended to be in use

Anchor: the House hearing of 9 June 2021 (CHRG-117hhrg45085) — Charles Carmakal's prepared statement ("the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile and an employee's username and password. The legacy VPN profile did not require a one-time passcode") and his answers under questioning — read alongside Blount's Senate testimony of 8 June (CHRG-117shrg46569). Folk claim tested: "one leaked password shut down the pipeline, and Colonial had no MFA" — the record says the password was "complicated", known to "one person", found among passwords "stolen from another website", used on a profile that "wasn't believed to actually be enabled" and "did not show up in any pen testing", while normal remote access "use[d] an RSA token"; the first login was 29 April, the ransom note 7 May, and the pipeline was stopped by Colonial's own "stop work order", not by the malware. Defense: OWASP's first prevention ("implement and enforce use of multi-factor authentication") set against what Google actually published — the 2016 Security Keys paper, the 2018 "no reported or confirmed account takeovers" statement, and the 2019 WWW paper's Table 3 — then Uber's September 2022 post ("the contractor accepted one") as the limit of push-MFA. Takeaway: the account nobody thinks is live is the one with no second factor on it; the control that failed here was the inventory of ways in, not the password.

B. The FBI got the ransom back

Anchor: the DOJ press release of 7 June 2021 and the Senate transcript of the next morning (Portman: "the recovery of 63.7 of those bitcoins, but DOJ will not be able to recover those ransom payments in other cases"). Folk claim tested: "the FBI recovered Colonial's ransom" — the record shows 63.7 of "approximately 75 bitcoins", "currently valued at approximately $2.3 million" against a payment reported as $4.4 million, seized under a warrant because "the FBI has the 'private key'" for one address, with no statement of how. Defense: thin for A07 — this is a chapter about response and money-tracing, and the authentication mechanism would be a paragraph. Takeaway: how a partial, one-off recovery became "they got it back".

C. Nineteen days from voluntary to directive

Anchor: TSA Security Directive Pipeline-2021-01 (26 May 2021, described in the Federal Register ratification notice 86 FR 52953) and -02 (19 July 2021; the public text is the 2024 renewal SD-02E, whose §III.C.2 requires "Multi-factor authentication, or other logical and physical security controls that supplement password authentication"), with GAO-21-105263 on what TSA's pipeline programme was before. Folk claim tested: "pipeline cybersecurity was unregulated" and "Colonial refused a TSA review" — the record shows voluntary guidelines and questionnaire-style reviews (Blount: it "probably would not have resulted in finding that legacy VPN"), then a mandate with a compensating-controls escape hatch. Defense: MFA as a regulator writes it, set against MFA as Google measured it. Takeaway: a directive can require a second factor; it cannot find the profile nobody knew was enabled.

D. Google's number, read carefully

Anchor: the defense record itself — Krebs's 23 July 2018 article carrying Google's statement (85,000+ employees, "no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys"), the 2019 Google/NYU/UCSD paper whose Table 3 gives security keys 100% ± 25% against bots and 100% ± 28% against phishing and no figure for targeted attacks, and the 2016 Security Keys paper on why a key is phishing-resistant (the origin is bound into the signature; WebAuthn L3 §1: "the public key credential can only be accessed by origins belonging to that Relying Party"). Folk claim tested: "hardware keys stopped 100% of phishing at Google" — a statement to a journalist plus a wide confidence interval, not a published zero. Defense-first chapter, with Colonial as the case that shows what the key would have refused to do (sign for a stranger's VPN login) and Uber 2022 as what a push prompt will do. Takeaway: the best-documented authentication defense in the book is still documented mostly by its vendor, and the reader should know exactly how much the numbers carry.

Recommendation

Pick A. It is the only pitch that covers the whole §6 contract from the primary record — the legacy account from the two transcripts, what a second factor would have changed, Google's published result, and Uber 2022 as the limit — and it yields the strongest ledger: "no MFA" is WRONG (RSA tokens on normal remote access), "one password" is OVERSTATED (the profile, not the password, was the flaw), "shut down the pipeline" is MISSING the stop-work order, and "how the password leaked" is OPEN (Carmakal: "we did not figure out exactly how"). B folds into A as one OVERSTATED ledger row; C supplies A's closing paragraph (SD-02E §III.C.2) and one row on the TSA review; D's careful reading of Table 3 becomes A's defense section, so none of the three is lost. Choosing B instead would cost the authentication mechanism and the hardware-key defense — the chapter would need a §8 amendment because it would not meet §6. Choosing C would cost the mechanism section its depth (a directive is a requirement, not a mechanism) and push the chapter toward policy, where the book has no other chapter to balance it. Choosing D would invert the book's skeleton — the folk story tested would be the defense's, not the incident's — and leave the incident ledger with two or three rows where the pilots have five to ten.
