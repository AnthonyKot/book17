Chapter 7 · A07:2025 Authentication Failures

Not Intended to Be in Use

You know the headline: one leaked password, an old VPN account with no second factor, and the fuel supply of the American East Coast stopped for most of a week. The record adds the detail the headline drops. The profile was believed not to be enabled; it did not show up in Colonial's penetration tests; and on 29 April 2021 it accepted a username and a password and let someone in. The password was the key. The door nobody thought was there was the flaw.

The break

The story the public has is the one Bloomberg published on 4 June 2021, under the headline "Hackers Breached Colonial Pipeline Using Compromised Password". CNN carried it the same day: the password "had been linked to a disused virtual private networking account used for remote access", the account "was not guarded by an extra layer of security known as multi-factor authentication", and the password "was part of a batch of leaked passwords found on the dark web, according to Bloomberg's interview with Carmakal". Wikipedia's article compresses it further: "a compromised password for an inactive virtual private network (VPN) account, which did not have multi-factor authentication enabled", and, in the lede, an attack "that afflicted computerized equipment managing the pipeline". Four days after Bloomberg, the Senate Homeland Security committee opened its hearing with the same summary: "Recent news reports indicate that hackers accessed the Colonial system through a compromised password of a virtual private network (VPN) account."

The record that tests this is two transcripts. On 8 June 2021 Joseph Blount, Colonial's chief executive, testified to the Senate committee. On 9 June he and Charles Carmakal of Mandiant, the incident-response firm Colonial hired, testified to the House Homeland Security committee. Both are printed by the Government Publishing Office. Everything below about the intrusion comes from what those two men said under oath, and from the written statements attached to their testimony.

Carmakal's prepared statement gives the start. "The earliest evidence of compromise that we have identified to date occurred on April 29, 2021. On that date, the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile and an employee's username and password. The legacy VPN profile did not require a one-time passcode to be provided." Mandiant had not worked for Colonial before. It was engaged "on the early morning of May 7, 2021" by the law firm Hunton Andrews Kurth, on Colonial's behalf, "to help respond to the ransomware event that was discovered earlier that day".

Blount's written statement gives the morning. Colonial "identified the ransomware attack just before 5:00 AM Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) on Friday, May 7th, when one of our employees identified the ransom note on a system in the IT network". The employee notified the operations supervisor at the control centre, "who put in the stop work order to halt operations throughout the pipeline". At about 5:55 AM the shutdown began; by 6:10 AM "they confirmed that all 5,500 miles of pipelines had been shut down". The reason, in his words, was "the imperative to isolate and contain the attack to help ensure the malware did not spread to the Operational Technology (OT) network, which controls our pipeline operations, if it had not already". When they learned of the attack, "we did not know the point of origination of the attack nor the scope of it".

This is the first place the folk story bends. The ransomware was on the IT network. The pipeline was stopped by a person with stop-work authority, on purpose, because nobody yet knew where the attack was. The joint CISA and FBI advisory of 11 May says the same from the outside: the ransomware was deployed "against the pipeline company's information technology (IT) network", and "at this time, there is no indication that the entity's operational technology (OT) networks have been directly affected by the ransomware". Wikipedia's "computerized equipment managing the pipeline" is not what either document describes.

The rest of the timeline is short. The FBI named DarkSide on 10 May, in two sentences. CISA's advisory describes DarkSide as ransomware-as-a-service, in which "the developers of the ransomware receive a share of the proceeds from the cybercriminal actors who deploy it, known as 'affiliates'". No document in this chapter identifies any person behind it, and the chapter does not try to. Blount decided to pay. The Department of Justice puts the payment on 8 May, "approximately 75 bitcoins"; in the Senate, the chairman put the figure at $4.4 million and Blount did not dispute it. The pipeline began returning to service "on Wednesday evening, May 12". On 7 June the Department of Justice announced it had seized 63.7 of the bitcoins, "currently valued at approximately $2.3 million", from an address "for which the FBI has the 'private key'". How it had the key, the release does not say; the affidavit it cites is not public.

Now the account. Blount's oral statement to the Senate: "we believe the attacker exploited the legacy VPN profile that was not intended to be in use". Asked by Senator Portman whether the company had required multi-factor authentication before the attack, he answered: "in the case of this particular legacy VPN, it did only have single-factor authentication. It was a complicated password, so I want to be clear on that. It was not a 'Colonial 123' type password." And then the sentence the folk story never carries: "But in our normal operation, we use an RSA token allowance in order to create authentication difficulties for remote access." Colonial had a second factor. The legacy profile sat outside it.

Senator Hassan summarised his testimony back to him as "you did not have two-step authentication in place" and called the VPN "a pretty old VPN". Blount's answer is the sentence this chapter is built on: "I did reference earlier that the VPN was a legacy VPN. That we could not see and it did not show up in any pen testing, that is unfortunate." Not a VPN they had decided to leave weak. A VPN they could not see.

Carmakal, the next day, was more specific. The earliest evidence "was a login to the Colonial Pipeline VPN. We do know that an employee's credentials were used." Then: "We did not figure out exactly how the attacker was able to get access to the username, but it is a possibility that the attacker was able to leverage credentials that the employee may have used on another website that was compromised prior to this date." He corrected the chairman's word: "Whether or not the vulnerability or the misconfiguration—and let me, you know, clarify it as a misconfiguration—whether it would have been picked up by a vulnerability assessment is hard to tell." And the finding itself: "there was a legacy VPN profile that was in place that wasn't believed to be active, and that enabled an attacker to leverage both the user and the password to login."

Later in the hearing, under a rapid series of questions, he added the last pieces. Why no second factor? "It was not required for the specific VPN profile that was used for this specific account. It is because the account and the VPN profile wasn't believed to actually be enabled." Who had the password? "One person, as far as we know." Where else was it used? "We do not know the exact source of the website that it was used, but presumably it was used on at least one other website because there are passwords that are readily available on the internet, and we did find that it was one of the passwords that was stolen from another website. But we don't know exactly where it came from." The employee is not named in any document here, and is not named in this chapter. Blount's written statement, for its part, says only that "we are still trying to determine how the attackers gained the needed credentials to exploit it". He said Colonial was ready to share the results of its Mandiant-supported review with the Senate committee, and added that a legacy asset of this kind "is not an unusual issue for companies". No public version of those results is in the record gathered for this chapter.

Set the two accounts against the headline. A compromised password: yes. A dark-web batch: Carmakal's testimony is "stolen from another website" and "we don't know exactly where it came from". An inactive account: the record's words are "not intended to be in use", "wasn't believed to be active", "wasn't believed to actually be enabled". A company without multi-factor authentication: a company whose normal remote access used a token, with one profile that did not. The password opened the door. The record's subject is the door.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category in one sentence: When an attacker is able to trick a system into recognizing an invalid or incorrect user as legitimate, this vulnerability is present. Its list of weaknesses includes the one the headlines used, an application that Has missing or ineffective multi-factor authentication. It also includes the one the record describes, which is quieter: an application that Allows use of weak or ineffective fallbacks if multi-factor authentication is not available. Among the CWEs the category maps are CWE-308 Use of Single-factor Authentication and CWE-288 Authentication Bypass Using an Alternate Path or Channel. Colonial's VPN is not a web application, and the OWASP list is built from tested web applications. But the weakness class fits exactly, and it is worth drawing.

Start with what a password proves. It proves that whoever typed it knows a string. It does not prove where they learned it. If the same string was used on another site, and that site's password store leaked, then the string is in a list, and OWASP's first weakness applies: the system Permits automated attacks such as credential stuffing, where the attacker has a breached list of valid usernames and passwords. Carmakal described one password stolen from another website and used against one VPN. A "complicated" password is no defence against reuse of a stolen password because it does not have to be guessed.

A second factor changes what the login proves. A one-time code from a token or an app proves possession of the token at that moment. An attacker holding only a leaked password does not have it. For the login Mandiant found, the profile asked for no code. Carmakal said there was no evidence of phishing of the employee and no earlier attacker activity; the login used a username and a password. The record does not show what the attacker would have done if that profile had demanded another factor.

Now the whiteboard. Draw the perimeter as a table, not a wall. Each row is a way in: a VPN profile, an RDP gateway, an API token path, a service account, a vendor portal. Each row has a column for what that path demands: password only, password plus code, password plus key. The security of the perimeter is not the average of the column. It is the weakest row that an attacker can reach with a valid username. A VPN appliance is exactly such a table; "profile" is the appliance's name for a row. Blount's normal rows demanded an RSA token. One row, the legacy profile, demanded a password.

Here is why this is hard to see. The profile was legacy, was believed not to be enabled, and did not show up in Colonial's penetration tests. The public record does not show why the tests missed it or when the profile was created. Yet it still accepted a login. Belief about a configuration is not a configuration. OWASP's phrase for the result, "weak or ineffective fallbacks", is precise: the strong path existed, and a weaker path was still accepted.

Two more properties of second factors matter for the rest of the chapter, and they are different from each other. First, a one-time code can be relayed. A phishing page that asks for the password and then, at once, the code, and forwards both to the real site, defeats a code as surely as a password. Google's 2016 paper on security keys says it plainly: OTPs "as a second factor are still vulnerable to relatively common attacks such as phishing". A security key does something a code cannot: it signs a challenge with a key that is bound to the site's origin. The WebAuthn standard states the rule: "the public key credential can only be accessed by origins belonging to that Relying Party. This scoping is enforced jointly by conforming User Agents and authenticators." A phishing site at the wrong origin cannot obtain a signature for the right one. The browser and the key refuse together.

Second, a push prompt on a phone can be approved by the right person for the wrong login. The prompt proves possession of the phone. It does not prove that the person holding it is the one typing the password, only that they tapped yes. That is a human decision, and it can be worn down. The record of what that looks like is Uber's, and it belongs to the defense section.

The defense

What was in place, and why it failed, is in the transcripts. Colonial's ordinary remote access required an RSA token. The defence did not fail on the path it covered; it was absent from a path nobody knew was live. Carmakal's remediation confirms the shape of the fix: "The legacy VPN profile has been completely removed. So, a user, whether an attacker or an employee, would not be able to attempt to login to the system without requiring multifactor authentication." Blount's written statement says Colonial "implemented additional layers of protection across our enterprise" and engaged two further outside specialists. That is the whole public record of what Colonial adopted. There is no measurement of it, and the chapter does not invent one.

Carmakal described the narrow change: after the profile was removed, a login to Colonial's VPN required a password and a one-time code. Whether the same attacker would then have found another row in the table, the record cannot say. OWASP's first prevention is the general form: Where possible, implement and enforce use of multi-factor authentication to prevent automated credential stuffing, brute force, and stolen credential reuse attacks. The CISA and FBI advisory's first mitigation, written four days after the ransom note, is the same instruction for this sector: "Require multi-factor authentication for remote access to OT and IT networks." The word "enforce" is doing the work in both. Colonial used a token in normal remote access, but not on the legacy profile.

The best-documented record of a second factor working at scale is Google's, and it needs reading carefully. In July 2018 Krebs on Security reported that Google "has not had any of its 85,000+ employees successfully phished on their work-related accounts since early 2017", when it required physical security keys. The spokesperson's actual words were narrower: "We have had no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys at Google." That is a statement to a journalist, with a qualifier the headline dropped. Google published no paper or blog post behind it. The published measurement is different and later. In 2019 Google, NYU and UCSD researchers evaluated "over 350,000 real-world hijacking attempts" against Google consumer accounts. Their Table 3 gives security keys 100% ± 25% against automated bots and 100% ± 28% against phishing, with 95% confidence intervals, and no figure at all for targeted attacks, because too few key-holders were targeted to measure. Google's blog on the paper says "zero users that exclusively use security keys fell victim to targeted phishing during our investigation", and, for comparison, that on-device prompts prevented "90% of targeted attacks" and an SMS code "76% of targeted attacks".

So the honest reading of Google's number is this. The design case for keys is strong and published: the origin binding above, and the 2016 paper's two-year deployment "which began within Google". The staff result is a vendor statement. The published result is on consumer accounts, with a point estimate of 100% and an interval a quarter as wide, and it does not cover the attack a pipeline company would most fear. The paper's own conclusion is measured: "for at-risk users looking to mitigate the risk of remote hijacking entirely, we emphasize that security keys offer the best immediate solution". The same paper reports the cost side: challenges caused 52% of legitimate users to fail a sign-in, and in Google's experiment "38% of users did not have access to their phone when challenged". A second factor is a friction as well as a barrier, which is one reason legacy profiles without one get created.

Then the limit. In September 2022 Uber published a post-incident statement. An outside contractor's corporate password was, Uber wrote, likely "purchased" on the dark web after malware on a personal device. "The attacker then repeatedly tried to log in to the contractor's Uber account. Each time, the contractor received a two-factor login approval request, which initially blocked access. Eventually, however, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker successfully logged in." The second factor was present, enforced, and held by the right person. It failed because the factor was a question put to a human, repeatedly, until the answer changed. Nothing in the Colonial record suggests this would have happened there, and nothing rules it out. The defence that closes a leaked-password login does not close a worn-down approval; only a factor that cannot be approved for the wrong origin does that.

The regulator's answer came in two steps. GAO's testimony of July 2021 describes what preceded it: "Prior to issuing a cybersecurity directive in May 2021, TSA's efforts included issuing voluntary security guidelines and security reviews of privately owned and operated pipelines", with 15 recommendations from GAO's 2018 and 2019 reports, 12 of them addressed. Blount, asked about the pre-attack TSA review, said it "probably would not have resulted in finding that legacy VPN. Again, they do not actually go into the system. It is a questionnaire format type thing." The Federal Register records the two directives: Pipeline-2021-01 on 26 May 2021, requiring incident reports to CISA within 12 hours, a named coordinator and a self-assessment; Pipeline-2021-02 on 19 July 2021, requiring mitigation measures, a contingency plan and an annual third-party architecture review. The 2021 text of the second directive is Sensitive Security Information and has not been published. What is public is its 2024 renewal, SD Pipeline-2021-02E, which says it "continues to require performance-based regulatory cybersecurity measures first issued by TSA on July 26, 2021". Its access-control section requires "Multi-factor authentication, or other logical and physical security controls that supplement password authentication to provide risk mitigation commensurate to multi-factor authentication". A directive can require a factor, with a compensating-controls clause. It cannot find the profile that was not believed to be enabled. Only an inventory does that.

What you can now see

Tomorrow, on the system you run, do not audit only the password policy. Print the table from the mechanism section. Ask the appliance, identity provider, or gateway for every profile, group, policy or connection path that will accept a login, and write down what each demands. Then test the rows documented as disabled. Colonial's profile did not show up in its penetration tests; the record does not show why. The row you seek was created before the current second factor, is no longer understood, and is believed off without evidence of a refused login. Blount said Colonial "could not see" its profile. The table is how you see yours. A reachable row that demands only a password is the misconfiguration Carmakal described.
