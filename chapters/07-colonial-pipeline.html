Chapter 7 · A07:2025 Authentication Failures

Not Intended to Be in Use

You know the headline: one password, a legacy VPN profile with no second factor, and Colonial's pipeline operations stopped for most of a week. The record adds the detail the headline drops. The profile was believed not to be enabled; it did not show up in Colonial's penetration tests; and on 29 April 2021 it accepted a username and a password and let someone in. The password was the key. The door Colonial believed closed was the flaw.

The break

The public story is the Bloomberg headline of 4 June 2021: "Hackers Breached Colonial Pipeline Using Compromised Password". CNN carried it that day. It linked the password to "a disused virtual private networking account" and said the account lacked multi-factor authentication. It attributed the dark-web batch detail to Bloomberg's Carmakal interview.

Wikipedia compresses it further: "a compromised password for an inactive virtual private network (VPN) account" without MFA, in an attack that "afflicted computerized equipment managing the pipeline". Four days after Bloomberg, the Senate committee opened with the same summary: "Recent news reports indicate that hackers accessed the Colonial system through a compromised password of a virtual private network (VPN) account."

The record that tests this is two transcripts. On 8 June 2021 Joseph Blount, Colonial's chief executive, testified to the Senate committee. On 9 June he and Charles Carmakal of Mandiant, the incident-response firm Colonial hired, testified to the House Homeland Security committee. Both are printed by the Government Publishing Office. The account of initial access below comes from their public congressional testimony and prepared statements.

Carmakal's prepared statement gives the start. "The earliest evidence of compromise that we have identified to date occurred on April 29, 2021. On that date, the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile and an employee's username and password. The legacy VPN profile did not require a one-time passcode to be provided." Mandiant had not worked for Colonial before. It was engaged "on the early morning of May 7, 2021" by the law firm Hunton Andrews Kurth, on Colonial's behalf, "to help respond to the ransomware event that was discovered earlier that day".

Blount's written statement gives the morning. Colonial "identified the ransomware attack just before 5:00 AM Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) on Friday, May 7th, when one of our employees identified the ransom note on a system in the IT network". The employee notified the operations supervisor at the control centre, "who put in the stop work order to halt operations throughout the pipeline". At about 5:55 AM the shutdown began; by 6:10 AM "they confirmed that all 5,500 miles of pipelines had been shut down". The reason, in his words, was "the imperative to isolate and contain the attack to help ensure the malware did not spread to the Operational Technology (OT) network, which controls our pipeline operations, if it had not already". When they learned of the attack, "we did not know the point of origination of the attack nor the scope of it".

This is the first place the folk story bends. The ransomware was on the IT network. A person with stop-work authority stopped the pipeline because Colonial did not yet know the attack's scope. The joint CISA and FBI advisory says ransomware was deployed "against the pipeline company's information technology (IT) network". At that time, it reported no indication that ransomware had directly affected OT. Wikipedia's "computerized equipment managing the pipeline" invites a stronger inference than those documents support.

The FBI named DarkSide on 10 May. CISA describes it as ransomware-as-a-service: developers receive a share from the criminal "affiliates" who deploy it. No document here identifies a person behind it.

Blount decided to pay. DOJ puts the 8 May payment at "approximately 75 bitcoins"; in the Senate, the chairman put it at $4.4 million and Blount described choosing to pay. The pipeline began returning to service "on Wednesday evening, May 12". On 7 June DOJ announced it had seized 63.7 bitcoins, then worth about $2.3 million, from an address for which the FBI had the private key. The release does not say how it had the key; the cited affidavit is not public.

Now the account. Blount told the Senate: "we believe the attacker exploited the legacy VPN profile that was not intended to be in use". Asked about MFA, he said this legacy VPN had only one factor and a complicated password, not "Colonial 123". Normal remote access used an RSA token. Colonial had a second factor. The legacy profile sat outside it.

Senator Hassan summarised his testimony back to him as "you did not have two-step authentication in place" and called the VPN "a pretty old VPN". Blount's answer is the sentence this chapter is built on: "I did reference earlier that the VPN was a legacy VPN. That we could not see and it did not show up in any pen testing, that is unfortunate." This was a profile Colonial said it could not see and Carmakal said was believed inactive.

Carmakal was more specific the next day. The earliest evidence was a Colonial VPN login using employee credentials. Mandiant did not determine how the attacker obtained the username; reuse on a previously compromised website was one possibility. Carmakal corrected the chairman's "vulnerability" to "misconfiguration". A legacy profile believed inactive had accepted the username and password.

Later he added the last pieces. MFA was absent because the account and profile were not believed enabled. One person had legitimate password access, as far as Mandiant knew. Mandiant found the password had been stolen from another website, but did not know exactly where it came from. No document here names the employee.

Blount's statement says Colonial was "still trying to determine how the attackers gained the needed credentials". He said Colonial was ready to share the results of its Mandiant-supported review with the Senate committee. Similar legacy assets, he added, were "not an unusual issue for companies". No public version of those results is in the record gathered here.

Set the two accounts against the headline. A compromised password: yes. A dark-web batch: Carmakal's testimony is "stolen from another website" and "we don't know exactly where it came from". An inactive account: the record's words are "not intended to be in use", "wasn't believed to be active", "wasn't believed to actually be enabled". A company without multi-factor authentication: a company whose normal remote access used a token, with one profile that did not. The password opened the door. The record's subject is the door.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category in one sentence: When an attacker is able to trick a system into recognizing an invalid or incorrect user as legitimate, this vulnerability is present. Its list of weaknesses includes the one the headlines used, an application that Has missing or ineffective multi-factor authentication. It also includes the one the record describes, which is quieter: an application that Allows use of weak or ineffective fallbacks if multi-factor authentication is not available. Among the CWEs the category maps are CWE-308 Use of Single-factor Authentication and CWE-288 Authentication Bypass Using an Alternate Path or Channel. Colonial's VPN is not a web application, and the OWASP list is built from tested web applications. But the weakness class fits exactly, and it is worth drawing.

Start with what a password proves. It proves that whoever typed it knows a string, not where they learned it. If the same string was used on a breached site, it may appear in a list. OWASP names the result: a system Permits automated attacks such as credential stuffing, where the attacker has a breached list of valid usernames and passwords. Carmakal described one password stolen from another website and used against one VPN. A complicated stolen password does not have to be guessed.

A second factor changes what the login proves. A one-time code from a token or an app proves possession of the token at that moment. An attacker holding only a leaked password does not have it. For the login Mandiant found, the profile asked for no code; it accepted a username and a password. The record does not show what the attacker would have done if that profile had demanded another factor.

Now the whiteboard. Draw the perimeter as a table, not a wall. Each row is a way in: a VPN profile, an RDP gateway, an API token path, a service account, a vendor portal. Each row has a column for what that path demands: password only, password plus code, password plus key. The security of the perimeter is not the average of the column. It is the weakest row that an attacker can reach with a valid username. A VPN appliance is exactly such a table; "profile" is the appliance's name for a row. Blount's normal rows demanded an RSA token. One row, the legacy profile, demanded a password.

Here is why this is hard to see. The profile was legacy, was believed not to be enabled, and did not show up in Colonial's penetration tests. The public record does not show why the tests missed it or when the profile was created. Yet it still accepted a login. Belief about a configuration is not a configuration. OWASP's phrase for the result, "weak or ineffective fallbacks", is precise: the strong path existed, and a weaker path was still accepted.

Two properties of second factors matter here. First, a one-time code can be relayed. A phishing page can ask for a password and code, then forward both to the real site. Google's 2016 security-key paper says OTPs "as a second factor are still vulnerable to relatively common attacks such as phishing".

A security key instead signs a challenge with a key bound to the site's origin. WebAuthn says a public-key credential is accessible only to origins belonging to its Relying Party. User agents and authenticators jointly enforce that scoping. A phishing site at the wrong origin cannot obtain a signature for the right one.

Second, a push prompt on a phone can be approved by the right person for the wrong login. The prompt proves possession of the phone. It does not prove that the person holding it is the one typing the password, only that they tapped yes. That is a human decision, and it can be worn down. The record of what that looks like is Uber's, and it belongs to the defense section.

The defense

Colonial's ordinary remote access required an RSA token. The defence was absent from a profile Carmakal said was not believed enabled. Carmakal said the legacy profile was completely removed. Afterward, neither an attacker nor an employee could attempt a VPN login without MFA. Blount said Colonial added protections and engaged two outside specialists. That is the public record of what Colonial adopted. It contains no measurement.

After the profile was removed, Carmakal said, a Colonial VPN login required a password and one-time code. The record cannot say whether the attacker would have found another path. OWASP's first prevention is to implement and enforce use of multi-factor authentication to prevent automated credential stuffing, brute force, and stolen credential reuse attacks. Four days after the ransom note, CISA and FBI likewise said to require MFA for remote access to IT and OT networks. Colonial used a token in normal remote access, but not on the legacy profile.

Google supplies the best-documented record of a second factor at scale, but it needs careful reading. In 2018 Krebs reported that none of Google's 85,000-plus employees had been successfully phished on work accounts since keys became mandatory. The spokesperson's narrower words were: "We have had no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys at Google." That is a qualified statement to a journalist. No supporting Google publication is in this record.

The published measurement came later. In 2019 Google, NYU and UCSD researchers evaluated over 350,000 hijacking attempts against Google accounts. Table 3 reports security keys at 100% ± 25% against bots and 100% ± 28% against phishing, with 95% confidence intervals. It has no targeted-attack figure because too few key-holders were targeted to measure. Google's blog says no exclusive security-key user fell to targeted phishing during the investigation. Device prompts prevented 90% of targeted attacks and SMS codes 76%.

The design case is published: origin binding, and a two-year deployment that "began within Google". The staff result remains a vendor statement; the paper studied Google accounts and cannot measure targeted attacks against key-holders. Its conclusion calls security keys "the best immediate solution" for at-risk users seeking to prevent remote hijacking.

The paper's usability result is narrower than a generic warning that MFA adds friction. In its samples, 88.2% of two-factor users completed sign-in in the same session, compared with 89.5% of password-only users. The authors called the friction similar. The result covers two-factor authentication as a group, not security keys alone.

Then the limit. In September 2022 Uber published a post-incident statement. An outside contractor's corporate password was, Uber wrote, likely "purchased" on the dark web after malware on a personal device. "The attacker then repeatedly tried to log in to the contractor's Uber account. Each time, the contractor received a two-factor login approval request, which initially blocked access. Eventually, however, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker successfully logged in." The second factor was present, enforced, and held by the right person. It failed because the factor was a question put to a human, repeatedly, until the answer changed. Nothing in the Colonial record suggests this would have happened there, and nothing rules it out.

The defence that closes a leaked-password login does not close a worn-down approval. In Google's paper, a security key tests for user presence while signing the server's challenge through the browser making the request. That design offers no out-of-band approval button for an attacker's separate login. Origin binding is a separate protection: it stops a phishing site from requesting a signature for the real site.

The regulator's answer came in two steps. GAO says TSA used voluntary guidelines and reviews before its May 2021 directive. Of 15 recommendations in GAO's 2018 and 2019 reports, 12 had been addressed. Colonial had not yet taken up one offered review. Blount said it probably would not have found the legacy VPN because it was questionnaire-based.

The Federal Register records two directives. Pipeline-2021-01, issued 26 May 2021, required incident reports to CISA within 12 hours, a coordinator and a self-assessment. Pipeline-2021-02, issued 19 July, required mitigation measures, a contingency plan and an annual outside architecture review. The second directive's 2021 text is Sensitive Security Information and unpublished.

Its public 2024 renewal says it continues measures first issued in July 2021. The access-control section requires MFA or other controls providing commensurate risk mitigation. A directive can require a factor, with a compensating-controls clause. It cannot find a profile believed disabled. Only an inventory can.

What you can now see

Tomorrow, on the system you run, do not audit only the password policy. Print the table from the mechanism section. Ask the appliance, identity provider, or gateway for every profile, group, policy or connection path that will accept a login, and write down what each demands. Then test the rows documented as disabled. Colonial's profile did not show up in its penetration tests; the record does not show why. The row you seek was created before the current second factor, is no longer understood, and is believed off without evidence of a refused login. Blount said Colonial "could not see" its profile. The table is how you see yours. A reachable row that demands only a password is the misconfiguration Carmakal described.
