Chapter 1 · A01:2025 Broken Access Control

Three Commands and a Role

On 29 July 2019 the FBI arrested a Seattle software engineer for taking credit-card application data on roughly a hundred million people from Capital One's cloud storage. The headline version has been stable ever since: an ex-Amazon employee, a misconfigured firewall, one of the largest bank breaches on record. The record adds a smaller detail, and a more useful one. The file that gave the whole thing away, posted to GitHub in April, was not the data. It was three commands and a list of more than 700 bucket names. The first command asked a firewall for its own credentials. The other two used them.

The break

The folk story has three tellers. The Department of Justice, on the day of the arrest: "The intrusion occurred through a misconfigured web application firewall that enabled access to the data". Wikipedia: the FBI arrested Paige Thompson, "who had previously worked as a software engineer for Amazon Web Services," and she "had accessed about 140,000 Social Security numbers". And Brian Krebs, four days after the arrest, citing "a source with direct knowledge of the breach investigation": a misconfigured open-source WAF, tricked by "a 'Server Side Request Forgery' (SSRF) attack" into relaying requests to the cloud's metadata service. The service handed over credentials for a role that "was allowed to list all of the files in any buckets of data, and to read the contents of each of those files". Krebs's version is the one engineers repeat. It is also the one no court document contains.

The document closest to the event is the criminal complaint, sworn by FBI Special Agent Joel Martini on 27 July 2019 and filed two days later. It is an affidavit for probable cause. Everything in it is an allegation, and it names the cloud provider only as "the Cloud Computing Company". Its account of the mechanism is one sentence: "A firewall misconfiguration permitted commands to reach and be executed by that server, which enabled access to folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space at the Cloud Computing Company". The words SSRF, metadata, Amazon and AWS do not appear anywhere in its twelve pages.

The complaint's timeline comes from Capital One's own logs, as reported to the agent. On or about 12 March 2019, an address belonging to a Swedish VPN provider "attempted to access Capital One's data". On or about 22 March, an account the complaint calls *****-WAF-Role "was used to execute the List Buckets Command several times," from what the agent believed were Tor exit nodes. The same day it was used "to execute the Sync Command a number of times to obtain data from certain of Capital One's data folders or buckets, including files that contain credit card application data". Then the sentence a defender should underline: "According to Capital One, the *****-WAF-Role account does not, in the ordinary course of business, invoke the List Buckets Command". The anomaly was in the logs from that day.

Nobody at the bank found it there. On 21 April another List Buckets call ran from a VPN address, and a file with that timestamp appeared in a GitHub gist whose address included Thompson's full name. On 17 July at 1:25 a.m. a stranger e-mailed Capital One's responsible-disclosure address: "There appears to be some leaked s3 data of yours in someone's github / gist … Let me know if you want help tracking them down". Capital One's own statement dates its determination that an intrusion had occurred to 19 July, and says: "We immediately fixed the issue and promptly began working with federal law enforcement".

What was in the gist is the most instructive paragraph in the record. Capital One "determined that the April 21 File contained code for three commands, as well as a list of more than 700 folders or buckets of data". The first command, "when executed, obtained security credentials for an account known as *****-WAF-Role". The second, "the List Buckets Command," used that role "to list the names of folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space". The third, "the Sync Command," used the role "to extract or copy data from those folders or buckets in Capital One's storage space for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions". Capital One re-ran the commands and "confirmed that the commands did, in fact, function"; the 700-plus names "matched the actual names" of its buckets, and the 21 April log entry matched the file's timestamp.

The data, per the complaint, was "primarily data related to credit card applications". Some fields, "such as Social Security numbers," had "been tokenized or encrypted"; names, addresses, dates of birth and credit history had not. The agent's figures, as of 27 July, were "approximately 120,000 Social Security Numbers and approximately 77,000 bank account numbers" across "likely tens of millions of applications". Capital One's public statement gave about 140,000 Social Security numbers, about 80,000 linked bank account numbers, roughly a million Canadian Social Insurance Numbers, and "approximately 100 million individuals in the United States and approximately 6 million in Canada". It stressed that "no credit card account numbers or log-in credentials were compromised". In January 2021 it added about 4,700 more Social Security numbers found on later analysis.

The evidence tying Thompson to the gist was mostly her own writing. On 26 June, on a Slack channel she organised, a user "erratic" listed files that included two named for *****-WAF-Role, the directory the Sync Command would create. On 18 June she had written to a stranger on Twitter: "Ive basically strapped myself with a bomb vest, fucking dropping capitol ones dox and admitting it. I wanna distribute those buckets i think first". Her resume, linked from the same GitHub profile, said she had been a systems engineer at the Cloud Computing Company in 2015–16. The Ninth Circuit later put the start of the intrusions at "over two years after her employment at Amazon ended". Agents searched her home on 29 July and seized devices that referenced Capital One.

Alleged, then narrowed, then found. The indictment of 28 August 2019 described "scanning software that allowed her to identify customers of a cloud computing company who had misconfigured their firewalls," and put Capital One among "more than 30 other entities". The same release said investigators had "found no evidence that THOMPSON sold or disseminated any of the information she accessed". In June 2022, after a seven-day trial, a jury found her guilty of wire fraud, five counts of unauthorized access to a protected computer and one of damaging one. It acquitted her of access-device fraud and aggravated identity theft. The Ninth Circuit's summary of the trial record: she "scanned millions of publicly available IP addresses associated with AWS" and got "credentials from at least 200 entities and stole data from at least 30 of them". She "did not sell or distribute any stolen information".

In October 2022 Judge Robert Lasnik sentenced her to time served and five years' probation; the government had asked for seven years. Restitution was later set at over $40 million. In March 2025 the Ninth Circuit vacated the sentence as "substantively unreasonable" against a Guidelines range of 168 to 210 months, and in November 2025, according to CyberScoop, the district court reimposed it with fuller reasons.

The bank's side of the record is the regulators'. On 6 August 2020 the OCC assessed an $80 million civil money penalty. The consent order's findings, which "the Bank neither admits nor denies," do not mention a firewall. They say that "in or around 2015, the Bank failed to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to migrating its information technology operations to the cloud operating environment". They say it failed at "appropriate design and implementation of certain network security controls, adequate data loss prevention controls, and effective dispositioning of alerts". Internal audit "failed to identify numerous control weaknesses and gaps". The Board "failed to take effective actions to hold management accountable". The Federal Reserve issued a cease-and-desist against the holding company the same day, citing "a significant data breach from March 2019". A class settlement followed, put at $190 million by the U.S. Attorney's office.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category as Access control enforces policy such that users cannot act outside of their intended permissions. Failures typically lead to unauthorized information disclosure, modification or destruction of all data, or performing a business function outside the user's limits. The first item on its list of common failures is Violation of the principle of least privilege, commonly known as deny by default, where access should only be granted for particular capabilities, roles, or users, but is available to anyone. Note the last three words. The *****-WAF-Role was not available to anyone on the internet. It was available to anyone who could make one particular server ask for it.

That is the shape of a server-side request forgery. A web application that will fetch a URL on your behalf, or a proxy that will forward a request to whatever host you name, is a machine that makes requests from inside the network. If you can choose the destination, the machine's network position becomes yours. In the 2021 list SSRF had its own category. The 2025 Introduction notes that Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) has been rolled into this category, and the A01 page lists CWE-918 Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) among its notable weaknesses. The reason is the one this case shows: the forged request is only a route. The harm at the end of it is always access the requester was never granted.

In a cloud the route has an obvious end. Amazon's own description of the EC2 instance metadata service: it "runs on a special 'link local' IP address of 169.254.169.254 that means only software running on the instance can access it". It also "makes the AWS credentials available for any IAM role that is attached to the instance". Those credentials are what let a machine call storage without a password on disk. The security of the arrangement rests on one assumption: that a request reaching the metadata address really came from a program on the instance. An open proxy on the instance breaks that assumption without breaking anything else.

The whiteboard version has four boxes. An attacker on the internet; a firewall or proxy instance that forwards requests; the metadata service, reachable only from that instance; and the storage buckets. The vulnerable pattern, in the shape it takes in a thousand codebases, is this:

# any of: a "fetch this URL" feature, a Host-header-driven proxy rule,
# a reverse proxy with no allow-list of upstream hosts
target = request.param("url")
return http.get(target)          # the server's identity, the caller's destination

Point target at the metadata address and the response is the instance role's temporary key, secret and session token. Nothing has been injected and nothing has crashed. The proxy did its job. What comes back is a credential, and a credential is portable: the complaint records the List Buckets and Sync commands running from Tor exit nodes and a Swedish VPN, not from anywhere inside Capital One. From that moment the story is ordinary AWS API calls by a legitimate principal.

That is why this is access control and not injection. The CWE list under A01 includes CWE-441 Unintended Proxy or Intermediary ('Confused Deputy'), which is the older name for the same thing: a trusted component made to use its authority on behalf of someone who has none. The fix is not in the request parser. It is in what the deputy is allowed to do. Every call the role made was authorised; the complaint's only word for the March calls is "unusual," and its only tell is that the role "does not, in the ordinary course of business" list buckets. A permitted call by a permitted identity is the hardest thing in a log to see, because nothing failed.

One honesty about the whiteboard. It is consistent with every document and asserted by none of them. The complaint says a firewall misconfiguration let commands reach the server. Amazon told Krebs the intrusion "was caused by a misconfiguration of a web application firewall and not the underlying infrastructure". The SSRF-to-metadata chain comes from Krebs's source and from the shape of what Amazon built next. The trial exhibits would settle it. Until someone publishes them, the mechanism section of this chapter is the most probable reading of the record, and the ledger says so.

The defense

Four months after the arrest, on 19 November 2019, Amazon published a change to the metadata service and did not mention Capital One. The post's title lists the threats it answers: "open firewalls, reverse proxies, and SSRF vulnerabilities". It calls IMDSv1 "fully secure" and describes IMDSv2 as "new 'belt and suspenders' protections for four types of vulnerabilities that could be used to try to access the IMDS". They go, it says, "beyond other types of mitigations, while working seamlessly with existing mitigations such as restricting IAM roles". The four types are a list of the ways a machine can be made to ask on someone else's behalf.

The change is small. A caller must first open a session with an HTTP PUT and receive a token, then send that token as a header on every read. Amazon's own recipe:

TOKEN=`curl -X PUT "http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token" \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 21600"`
curl http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/profile \
  -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN"

Each layer maps onto one of the four boxes. Against an open WAF: "our analysis of third-party WAF products and open WAF misconfigurations found that the vast majority do not permit HTTP PUT requests". Against an open reverse proxy: IMDSv2 "will also not issue session tokens to any caller with an X-Forwarded-For header," the header proxies add. Against an SSRF that can set arbitrary headers: requiring a PUT and then a secret token "is always strictly more effective than requiring only a static header". And against an instance misconfigured as a router or NAT, the token response is sent with an IP TTL of 1, so it "won't be able to travel outside the instance". A proxy that forwards GETs, the most common misconfiguration, now forwards a request the service refuses.

Here the defense record is honest and thin. Both versions stayed "enabled by default," with v1 kept "for customers who have tools and scripts using v1". Amazon shipped a CloudWatch metric counting v1 calls per instance and an IAM condition key, ec2:RoleDelivery, so that a bucket policy can refuse credentials that came through v1. What Amazon has not published, in that post or since as far as this chapter could find, is a number: how many accounts turned v1 off, or how many SSRF attempts IMDSv2 has refused. The design and the rationale are on the record. The outcome is not. A fleet-wide v2-only share, or an incident count, would fill the gap.

The other defense is older, cheaper and less interesting, which is why it is the one that would have worked. OWASP's first prevention line is Except for public resources, deny by default. A web application firewall has a job: inspect traffic and pass or drop it. Nothing in that job requires listing every bucket in an account or reading credit-card applications. The complaint's own qualifier, buckets "for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions," is the whole defense in one clause. IMDSv2 protects the credential; least privilege caps what the credential is worth. With a role scoped to the firewall's own logs and configuration, the three commands in the gist would have returned a list and nothing worth posting.

Why that did not happen is the part of the record the folk story skips, and it is the regulator's, not the FBI's. The OCC did not find a bad firewall rule. It found a bank that in 2015 "failed to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to migrating" to the cloud, and afterward failed at "effective dispositioning of alerts". Read that against the complaint's line that the role's March calls were outside the ordinary course of business, and the four-month gap before a stranger's e-mail, and the picture is not one mistake. It is a role nobody scoped, an anomaly nobody triaged, and an audit that, per the order, did not report the gaps it should have found. OWASP's list also says Log access control failures, alert admins when appropriate; the hard case, shown here, is that nothing failed.

Whether Capital One's fixes worked is not on the public record. The OCC order says the bank "has begun addressing the identified corrective action"; the Federal Reserve required an enhanced program. Neither published what was changed, and the bank's statement says only that the configuration was fixed. The measurable defense in this chapter is Amazon's, and its measure is missing. The provable one is the boring one, and it is provable from the complaint alone.

What you can now see

Take one internet-facing machine you run: a proxy, a WAF, a bastion, anything that forwards requests. Find the identity attached to it: the instance role, the service account, the workload identity. Then ask one question of that identity, not of the machine: what can it list?

Not what it reads in practice, what it is allowed to enumerate. A list call is the first thing an attacker with a stolen credential runs, because it turns one machine's key into a map of the account. The complaint's answer for one firewall's role was more than 700 buckets, and the role had never needed that call in the ordinary course of business. The gap between "never uses" and "is allowed to" is the whole of A01, and it is visible in an afternoon with the permissions console open and no attacker anywhere.

If the answer is "everything," you have found the bug before the SSRF that would reach it. Fix the role first. Then turn off the version of the metadata service that answers a plain GET, and watch the metric until nothing still needs it.
