Chapter 9 · A09:2025 Security Logging & Alerting Failures

Between alert and action

The durable Target story is that an alert sounded and nobody looked. John Mulligan, speaking for Target in prepared Senate testimony, gave a narrower account. Target then believed some unspecified intruder activity was detected, logged, surfaced to the security operations center, and evaluated. He did not identify that activity as the reported FireEye alerts. The useful question is what happened between a machine raising an event and a person causing an effective response. The public record does not fully answer it. Its silence is part of the lesson.

The break

The sequence begins before any alert. Mulligan's prepared statement says intruders appeared to enter Target's system on November 12, 2013. It does not make the entry route the whole story. The statement was testimony, not a forensic report, and it carefully described what Target then believed.

A famous headline supplied the compact alternative: KrebsOnSecurity called its account “Target Hackers Broke in Via HVAC Company.” The Senate Commerce Committee's majority staff report likewise said the attackers reportedly first gained access with credentials stolen from Fazio Mechanical Services. The report described that company as an HVAC and refrigeration contractor with remote access for business functions. That account explains a door. It does not explain why later layers failed to end the intrusion.

Target's own description shows the scale of the judging problem. Mulligan said its systems logged more than one billion technology activities on an average day. Tools narrowed those activities to a few hundred events for the security operations center. Reviews of those events produced dozens of cases for further assessment each day. Logging was abundant, filtering was already happening, and people were already reviewing selected events.

Then comes the sentence that changes the question. “We now believe that some intruder activity was detected,” Mulligan testified. He continued that it was logged, surfaced to the SOC, and evaluated by security professionals. He did not identify the activity as the FireEye alerts reported elsewhere. With hindsight and new information, he said, Target was asking about the judgments made then and whether different judgments might have produced different outcomes.

This is not Target claiming that everything worked. In its retrospective account, some unspecified activity crossed three boundaries: sensor to record, record to surfaced item, and surfaced item to human evaluation. The statement does not identify the activity, evaluator, available context, disposition, or attached action. It cannot show whether this event was one of the reported FireEye alerts. Those omissions prevent a confident diagnosis.

The competing public account is stronger. The Senate staff report says FireEye generated urgent alerts as data-exfiltration malware was installed. It says Target's team neither reacted nor enabled automatic deletion. On another page, it describes an alert with the generic label “malware.binary” and raises the possibility that frequent alarms made it look like a false positive. But the report attributes those details to Bloomberg Businessweek.

That attribution matters because the report announces its own limits. It says its explanation is based on published media reports and expert analyses. It adds that the complete story might not be known until Target finished its forensic examination. The staff report is a useful government synthesis, not an independent finding that resolves every disputed step. Its crucial alert narrative rests on a publication that could not be fetched for this chapter.

The same report says Symantec antivirus detected malicious behavior around November 28 and implicated the server FireEye had flagged. It later says the FireEye system reportedly decoded the destination servers where stolen payment-card data sat for days. These are claims in the staff report's reconstruction from outside material. It names no source for the Symantec sentence and only says “reportedly” for the destination-server claim.

CBS broadened the Businessweek account: the attack “could have been prevented” if Target had reacted to its system's warnings. The Senate report makes a narrower counterfactual. Target “could have potentially blocked” the malware's effect through automatic deletion or follow-up. Mulligan narrows it again: different judgments may have led to different outcomes. The escalation is from a possible changed outcome or blocked malware effect to possible prevention of the attack as a whole.

There is one firm endpoint. Mulligan said the Justice Department notified Target on the evening of December 12 about suspicious activity involving cards used at its stores. He said Target immediately opened an internal investigation. That external notification arrived after the internally generated activity had been detected and evaluated, according to the same testimony.

Nor was this a company with no named controls. Mulligan listed firewalls, malware detection, intrusion detection and prevention, and data-loss-prevention tools. He also said Target's systems had been certified compliant with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standards in September 2013. His point was not that certification disproved the breach. It was that the layers and assessment existed, yet the systems were breached.

The honest folk-story verdict therefore splits. The Senate reconstruction says the system warned: that HOLDS within its published account. Treating the HVAC entry as the breach's whole explanation is MISSING what followed. Preventability of the attack is OVERSTATED from narrower counterfactuals. Whether Target received and dispositioned the particular FireEye alerts remains OPEN. Mulligan's unspecified evaluated activity and the secondhand non-reaction claim can both be true.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the dependency in one sentence: Without logging and monitoring, attacks and breaches cannot be detected, and without alerting it is very difficult to respond quickly and effectively during a security incident. The category name matters. It now says logging and alerting. A durable record is evidence; an alert is a request for attention. Neither is yet a response.

Draw the mechanism as six boxes: activity → event → detection → alert → decision → action. Under the boxes, draw context and a clock. An application first emits an event with enough identity, time, resource, and result to distinguish expected behavior from abuse. A detector combines that event with rules or other events. An alert routes the finding to someone or something. A decision gives it a disposition. An action contains, blocks, escalates, or investigates it. Verification checks that the action happened and worked.

Each arrow is a separate failure boundary. Activity can leave no event. An event can omit the user or affected asset. A useful detection can drown among low-value detections. An alert can reach the wrong queue. An analyst can reasonably classify an ambiguous item with too little context. A correct decision can invoke a stale playbook. An assigned containment step can fail without feeding its result back. Buying a better sensor repairs only some of those arrows.

OWASP names several of these conditions. It includes cases where Appropriate alerting thresholds and response escalation processes are not in place or effective. It also includes a detected alert that cannot be processed because its playbook is incomplete, obsolete, or absent. That is a broader and more useful model than “logs missing.” It treats the operational route to action as part of the control.

Mulligan's account places facts beside one another without connecting them. Target then believed some unspecified intruder activity became a logged event, was surfaced, and was evaluated. The account later reaches external notification and investigation. Put a question mark between that evaluation and effective action. Do not use it to confirm or clear the FireEye non-reaction claim. The fetched sources never identify the two events as the same.

Now add two feedback arrows. From decision, send the reason back to detection engineering: true positive, false positive, duplicate, or insufficient evidence. From action, return proof: host isolated, credential disabled, process stopped, owner reached. Without the first loop, rules do not learn from judgments. Without the second, a ticket can close while exposure continues. The control is not complete merely because the interface records a click.

This example sits awkwardly but usefully under an application-security list. The Senate account spans an enterprise network and payment environment, not simply one web application's log statement. OWASP itself describes applications, APIs, log management, operations teams, and incident response in the same category. The lens fits because the mechanism is the same: application and infrastructure signals must cross an owned, testable path to response.

The hard engineering problem is therefore a contract between components and people. For one high-consequence detection, you should be able to state what evidence it emits, who owns it, what context travels with it, how quickly it must be judged, what action follows each disposition, and where success is recorded. If one answer is “the SOC will know,” the whiteboard has exposed a missing interface.

The defense

Target later published a short list of security and technology changes. Under “Enhancing monitoring and logging,” it said it had implemented additional rules and alerts, centralized log feeds, and enabled more logging. The same update named application whitelisting on point-of-sale systems, stronger segmentation, streamlined firewall rules, and reduced vendor access. These are recorded changes, not a recorded result. The fetched Target material does not measure whether its new decision path stopped a later incident.

That thin outcome record matters. More rules and more logs could improve coverage, but they could also increase the judging load. Central feeds can preserve and correlate evidence, but they do not choose an owner or make a playbook work. OWASP's defense reaches the missing interface: DevSecOps and security teams should establish effective monitoring and alerting use cases including playbooks such that suspicious activities are detected and responded to quickly by the Security Operations Center (SOC) team.

Equifax supplies a physical mirror for the same idea. A House Oversight majority staff report says the certificate on a device inspecting traffic to its ACIS environment expired on January 31, 2016. The device's default allowed encrypted web traffic to continue, but the inspection systems could not analyze it. The monitoring product remained in the path while its view disappeared.

The report says Equifax lacked that visibility for nineteen months. When the certificate was updated on July 29, 2017, a countermeasures team detected a suspicious request almost immediately. Employees could analyze the current packet, but the report says they could not determine what the actor had done before that date from this missing traffic view. This was not an alert waiting for judgment. It was a dependency silently preventing the sensor from seeing.

The popular sentence that the certificate had expired for nine months is WRONG against the saved reports. The House report dates expiry to January 31, 2016 and intrusion onset to May 13, 2017, more than fifteen months later. It labels the interval from expiry to discovery as nineteen months. GAO relays Equifax officials' conflicting claim that expiry occurred about ten months before the breach. The origin of Wikipedia's nine-month figure remains unknown.

The House report widens the view beyond the breach-relevant device. It records at least 324 expired SSL certificates, including 79 on devices monitoring highly business-critical domains. That evidence supports an inventory and ownership lesson without making Wikipedia's singular sentence claim that this was the only monitoring problem.

Logs still mattered after discovery. GAO says Equifax officials reported that undamaged electronic logs helped reconstruct attacker commands and determine which data was compromised. Those same officials described new vulnerability-management processes and continuous network monitoring. GAO immediately limits the claim: it did not independently assess those efforts. The record supports adoption and forensic value, not a demonstrated prevention result.

The later FTC documents preserve another important distinction. Its complaint alleges failures, including a scan that was not correctly configured and did not identify the vulnerable portal. The stipulated order is different: it requires an asset inventory, protections across legacy assets, confirmation that patch directives are completed, and periodic testing of safeguards. An allegation is not a finding, and an ordered program is not proof that a particular control succeeded.

For measured outcomes, the best saved record is broader than either company. Mandiant's 2024 infographic reports a global median dwell time of 416 days in 2011 and 10 days in 2023. Its scope is Mandiant Consulting investigations, not all breaches. “Dwell time” ends at detection, so it measures one interval on the whiteboard. It does not measure whether containment followed or succeeded.

Even that interval mixes different discovery paths. In the 2023 investigations, 46% of organizations first found evidence internally and 54% first learned from an external source. For ransomware cases, 70% learned externally; among those external notifications, 76% came from the adversary. A ransom note can end measured dwell time while demonstrating that prevention and early containment failed.

The series also moves with the cases Mandiant handles. Its 2026 executive report says median dwell time rose from 11 days in 2024 to 14 in 2025. It attributes the increase largely to long-running espionage and North Korean IT-worker operations. The same report says internal discovery accounted for 52% of its 2025 investigations, while outside entities accounted for 34% and adversaries 14%. Faster detection is not one stable score of defender quality.

Mandiant does document practices, including red-team measurement of detection and response time, tabletop exercises, centralized long-term log storage, and alerts when logging unexpectedly stops. It does not run a controlled experiment showing that one practice caused the long decline. The defense record is strongest when read modestly: test the complete path, retain evidence beyond the compromised host, and separate internal detection from outside or adversary notification.

What you can now see

Choose one detection whose failure would matter. Follow a test event all the way through your real system. Do not stop when it appears in a dashboard. Watch which context reaches the alert, which queue receives it, who owns the judgment, which playbook branch opens, and whether the requested action returns evidence of success.

Then inspect the silence around that path. What tells you the sensor has lost visibility? What tells you forwarding has stopped, a queue is unstaffed, or an action failed? The object to test is not an alert rule. It is the closed loop from suspicious activity to verified response. If the test ends with “someone would notice,” you have found the same gap this chapter leaves open.
