Chapter 6 · A06:2025 Insecure Design

What “Turn Off GPS” Left Out

In January 2018 a student of international security noticed that a public fitness map had drawn the outlines of military bases in Syria and Afghanistan, and at the Royal Navy's Clyde base. The Guardian said Strava had given away their locations. Ars Technica later said troops should turn off tracker GPS in warzones. Strava's engineering account says the map used only public activities and respected its privacy settings. The Pentagon policy added a precise scope, two authorization paths, and risk-based rules elsewhere. The revealing question was what an aggregate would show where almost nobody exercised.

The break

On 1 November 2017, Strava Engineering published an account of what it called the largest and richest dataset of its kind: an updated Global Heatmap visualizing two years of trailing exercise data from its worldwide user base. Writing on the company engineering blog, Drew Robb described the technical scale of the accomplishment. The dataset comprised 700 million activities, 1.4 trillion latitude and longitude points, 7.7 trillion rasterized pixels, and 5 terabytes of raw input data. The total recorded distance spanned 16 billion kilometers, capturing 100,000 years of recorded activity. Strava presented the heatmap as a resource for athletes to discover places to exercise.

Robb documented cleanup filters in the data pipeline. Most importantly, he wrote that the heatmap contained only public activities and respected all privacy settings. Running activities at implausible speeds were excluded from the running layer. A higher ceiling for cycling discarded records from cars and airplanes. Nothing in the collected record describes a processing defect. By the post's account, every included activity was classed as public under Strava's settings.

The retelling that stuck is Wikipedia's: in January 2018 "an Australian National University student studying international security discovered that this map had mapped military bases, including known U.S. bases in Syria, and forward operating bases in Afghanistan, and HMNB Clyde". The mechanism behind the discovery is in the USENIX paper that studied the incident. "As a result of the sparsity of background noise in some regions", it says, the heatmap "was observed to leak sensitive and classified information regarding the locations of military bases, covert black sites and patrol routes". Strava's chief executive said the same from the inside: members "may have shared their location in areas without other activity density and, in doing so, inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations".

On 28 January 2018 The Guardian ran the story as "Fitness tracking app Strava gives away location of secret US army bases". The absolution came later and was just as clean. The textbook Data Science in Context, by Alfred Spector, Peter Norvig, Chris Wiggins and Jeannette Wing, puts it in one sentence: "Strava did nothing wrong; they displayed anonymized tracks for opted-in users who hadn't realized this compromised military security."

That absolution is too simple in one word. Strava's own documents describe an opt-out, not an opt-in. In an open letter on 29 January 2018, CEO James Quarles wrote that the company respected privacy selections, including an existing ability to opt out of heatmaps altogether. He said military members and humanitarian workers may have shared locations in areas without other activity density and, in doing so, "inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations". Quarles wrote that Strava understood its responsibility for shared data. He pledged to review motivating features so people with bad intent could not abuse them.

The regulatory and political reaction pushed deeper into the architecture. On 14 February 2018, United States Senators Chris Coons and Jeff Flake sent a formal inquiry to Quarles pressing the company on consumer confusion. The senators wrote that while the heatmap contained aggregated and anonymized location data, the information could be easily cross-referenced with other publicly available data. They argued that many consumers were confused by Strava's opt-out provisions or unaware of what information was tracked, noting that privacy protections must be clear and understandable to be meaningful.

Strava moved to alter its platform rules. In a later post, "Heatmap Updates", preserved in a July 2018 archive capture, the company announced changes to the Global Heatmap. Strava shifted the visualization to a monthly refresh schedule, ensuring that when an athlete opted out, their historical contributions would be removed at the next monthly cycle. To protect low-density regions, Strava implemented a new threshold rule: roads and trails with very little activity would not show any heat until several different athletes uploaded activities in that area. Finally, the company restricted detailed street-level zoom functionality on the public map to registered, authenticated users.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category as Insecure design is a broad category representing different weaknesses, expressed as “missing or ineffective control design.”

The framework separates conceptual design errors from everyday programming bugs. It explains: We differentiate between design flaws and implementation defects for a reason, they have different root causes, take place at different times in the development process, and have different remediations. A secure design can still have implementation defects leading to vulnerabilities that may be exploited. An insecure design cannot be fixed by a perfect implementation as needed security controls were never created to defend against specific attacks.

The page also names one contributing factor. One of the factors that contributes to insecure design is the lack of business risk profiling inherent in the software or system being developed, and thus the failure to determine what level of security design is required. Robb's post discusses filters for mislabelled activities, cars, and planes. It also says the chosen normalization "looks really nice". The saved engineering account does not describe a threat model for a reader examining sparse traces.

No implementation defect is needed to explain the exposure described in the collected record. Robb's account instead points to two design decisions: rasterization geometry and color normalization. Together they made sparse activity legible without any documented failure of the processing pipeline.

Threat modeling changes the unit of analysis. A code review can ask whether the tile generator honors each privacy setting. A design review asks what a permitted output reveals when its audience, geography, or scale changes. Start by assuming that every input is authorized and every branch behaves correctly. Then name the reader, the sensitive asset, and the observation available to that reader. Compare a dense city with an isolated installation. Ask when a cohort becomes a person, when a route becomes a routine, and when historical detail becomes operational knowledge. Those questions do not search for a broken conditional. They test whether correct output becomes dangerous in a plausible context.

Strava's old heatmap rasterized each GPS point as one pixel. Activities recorded at most one point per second, so their speed and recording rate could leave multi-pixel gaps. The replacement rendered an additional zoom level, raising the highest resolution from four metres to two. It then drew each activity as a continuous path between successive GPS points. The design made traces legible as paths rather than isolated dots. Sparse background activity gave those paths less visual cover.

The second decision amplified that geometry through statistical contrast. Mapping raw activity counts from an unbounded range onto a fixed display colormap presents a fundamental visualization challenge. If a platform applies a linear scale, dense areas can saturate the palette while quiet trails remain invisible. Strava instead used histogram equalization and the cumulative distribution function of the raw heat values. A pixel's normalized value represented the percentage of pixels with lower heat, producing a uniform distribution over the colormap.

Robb states the cost himself: the map "is not absolutely quantitative. The same color only locally represents the same level of heat data". Dense regions contain many competing traces; sparse regions provide far less cover. The USENIX paper's phrase for the condition is "the sparsity of background noise in some regions".

The platform exposed two different kinds of information by different paths: who and where. The USENIX paper says "the identities of specific athletes were not directly leaked in the heatmap". The map was aggregate tiles without user identifiers. The paper then demonstrated an adjacent method: "a related vulnerability allows an attacker to upload spoofed GPS data in order to discover the IDs of Athletes in a given area". The heatmap exposed locations directly; this study's identity path required another interaction with Strava.

Ordinary static and dynamic defect scanning would not identify the documented assumption. Static analysis looks for patterns such as unsafe memory use, unescaped queries, and missing authorization checks. Dynamic scanning probes running endpoints with unexpected inputs. Here the consequential behavior appears at a different level: public records are aggregated and rendered across regions with radically different activity density. Testing that assumption requires a misuse case, not another malformed request.

The defense

In August 2018 Ars Technica summarized the government's action with the headline "Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones". The primary record supports the core instruction but adds boundaries that the headline cannot carry. It distinguishes operational areas from other locations and provides separate authorization paths for government and non-government devices.

On 3 August 2018 the Deputy Secretary of Defense issued a two-page memorandum, "Use of Geolocation-Capable Devices, Applications, and Services". The policy established an immediate prohibition, but it drew precise boundaries. It prohibited DoD personnel from using "geolocation features and functionality on both non-government and government-issued devices, applications, and services while in locations designated as operational areas." The next clauses define who may grant exceptions and on what evidence.

Crucially, the memorandum invoked an existing threat-modeling process for one operational exception. It allowed Combatant Commanders or their designees to authorize non-government geolocation capabilities in operational areas after conducting a "threat-based comprehensive Operations Security (OPSEC) survey" under DoD Directive 5205.02E. For government-issued devices, commanders could grant authorization based on mission necessity after weighing potential OPSEC risks.

For every other location, the memorandum rejected a single rule. Component heads "will consider the inherent risks" and "Should apply a tiered structure for categorizing location and operations sensitivity while incorporating risk factors". This is risk classification, not OWASP's separate advice to segregate application tiers. The direct connection is threat modeling: test assumptions about a key flow against a defined adversary and operating condition.

Defense planners had described the problem before the map existed. In July 2017, the Government Accountability Office published report GAO-17-668 on Internet of Things security in the military. GAO reported that DoD's 2016 IoT policy recommendations had warned that aggregated device data could inform adversaries about capabilities, deployments, or work patterns. Yet GAO found that the comprehensive OPSEC surveys required by DoD Directive 5205.02E were not being conducted for IoT risks. DoD concurred with GAO's recommendation to conduct those surveys or address the risks through other assessments.

That sequence separates a control's design from its operation. DoD already had a survey process capable of examining adversaries, collection methods, and critical information. GAO nevertheless found that the surveys relevant to IoT risk were not being conducted. The August memorandum then made such a survey a condition for authorizing non-government geolocation capabilities in operational areas. A threat model written into policy is a decision gate. Its value depends on someone running it before granting the exception, then checking whether the resulting restriction changes exposure.

Alongside the military policy, Strava made architectural changes on the server side. The company established a multi-athlete threshold for sparse roads and trails, instituted monthly privacy update cycles, and restricted street-level zoom to registered athletes. A separate academic study examined user-specified Privacy Zones. It reports that Strava adopted randomized spatial cloaking after disclosure; the countermeasure makes endpoint inference significantly harder and may dissuade attackers.

One line in the updates post shows how narrow a per-user control is against an aggregate. Privacy Zones hide an athlete's own activities within the zone, but "Other athletes recording on Strava within an athlete's Privacy Zone may create “heat” on the map through that zone". A control that protects one person's rows does not protect the place. That is why the threshold, not the opt-out, is the design change.

The defense record collected for this chapter has an evidentiary gap. It documents DoD's policy and Strava's platform changes, but contains no follow-up measurement of whether sensitive sites stopped appearing or whether personnel followed the geolocation directive as of 3 September 2026. Publishing a policy is not the same as measuring its effect.

What you can now see

Find one feature in a system you run that publishes an aggregate: a heatmap, a leaderboard, a usage summary, a "popular near you". Assume the code is correct and every row in it was shared on purpose. Then ask what a hostile reader learns from the places where the aggregate is nearly empty, because that is where a handful of ordinary actions stops being a crowd and becomes an outline.

Strava's record supplies the test: the map used public activities and maximized relative contrast, while sparse background activity exposed sensitive locations. If your aggregate has no minimum below which it shows nothing, you have that design. Strava published a threshold requiring several athletes before any heat appeared. DoD made an OPSEC survey a condition for one kind of exception. Neither is a scanner finding. Both required someone to ask what the empty map would reveal.
