Chapter 6 · A06:2025 Insecure Design

The Pentagon Did Not Ban the Watch

In January 2018 a student of international security noticed that a public fitness map had drawn the outlines of military bases, in Syria, in Afghanistan, and at the Royal Navy's Clyde base. The app had no bug. Its engineering post, published two months earlier, says the map contains only public activities and respects every privacy setting. The folk story says Strava gave away secret bases and the Pentagon banned fitness trackers. The record says the map did what it was built to do, and the Pentagon's memorandum banned a feature, not a device, with an exception that required a threat model first. The flaw was a design that never asked what an empty map would show.

The break

On 1 November 2017, Strava Engineering published an account of what it called the largest and richest dataset of its kind: an updated Global Heatmap visualizing two years of trailing exercise data from its worldwide user base. Writing on the company engineering blog, Drew Robb described the technical scale of the accomplishment. The dataset comprised 700 million activities, 1.4 trillion latitude and longitude points, 7.7 trillion rasterized pixels, and 5 terabytes of raw input data. The total recorded distance spanned 16 billion kilometers, capturing more than 100,000 years of human physical exertion. The pipeline was designed to show athletes where their peers were running and cycling, turning individual GPS tracks into a collective map of human recreation across the planet.

From an engineering perspective, the data pipeline had been constructed with careful hygiene. Robb documented that raw activity streams were drawn from a warehouse and processed through automated cleanup filters. Most importantly, Robb noted, the heatmap only contained public activities and respected all user privacy settings. Speeds were filtered to remove anomalies: running activities at implausible speeds were excluded from the running heat layer, and cycling tracks were subjected to a ceiling to discard records recorded by cars and airplanes. Nothing in the record describes a defect. By the post's own account, every point on the map came from an activity its owner had left public.

The map ran for nearly three months before anyone said in public what it showed. The retelling that stuck is Wikipedia's: in January 2018 "an Australian National University student studying international security discovered that this map had mapped military bases, including known U.S. bases in Syria, and forward operating bases in Afghanistan, and HMNB Clyde". The mechanism behind the discovery is in the USENIX paper that studied the incident. "As a result of the sparsity of background noise in some regions", it says, the heatmap "was observed to leak sensitive and classified information regarding the locations of military bases, covert black sites and patrol routes". Strava's chief executive said the same from the inside: members "may have shared their location in areas without other activity density and, in doing so, inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations".

The headlines were immediate. On 28 January 2018 The Guardian ran the story as "Fitness tracking app Strava gives away location of secret US army bases". The absolution came later and was just as clean. The textbook Data Science in Context, by Alfred Spector, Peter Norvig, Chris Wiggins and Jeannette Wing, puts it in one sentence: "Strava did nothing wrong; they displayed anonymized tracks for opted-in users who hadn't realized this compromised military security."

That absolution is too simple in one word. Strava's own documents describe an opt-out, not an opt-in. In an open letter to the community on 29 January 2018, Strava CEO James Quarles acknowledged the gravity of the exposure. Quarles wrote that while the company respected privacy selections, including an existing ability to opt out of heatmaps altogether, Strava learned that members in the military and humanitarian workers living abroad had shared locations in areas without other activity density. In doing so, Quarles admitted, they had "inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations". He did not claim the platform bore no responsibility. He pledged to review motivating features to ensure they could not be abused by people with bad intent.

The regulatory and political reaction pushed deeper into the architecture. On 14 February 2018, United States Senators Chris Coons and Jeff Flake sent a formal inquiry to Quarles pressing the company on consumer confusion. The senators wrote that while the heatmap contained aggregated and anonymized location data, the information could be easily cross-referenced with other publicly available data. They argued that many consumers were confused by Strava's opt-out provisions or unaware of what information was tracked, noting that privacy protections must be clear and understandable to be meaningful.

Strava moved to alter its platform rules. In a later post, "Heatmap Updates", archived in July 2018, the company announced changes to the Global Heatmap. Strava shifted the visualization to a monthly refresh schedule, ensuring that when an athlete opted out, their historical contributions would be removed at the next monthly cycle. To protect low-density regions, Strava implemented a new threshold rule: roads and trails with very little activity would not show any heat until several different athletes uploaded activities in that area. Finally, the company restricted detailed street-level zoom functionality on the public map to registered, authenticated users.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category as Insecure design is a broad category representing different weaknesses, expressed as “missing or ineffective control design.”

The framework separates conceptual design errors from everyday programming bugs. It explains: We differentiate between design flaws and implementation defects for a reason, they have different root causes, take place at different times in the development process, and have different remediations. A secure design can still have implementation defects leading to vulnerabilities that may be exploited. An insecure design cannot be fixed by a perfect implementation as needed security controls were never created to defend against specific attacks.

The page also names the usual cause. One of the factors that contributes to insecure design is the lack of business risk profiling inherent in the software or system being developed, and thus the failure to determine what level of security design is required. Read that against Robb's post, which describes filters for mislabelled runs and for cars and planes, and a normalisation chosen because it "looks really nice". The risks profiled were data quality and appearance. Nobody in the record profiled the reader.

Strava's heatmap represents a textbook example of insecure design with zero implementation defects. Not a single line of software failed to execute its intended specification. To understand why sensitive military outposts were unveiled to the world, one must examine two explicit mathematical decisions detailed by Strava engineer Drew Robb: rasterization geometry and color normalization.

In Strava's original heatmap, each GPS coordinate recorded by a user device was rasterized as a single isolated pixel. Because fitness trackers record at most one coordinate per second, runners moving quickly created discontinuous dotted lines separated by multi-pixel gaps. When Strava rebuilt the system in late 2017, it increased resolution to two meters per pixel and replaced point plotting with geometric interpolation. The new engine drew each activity as a continuous, pixel-perfect path connecting sequential GPS coordinates. This change produced elegant trails in suburban parks. In an area with no other activity, one repeated route is drawn as a continuous line at two-metre resolution.

The second decision amplified that geometry through statistical contrast. Mapping raw activity counts from an unbounded range onto a fixed display colormap presents a fundamental visualization challenge. If a platform applies a linear scale, high-density areas like Central Park saturate the color palette while rural trails remain invisible. Strava solved this by applying histogram equalization, using the cumulative distribution function of the raw heat values. Under this algorithm, the normalized value of any given pixel represented the exact percentage of pixels with a lower heat value, guaranteeing a perfectly uniform distribution of colors across the display map.

Robb states the cost himself: the map "is not absolutely quantitative. The same color only locally represents the same level of heat data". Where the background is dense, a route needs company to show. Where it is empty, the normalisation stretches whatever is there across the full colour range. The USENIX paper's phrase for the condition is "the sparsity of background noise in some regions".

The platform also conflated two different secrets: who and where. The USENIX paper draws the line: "the identities of specific athletes were not directly leaked in the heatmap". The map was aggregate tiles without user identifiers. Identifying a person needed a different feature; the paper reports that "a related vulnerability allows an attacker to upload spoofed GPS data in order to discover the IDs of Athletes in a given area". The heatmap did not leak who was running. It leaked where anyone was running.

No automated code scanner could ever have detected this exposure. A static analysis tool examines source code for known defect patterns: buffer overflows, format string vulnerabilities, unescaped SQL queries, or improper authorization checks. A dynamic scanner tests running endpoints for injection flaws and malformed inputs. Nothing in Strava's record describes a defect of that kind for a scanner to find. The flaw lay entirely in the business logic: the unverified architectural assumption that aggregating public data across geography is inherently safe, regardless of baseline population density.

The defense

When the military establishment responded to the heatmap disclosures, the popular press seized upon a simple narrative. In August 2018 Ars Technica summarized the government's action with the headline "Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones". But the primary record reveals a far more structured and nuanced operational policy. The Pentagon did not ban the watch. It designed an operational threat model to manage the interface between commercial technology and military deployments.

On 3 August 2018 the Deputy Secretary of Defense issued a two-page memorandum, "Use of Geolocation-Capable Devices, Applications, and Services". The policy established an immediate prohibition, but it drew precise boundaries. It prohibited DoD personnel from using "geolocation features and functionality on both non-government and government-issued devices, applications, and services while in locations designated as operational areas." The prohibition names features, not devices. A watch with its geolocation switched off is not mentioned.

Crucially, the memorandum built formal threat modeling directly into its operational exceptions. The policy recognized that operational realities vary across units and environments. Instead of a blanket ban, the directive lets Combatant Commanders or their designees to authorize non-government geolocation capabilities in operational areas after conducting a "threat-based comprehensive Operations Security (OPSEC) survey" under DoD Directive 5205.02E. For government-issued devices, commanders could grant authorization based on mission necessity after weighing potential OPSEC risks.

For every other location, the memorandum rejected a single rule. Component heads "will consider the inherent risks" and "Should apply a tiered structure for categorizing location and operations sensitivity while incorporating risk factors". That is OWASP's segregation advice in operational form: different exposures, different rules.

Defense planners had described the problem before the map existed. In July 2017, the Government Accountability Office published report GAO-17-668 on Internet of Things security in the military. GAO revealed that DoD's own 2016 IoT policy recommendations had warned that data collected from IoT devices and subsequently aggregated could inform adversaries about unit capabilities, troop deployments, or overtime work patterns. Yet GAO found that the comprehensive OPSEC surveys mandated by DoD Directive 5205.02E were simply not being conducted for IoT devices. DoD concurred with the recommendation. Thirteen months later came the memorandum, which, as saved for this chapter, does not name Strava or the heatmap.

Strava complemented the military's operational controls with architectural changes on the server side. As noted, the company established a multi-athlete threshold for sparse roads and trails, instituted monthly privacy update cycles, and restricted street-level zoom to authenticated users. When academic researchers discovered vulnerabilities in Strava's Privacy Zones—where users set an excluded radius around their homes—Strava adopted randomized spatial cloaking to prevent reverse-engineering of endpoints.

One line in the updates post shows how narrow a per-user control is against an aggregate. Privacy Zones hide an athlete's own activities within the zone, but "Other athletes recording on Strava within an athlete's Privacy Zone may create “heat” on the map through that zone". A control that protects one person's rows does not protect the place. That is why the threshold, not the opt-out, is the design change.

Yet an honest examination of the defense record reveals a profound evidentiary gap. While the Department of Defense documented its policy rules and Strava announced its platform updates, neither entity ever published empirical follow-up audits measuring their real-world impact. The public record does not contain data showing whether sensitive military installations actually vanished from subsequent open-source aggregations, or whether personnel compliance with the August 2018 geolocation directive was achieved. Publishing a policy is not the same as measuring its effect.

What you can now see

Find one feature in a system you run that publishes an aggregate: a heatmap, a leaderboard, a usage summary, a "popular near you". Assume the code is correct and every row in it was shared on purpose. Then ask what a hostile reader learns from the places where the aggregate is nearly empty, because that is where a handful of ordinary actions stops being a crowd and becomes an outline.

Strava's record supplies the test: the map was built from public activities, normalised for maximum contrast, and shown everywhere at the same resolution, so that in an empty region one person's routine drew a base. If your aggregate has no minimum below which it shows nothing, you have that design. The fix Strava published is a threshold, several athletes before any heat; the fix the Pentagon published is a threat survey before the exception. Neither is a scanner finding. Both are a question someone had to ask before the empty map was drawn.
