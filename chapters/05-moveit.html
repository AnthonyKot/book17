Chapter 5 · A05:2025 Injection

One Function, Three Patches

On 31 May 2023, Progress warned MOVEit Transfer customers about a critical vulnerability and published fixes for supported versions. The theft had already started: incident responders found the earliest evidence on 27 May, with data sometimes leaving within minutes of a web shell arriving. The familiar story is one zero-day SQL injection in a file-transfer product. That label is right. It hides the useful parts. The route into the query did not look like a form field, and the vulnerable function did try to escape its input. It escaped one use and missed another. The first patch changed that function; two more SQL-injection advisories followed within fifteen days. One function explains both the break and the defense.

The break

TechCrunch supplied the durable headline: a zero-day let the Cl0p extortion group raid MOVEit Transfer servers and steal customer data. TechTarget called CVE-2023-34362 a critical SQL-injection flaw and said servers were already under attack when Progress released its patch. A Progress spokesperson, quoted by TechTarget, called the operation a sophisticated, multi-stage zero-day attack and said the 31 May patch “fixed the issue.” That last phrase matters. It is the vendor’s statement as relayed by TechTarget, not a quotation from a Progress filing.

The clock began before any public warning. Mandiant placed the earliest evidence of exploitation on 27 May 2023. Progress’s first formal account says its support team received a customer call about unusual activity on the evening of 28 May. An investigative team mobilized, contacted customers and took MOVEit Cloud down on 30 May. It released a patch for supported versions on 31 May and restored the cloud service that day.

The saved first capture of Progress’s 31 May Knowledge Base article catches the advisory while the patch was still being made. It told customers to block HTTP and HTTPS traffic, inspect at least thirty days of activity, and leave only SFTP and FTP/S operating normally. The article’s revision history says all supported-version fixes were posted on 31 May. By 2 June it named CVE-2023-34362 and described unauthenticated access to the MOVEit database through SQL injection. CISA added that CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 June and marked ransomware campaign use as known.

CISA and the FBI later summarized the campaign with an important qualification: “According to open source information,” Cl0p began exploiting the previously unknown flaw on 27 May. Their advisory says the group used the SQL injection to install a web shell called LEMURLOOT. Mandiant saw data theft within minutes of some web-shell deployments and described the activity as seemingly opportunistic. It initially used the cluster name UNC4857, then merged that activity into FIN11 from overlaps in targeting, infrastructure and other evidence. The chapter need not reproduce the requests or payload. The public patch is enough to understand the broken boundary.

That boundary sat on an internet-facing file-transfer service. Rapid7 counted roughly 2,500 exposed MOVEit Transfer instances on 31 May; Huntress reported a Shodan result above 2,500. The later totals count a much larger downstream event. In September, Emsisoft told TechTarget that 1,690 of 2,098 then-known victim organizations were compromised through third parties. Genworth shows the shape: it reported that a vendor using MOVEit exposed information for about 2.5 to 2.7 million policyholders, while Genworth itself did not use MOVEit.

This is why the large people number needs a label. Emsisoft’s June 2024 tally lists 2,773 organizations and 95,788,491 individuals, but says some people will overlap and it cannot account for them. KonBriefing makes the denominator plainer: its sum is people records, not distinct people, and a person affected through several organizations appears several times. These compilations measure public notifications and disclosures. They do not turn 2,500 exposed servers into ninety-five million separate human beings, nor do they claim to.

The first patch changed two places that Rapid7 found by decompiling the application and comparing the builds. It removed SetAllSessionVarsFromHeaders(), a function used by machine2.aspx, and rebuilt UserGetUsersWithEmailAddress(). Rapid7 traced the removed function reading key-value pairs from an X-siLock-SessVar header. Logs showed machine2.aspx being fetched from localhost after an ISAPI call. Huntress described the same division of labor: the ISAPI component performed SQL injection under particular headers, while guestaccess.aspx prepared a session. A value could therefore reach the query through session state, outside the ordinary form-input path a reviewer might first test.

Then one patch became three advisories. On 9 June, Progress said a third-party code review had found vulnerabilities distinct from CVE-2023-34362; its advisory credited Huntress and described multiple SQL-injection vulnerabilities under CVE-2023-35036. On 15 June, Progress issued another patch for CVE-2023-35708 after a new SQL-injection vulnerability had been posted publicly. It said it had seen no evidence that flaw was exploited. The July service pack added CVE-2023-36934, an unauthenticated SQL injection, and CVE-2023-36932, authenticated SQL injections. Across five weeks, Progress patched five CVE identifiers for SQL injection in MOVEit Transfer. Only the first is recorded here as exploited.

There is also a legal record, but it answers a different question. By its January 2024 annual filing, Progress reported about 118 class actions and three formal government investigations. In August 2024 it said the SEC would not recommend an enforcement action at that time. In July 2025, MDL Order 22 allowed some claims against Progress to continue past a motion to dismiss. The order expressly says its facts are the plaintiffs’ well-pleaded allegations. Its discussion of a persistent SQL-injection vulnerability is a pleading-stage ruling, not a finding that the allegation is true. The public record does not establish how long this function existed or what Progress should have known about it.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category this way: An injection vulnerability is an application flaw that allows untrusted user input to be sent to an interpreter (e.g. a browser, database, the command line) and causes the interpreter to execute parts of that input as commands. The interpreter here is a SQL database. Its grammar gives quotation marks, operators and keywords structural meaning. If an application makes one string from its SQL and a caller’s text, the database must decide which characters are instructions and which are data.

The smallest unsafe shape is query = prefix + input + suffix. Escaping tries to rewrite every meaningful character in input for one exact context. That promise is local. Change the surrounding quotes, use the value twice, switch database engines, or forget one occurrence, and the promise must be proved again. A parameterized query changes the interface. The application sends a statement containing placeholders and supplies the values separately. The driver presents those values as data, not as more statement text.

Rapid7’s diff turns that distinction into something you can draw. In the old UserGetUsersWithEmailAddress(), one format argument held an escaped email and another held the raw email. Selected pre-patch expressions are below. Rapid7 published them from its decompilation; line breaks and indentation have been added for legibility.

// pre-patch: one formatted SQL string
(object) "Email='{2}' OR "
(object) this.siGlobs.objUtility.BuildLikeForSQL(
    "Email", "{1},%", bEscapeAndConvertMatchString: false)

(object) InstID,
(object) this.siGlobs.objUtility.EscapeLikeForSQL(EmailAddress), // {1}
(object) EmailAddress                                           // {2}

this.siGlobs.objWrap.DoReadQuery(
    Conversions.ToString(obj), ref MyRS, true);

Follow the subscripts, not the surrounding decompiler noise. The LIKE branch consumes {1}, the escaped value. The equality branch spells Email='{2}' and consumes the raw value. The developer did treat the address as hostile. The treatment covered one use and not the other, inside the same statement. Rapid7 reported that naive attempts through the paths it first traced failed because arguments were escaped. The route through session state reached the use that was not.

The replacement keeps query construction and values apart. These are selected post-patch lines from the same published diff, again wrapped only for display. 

// post-patch: a builder plus named values
SQLBasicBuilder where = this._sqlBuilderUsers.SelectBuilder()
    .AddColumnsToSelect("Username", "Permission", "LoginName", "Email")
    .AddAndColumnEqualsToWhere<string>(nameof (InstID), InstID, true)
    .AddAndColumnEqualsToWhere<int>("Deleted", 0);

string.Format("Email={0}", (object) func("Email"))
where.WithParameter("Email", (object) EmailAddress);
where.WithParameter("FirstEmail", (object) string.Format("{0},%", (object) str));
where.WithParameter("MiddleEmail", (object) string.Format("%,{0},%", (object) str));
where.WithParameter("LastEmail", (object) string.Format("%,{0}", (object) str));

this.siGlobs.objWrap.DoReadQuery(
    where.GetQuery(), where.Parameters, ref MyRS, true);

The final call is the whiteboard boundary. Before the patch, DoReadQuery received one completed string. After it, the call receives a query and a parameter collection. The pattern values still need handling for the meaning of LIKE, but they no longer become SQL syntax merely by entering the statement. Four named bindings make four data flows visible. A reviewer can count them against the placeholders.

The removed header function explains why “sanitize the form” is too narrow. It turned a special request header into session values; a later page loaded its package information from that session; four application calls led to the vulnerable user lookup. None of those facts makes headers inherently dangerous. They show why trust must follow the value, not the widget that normally supplies it. Once external influence crosses into session state, its polite internal name does not make it internal data.

The defense

OWASP’s preferred prevention is explicit: The preferred option is to use a safe API, which avoids using the interpreter entirely, provides a parameterized interface, or migrates to Object Relational Mapping Tools (ORMs). That is not the same as banning punctuation from email addresses. It changes who is responsible for separating SQL from data. With escaping, each call site must get the transformation and context right. With a parameterized interface, the database driver performs that separation for every bound value.

The first MOVEit patch is a record of that defense being applied to the exploited function. Rapid7’s comparison shows string formatting replaced by SQLBasicBuilder, four calls to WithParameter(), and a read call that accepts both the generated query and its parameters. The patch also removed the function that copied the special headers into session state. One change closed the sink; the other closed the route that reached it. A code review can reason about either change independently.

Parameterization does not certify an entire product. A builder can expose a raw-string escape hatch, and an application can keep older query paths beside the new one. The June sequence demonstrates that scope problem. Progress said outside scrutiny led to additional findings and prompt patches; it reported no evidence that those later vulnerabilities were exploited before fixes were available. That is evidence of review and remediation, not evidence that every database call had become parameterized.

In March 2024, CISA and the FBI issued a Secure by Design alert in response to a widely publicized campaign against SQL-injection defects in a managed file-transfer application. The alert says the campaign affected thousands of organizations, but it does not name Progress or MOVEit. It notes that MySQL introduced prepared statements in 2004 and says SQL injection had been considered “unforgivable” by others since at least 2007. Those dates establish that the technical control was mature. They do not establish why this particular code was written or reviewed as it was.

The alert’s stronger proposal is about defaults inside a manufacturer. It calls for libraries that make the safe route the default and checks at pull-request time. It also asks senior executives to commission formal code review, because a simple review can reveal the prevalence of the class. That recommendation fits what happened after 31 May: Progress says third-party reviews found more vulnerabilities, including those disclosed on 9 June. Finding two rounds of SQL injection after the emergency patch is not an argument against review. It is evidence that repairing one observed path and eliminating a defect class are different jobs.

Progress also announced a regular service-pack program in July, with a roughly two-month cadence, and said the first pack contained three newly disclosed CVEs. Release cadence helps customers plan updates. It does not answer whether unsafe query construction remains possible inside the product. The company’s October filing says on-premises MOVEit Transfer gives Progress no continuing telemetry about installed versions, file activity or whether customers applied patches. That makes patch delivery visible to the vendor and patch adoption partly invisible.

So the defense record has a sharp center and soft edges. The center is the published diff: one vulnerable function moved from a completed SQL string to bound parameters. The edges are not measured. The documents saved for this chapter contain no product-wide count of dynamic queries before or after review, no published root-cause analysis, and no adoption rate for the on-premises patches. A future engineering report could fill that gap. The court orders cannot: Order 22 decides whether allegations plausibly state claims, not whether Progress’s development process caused the defect.

The honest answer to “why did this ship?” therefore has two layers. The code answers how: escaping was a promise repeated at each use, and one use broke it. The public record does not answer the institutional why. CISA’s response is a prescription, not a finding about Progress: make parameterization the normal API, reject raw construction during review, and count the remaining exceptions. The patched function shows exactly what the first step looks like.
