Chapter 5 · A05:2025 Injection

One Function, Three Patches

On 31 May 2023, Progress warned MOVEit Transfer customers of a critical vulnerability and posted fixes. The theft had already begun: responders’ earliest evidence is dated 27 May, and data sometimes left within minutes of a web shell arriving. The familiar story is one zero-day SQL injection in a file-transfer product. The label is right and hides the useful parts. The route in was not a form field, and the vulnerable function did escape its input, for one use and not the other. The first patch rebuilt that function; two more SQL-injection advisories followed within fifteen days. One function explains the break and the defense; the patches after it, why fixing a function and removing a class are different jobs.

The break

TechCrunch supplied the durable headline: a zero-day let the Cl0p extortion group raid MOVEit Transfer servers and steal customer data. TechTarget called CVE-2023-34362 a critical SQL-injection flaw and said instances were already under attack when Progress released its patch. The same piece quoted a Progress spokesperson: an advanced and persistent threat actor had used “a sophisticated, multi-stage attack” to exploit the zero-day, and the patch deployed on 31 May “fixed the issue”. That last phrase is the vendor speaking through a reporter, not a filing, and it is the one folk claim in this chapter that comes from Progress itself.

The clock began before any public warning. Mandiant placed the earliest evidence of exploitation on 27 May 2023. Progress’s 8-K, its first formal account, says its support team received a customer call about unusual activity on the evening of 28 May. An investigative team mobilized, contacted customers and took MOVEit Cloud down on 30 May. It released a patch for supported versions on 31 May and restored the cloud service that day.

The earliest saved capture of Progress’s 31 May Knowledge Base article still reads “while our team produces a patch”. It told customers to block HTTP and HTTPS traffic, check at least thirty days of activity for signs of unauthorized access, and noted that SFTP and FTP/S would keep working. The article’s own revision history says all supported-version fixes were posted on 31 May. By 2 June it carried the CVE number, CVE-2023-34362, and a description of unauthenticated access to the MOVEit database through SQL injection. CISA added the CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 June; the entry records known ransomware-campaign use.

CISA and the FBI later summarized the campaign with an important qualification: “According to open source information,” Cl0p began exploiting the previously unknown flaw on 27 May. Their advisory says the group used the SQL injection to install a web shell called LEMURLOOT. Mandiant saw data theft within minutes of some web-shell deployments and described the activity as seemingly opportunistic. It first tracked the activity as UNC4857, then merged it into FIN11 on overlaps in targeting, infrastructure and other evidence.

The vulnerable page sat on an internet-facing file-transfer service. Rapid7 counted roughly 2,500 exposed MOVEit Transfer instances on 31 May; Huntress reported a Shodan result above 2,500. The later totals count a much larger downstream event. In September, TechTarget reported that 1,690 of 2,098 then-known victim organizations had been compromised through third parties rather than directly. Genworth shows the shape: it reported that a vendor using MOVEit exposed information for about 2.5 to 2.7 million policyholders, while Genworth itself did not use MOVEit.

That is why the big number needs a label. Emsisoft’s June 2024 tally lists 2,773 organizations and 95,788,491 individuals, but says some people will overlap and it cannot account for them. KonBriefing makes the denominator plainer: its sum is people records, not distinct people, and a person affected through several organizations appears several times. These compilations measure public notifications and disclosures. They do not turn 2,500 exposed servers into ninety-five million separate human beings, nor do they claim to.

The first patch changed two places that Rapid7 found by decompiling the application and comparing the builds. It removed SetAllSessionVarsFromHeaders(), a function used by machine2.aspx, and rebuilt UserGetUsersWithEmailAddress(). Rapid7 traced the removed function reading key-value pairs from an X-siLock-SessVar header. Logs showed machine2.aspx being fetched from localhost after an ISAPI call. The next request loaded that session in guestaccess.aspx, whose call path reached the vulnerable lookup. A value could therefore reach the query through session state, outside the ordinary form-input path a reviewer might first test.

Then the one patch became a sequence. On 9 June, Progress said a third-party code review had found vulnerabilities distinct from CVE-2023-34362; its advisory credited Huntress and described multiple SQL-injection vulnerabilities under CVE-2023-35036. On 15 June, Progress issued another patch for CVE-2023-35708 after a new SQL-injection vulnerability had been posted publicly. It said it had seen no evidence that flaw was exploited. The July service pack added CVE-2023-36934, an unauthenticated SQL injection, and CVE-2023-36932, authenticated SQL injections.

Across five weeks, Progress patched five CVE identifiers for SQL injection in MOVEit Transfer. Only the first is recorded as exploited in the sources saved for this chapter.

There is also a legal record, but it answers a different question. By its January 2024 annual filing, Progress reported about 118 class actions and three formal government investigations. In August 2024 it said the SEC would not recommend an enforcement action at that time. In July 2025, MDL Order 22 allowed some claims against Progress to continue past a motion to dismiss. The order expressly says its facts are the plaintiffs’ well-pleaded allegations. Its discussion of a persistent SQL-injection vulnerability is a pleading-stage ruling, not a finding that the allegation is true. The public record does not establish how long this function existed or what Progress should have known about it.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category this way: An injection vulnerability is an application flaw that allows untrusted user input to be sent to an interpreter (e.g. a browser, database, the command line) and causes the interpreter to execute parts of that input as commands. The interpreter here is a SQL database. Its grammar gives quotation marks, operators and keywords structural meaning. If an application makes one string from its SQL and a caller’s text, the database must decide which characters are instructions and which are data.

The smallest unsafe shape is query = prefix + input + suffix. OWASP’s list of when an application is vulnerable includes this line: Dynamic queries or non-parameterized calls without context-aware escaping are used directly in the interpreter. Context-aware is the load-bearing phrase. Escaping rewrites the meaningful characters in input for one exact context: inside these quotes, in this clause, for this engine. Change the surrounding quotes, use the value twice, switch engines, or forget one occurrence, and the promise must be proved again. A parameterized query changes the interface. The application sends a statement with placeholders and supplies the values separately; the driver presents them as data, never as more statement text.

Rapid7’s diff turns that distinction into something you can draw. In the old UserGetUsersWithEmailAddress(), one format argument held an escaped email and another held the raw email. The pseudocode below abridges Rapid7’s published decompilation: it keeps the SELECT, every use of the address and the final call, and drops two optional clauses and the decompiler’s late-binding scaffolding.

// abridged pseudocode: one completed SQL string, then one call
string sql = string.Format(
    "SELECT Username, Permission, LoginName, Email FROM users " +
    "WHERE InstID={0} AND Deleted=0 AND (" +
    "Email='{2}' OR " +                                    // raw
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "{1},%",   false) + " OR " +  // escaped
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "%,{1}",   false) + " OR " +  // escaped
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", "%,{1},%", false) +           // escaped
    ") ORDER BY LoginName",
    InstID,                          // {0}
    EscapeLikeForSQL(EmailAddress),  // {1}
    EmailAddress);                   // {2}

DoReadQuery(sql, ref results, true);

Follow the subscripts. The three LIKE branches consume {1}, the escaped value. The equality branch spells Email='{2}' and consumes the raw one. That is the whiteboard: one address, four slots in one string, an escaper in front of the pattern slots and nothing in front of the equality. The developer did treat the address as hostile. The treatment covered one kind of use and not the other, inside the same statement. Rapid7 reported that naive injection attempts along the paths it first traced failed, because in every case the application escaped the arguments; the path it eventually found ran through session state to this same function.

The replacement keeps query construction and values apart. This pseudocode abridges the patched side of the same diff: it keeps the four clauses, their named placeholders, the bindings and the call that consumes both collections, and drops the column list, the InstID and Deleted conditions and the ordering. 

// abridged pseudocode: attach clauses, then bind their values
var where = SelectBuilder();
var escaped = EscapeLikeForSQL(EmailAddress);
var clauses = new List<string> {
    "Email=" + ParameterName("Email"),
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("FirstEmail"), false, false),
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("MiddleEmail"), false, false),
    BuildLikeForSQL("Email", ParameterName("LastEmail"), false, false)
};

where.WithParameter("Email", EmailAddress);
where.WithParameter("FirstEmail", escaped + ",%");
where.WithParameter("MiddleEmail", "%," + escaped + ",%");
where.WithParameter("LastEmail", "%," + escaped);
where.AddAndToWhere("(" + string.Join(" OR ", clauses) + ")");

DoReadQuery(where.GetQuery(), where.Parameters, ref results, true);

The final call is the whiteboard boundary. Before the patch, DoReadQuery received one completed string. After it, the call receives a query and a parameter collection. The pattern values still need handling for the meaning of LIKE, but they no longer become SQL syntax merely by entering the statement. Four named bindings make four data flows visible; a reviewer can count them against four placeholders.

The removed header function explains why “sanitize the form” is too narrow. It turned a special request header into session values; a later page loaded its package information from that session; four application calls led to the vulnerable user lookup. None of that makes headers dangerous in themselves. It shows that trust must follow the value, not the widget that normally supplies it. A value that crossed from a request header into session state is still the caller’s value, whatever the variable holding it is now called.

The defense

OWASP’s preferred prevention is explicit: The preferred option is to use a safe API, which avoids using the interpreter entirely, provides a parameterized interface, or migrates to Object Relational Mapping Tools (ORMs). That is not the same as banning punctuation from email addresses. It changes who is responsible for separating SQL from data. With escaping, each call site must get the transformation and context right. With a parameterized interface, the database driver performs that separation for every bound value.

The first MOVEit patch is a record of that defense being applied to the exploited function. Rapid7’s comparison shows string formatting replaced by SQLBasicBuilder, four calls to WithParameter(), and a read call that accepts both the generated query and its parameters. The patch also removed the function that copied the special headers into session state. One change closed the sink; the other closed the route that reached it. A code review can reason about either change independently.

Parameterization does not certify an entire product. A builder can expose a raw-string escape hatch, and an application can keep older query paths beside the new one. The June sequence demonstrates that scope problem. Progress said outside scrutiny led to additional findings and prompt patches; it reported no evidence that those later vulnerabilities were exploited before fixes were available. That is evidence of review and remediation, not evidence that every database call had become parameterized.

In March 2024, CISA and the FBI issued a Secure by Design alert in response to a widely publicized campaign against SQL-injection defects in a managed file-transfer application. The alert says the campaign affected thousands of organizations, but it does not name Progress or MOVEit. It notes that MySQL introduced prepared statements in 2004 and says SQL injection had been considered “unforgivable” by others since at least 2007. Those dates establish that the technical control was mature. They do not establish why this particular code was written or reviewed as it was.

The alert’s stronger proposal is about defaults inside a manufacturer. It calls for libraries that make the safe route the default and checks at pull-request time. It also asks senior executives to commission formal code review, because a simple review can reveal the prevalence of the class. That recommendation fits what happened after 31 May: Progress says third-party reviews found more vulnerabilities, including those disclosed on 9 June. Finding two rounds of SQL injection after the emergency patch is not an argument against review. It is evidence that repairing one observed path and eliminating a defect class are different jobs.

Progress also announced a regular service-pack program in July, with a roughly two-month cadence, and said the first pack contained three newly disclosed CVEs. Release cadence helps customers plan updates. It does not answer whether unsafe query construction remains possible inside the product. The company’s October filing says on-premises MOVEit Transfer gives Progress no continuing telemetry about installed versions, file activity or whether customers applied patches. That makes patch delivery visible to the vendor and patch adoption partly invisible.

So the defense record has a sharp center and soft edges. The center is the published diff: one vulnerable function moved from a completed SQL string to bound parameters. The edges are unmeasured. The documents saved for this chapter contain no product-wide count of dynamic queries before or after the reviews, no published root-cause analysis, and no adoption rate for the on-premises patches. That is why the honest question, why a product shipped in 2023 with this function built this way, gets a two-layer answer. The code answers how: escaping was a promise repeated at each use, and one use broke it. The record is silent on the institutional why, and CISA’s alert is a prescription, not a finding about Progress. What the patched function shows is the first step: make the bound-parameter path the normal one, then count what is left outside it.

What you can now see

This week, open one function in a system you run that takes a value from outside and searches the database with it; a lookup by email address is the obvious candidate. Do not begin with the input validator. Begin at the final database call. Does that call receive one completed SQL string, or a statement and a separate parameter collection?

If it receives one string, follow a single value backward through every appearance in that statement. A value used in an equality test and three pattern tests has four contexts, even when it came from one field. Mark each occurrence as raw, escaped, or bound. The dangerous finding is not a missing escape. It is an interface that lets the answer differ at each occurrence.

Then follow the value one step farther back than its name suggests. Session variables, cached objects and internal request headers can still contain caller-controlled data. Trust is a property of the route a value travelled, not of the variable holding it now.

The useful end state fits in one line: the query text contains placeholders, and the values cross the driver boundary separately. If your ordinary database API makes that shape awkward, the API is part of the finding. Change the default path before auditing every exception. That is how one missed clause becomes difficult to write, instead of merely easier to catch.
