Chapter 3 · A03:2025 Software Supply Chain Failures

The Tarball Was Not the Tree

You know the headline. WIRED tells it as a patient attacker who would "spend two years politely and enthusiastically volunteering to help" a compression library that nearly every Linux distribution carries. The record is narrower. An account using the name Jia Tan contributed to xz for two years, became the tagger and signer of its releases, and signed the two tarballs that carried a backdoor reaching for SSH. An engineer who, by his own description, was not a security researcher caught it because logins were half a second slow. The detail the headline drops is the one a defender needed. The code everyone could review, in git, never contained the trigger; the release archive that distributions built from did. The attack lived in the gap between the tree people read and the tarball machines compiled, and nothing in the collected record shows anyone measuring that gap before Freund.

The break

On 29 March 2024, at 08:51 Pacific time, Andres Freund posted to the oss-security list under the subject "backdoor in upstream xz/liblzma leading to ssh server compromise". He had been chasing "a few odd symptoms around liblzma (part of the xz package) on Debian sid installations over the last weeks (logins with ssh taking a lot of CPU, valgrind errors)". His timing table is the most quoted number of the incident. A failed login to a non-existent user took 0.299 seconds before the new library was installed and 0.807 seconds after. He was careful about his standing: "I am *not* a security researcher, nor a reverse engineer".

What he had found was in two places. The first was the release archive. "One portion of the backdoor is *solely in the distributed tarballs*", he wrote. The tarballs for 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 carried a modified copy of a build-system macro file, m4/build-to-host.m4, that was "*not* in the upstream source of build-to-host, nor is build-to-host used by xz in git". The second place was the repository itself. Two files under tests/files/ held the payload in obfuscated form, and they "were not even used for any 'tests' in 5.6.0". Red Hat assigned CVE-2024-3094. NVD published it the same day with a CVSS 3.1 base score of 10.0 and the weakness CWE-506, embedded malicious code.

The folk story of how the account got there is told best by WIRED, which describes an attacker who "can spend two years politely and enthusiastically volunteering to help". WIRED adds that "it's unlikely that Jia Tan is a real person, or even one person working alone". Wikipedia's account calls the emails of June 2022 "a pressure campaign against the original maintainer". The commits and the list archive let us check the chronology, which is all they can check. The first patch from the address that would later sign the tarballs was authored on 28 January 2022 and committed by the maintainer, Lasse Collin, on 7 February.

In June 2022, on the xz-devel list, a poster named Jigar Kumar wrote that "Submitting patches here has no purpose these days. The current maintainer lost interest or doesn't care to maintain anymore". Collin answered the next day. "I haven't lost interest but my ability to care has been fairly limited mostly due to longterm mental health issues but also due to some other things". He had "worked off-list a bit with Jia Tan on XZ Utils and perhaps he will have a bigger role in the future", and "this is an unpaid hobby project". Kumar pressed again a week later ("Why wait until 5.4.0 to change maintainer? Why delay what your repo needs?"). A second poster, Dennis Ens, added that "its important to be aware of your own limits". On 29 June Collin wrote that Jia Tan "has been helping a lot off-list and is practically a co-maintainer already".

The record then moves into git. Jia Tan's first commit as committer, not just author, is dated 30 December 2022. Every release tag from 5.4.2 (18 March 2023) to 5.6.1 (9 March 2024) names Jia Tan as tagger and carries a PGP signature block, nine tags in all. The tags before them, 5.4.0 and 5.4.1, and the tags after, 5.4.7 and 5.6.2, are Collin's. Among commits dated 2023 the repository records 313 authored by Jia Tan against 164 by Collin. Collin did not leave. His last commits before the 5.6.0 tag are dated 22 February 2024, two days earlier.

The payload's own timeline is longer than a February. Collin's commit-by-commit review, published on 29 May 2024, marks a commit of 22 January 2024 adding two RISC-V test files as "the first commit preparing for the backdoor". Three days earlier, four commits had moved the project's published URLs from tukaani.org to the subdomain xz.tukaani.org. Collin later said that subdomain was hosted on GitHub and was the only part of the domain Jia Tan controlled. On 15 February a one-line commit added build-to-host.m4 to m4/.gitignore. Collin's review notes: "Modified build-to-host.m4 had the backdoor trigger". Ignoring it was correct, he adds, "because it is added among several other .m4 files when running ./autogen.sh or autoreconf -fi". On 23 February came "Tests: Add a few test files", five binary files including the two that carried the payload; the review calls them "Backdoor files". The 5.6.0 tag followed on 24 February.

Two days after that, a commit titled "Build: Fix Linux Landlock feature test in Autotools and CMake builds" placed a line consisting of a single "." inside the C snippet that CMake compiles to detect the Landlock sandbox. A bare dot is not C, so the detection always failed and the sandbox was never enabled. Collin fixed it on 30 March with the message "CMake: Fix sabotaged Landlock sandbox check. It never enabled it".

In early March the backdoor was producing crashes and Valgrind errors on some systems. Freund lists three commits of 5 and 9 March presented as fixes for an ifunc and Valgrind problem, then a fourth, "Tests: Update two test files", that replaced the payload files. The 5.6.1 tag is dated 9 March. "Given the activity over several weeks", Freund wrote, "the committer is either directly involved or there was some quite severe compromise of their system. Unfortunately the latter looks like the less likely explanation".

Who had shipped it? Debian's advisory DSA-5649-1 says the compromised packages "were part of the Debian testing, unstable and experimental distributions, with versions ranging from 5.5.1alpha-0.1 (uploaded on 2024-02-01), up to and including 5.6.1-1". No Debian stable version was known to be affected at the time of the advisory, and the package was reverted to the 5.4.5 code. Red Hat told Fedora Rawhide users to "IMMEDIATELY STOP USAGE". In an update the next day it confirmed that the Fedora 40 beta contained two affected builds of xz-libs 5.6.0. openSUSE said Tumbleweed and MicroOS had carried 5.6.0 "between March 7 and March 28". It recommended that users with SSH exposed to the internet install fresh, "as it's unknown if the backdoor has been exploited".

Collin's statement on tukaani.org is short. "XZ Utils 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 release tarballs contain a backdoor. These tarballs were created and signed by Jia Tan". Only Collin had access to tukaani.org and git.tukaani.org; "Jia Tan only had access to things hosted on GitHub". The forwarding of the project's email address to Jia Tan was cut on 30 March.

On 8 April Collin committed "Remove the backdoor found in 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 (CVE-2024-3094)", reverting eight commits. Among his stated reasons: "The maintainer who added the backdoor has disappeared". Clean releases 5.4.7 and 5.6.2 followed on 29 May. The ifunc mechanism the backdoor had used was removed, and the release notes insist this was "*NOT* done for security reasons" but because its benefit was too small. The documents collected for this chapter, fetched in September 2026, contain no charge and no attribution of the account to a person or a state; Collin's statement uses the name and nothing more. The chapter therefore names Jia Tan as a git identity and a signer of tarballs, which is what the record establishes.

The mechanism

OWASP's 2025 list expands the old "Vulnerable and Outdated Components" category into a wider one. It defines the new category so: Software supply chain failures are breakdowns or other compromises in the process of building, distributing, or updating software. They are often caused by vulnerabilities or malicious changes in third-party code, tools, or other dependencies that the system relies on. The word to underline is building. Nothing in the xz story is a vulnerability in the compression code. It is a change to the process that turns reviewed source into a shipped library.

Draw it as a pipeline with five boxes. Box one is the git tree, which is what reviewers read. Box two is the release tarball, which is what distributions download. For an Autotools project the two are not the same. The tarball adds generated files: a configure script tens of thousands of lines long, and a directory of .m4 macro files that autoreconf copies in from the GNU tool chain. They are usually treated as generated noise, because they are the output of a tool. build-to-host.m4 is one of them, and the copy in the 5.6.x tarballs was not what the tool produces: it carried a line that is "*not* in the upstream source of build-to-host".

Box three is configure running. In Freund's description, the modified macro "injects an obfuscated script to be executed at the end of configure", which, "if some preconditions match, modifies $builddir/src/liblzma/Makefile". The script goes on only on x86-64 Linux, only with gcc and GNU ld, and only inside a Debian or RPM package build. Freund thought the last condition was "likely aimed at making it harder to reproduce the issue for investigators". Box four is the Makefile, and here the record gives us the hook itself:

am__test = bad-3-corrupt_lzma2.xz
...
am__test_dir=$(top_srcdir)/tests/files/$(am__test)
...
sed rpath $(am__test_dir) | $(am__dist_setup) >/dev/null 2>&1

Freund shows that this expands to a pipeline: read a "corrupt" test file, fix its bytes with tr, decompress it with xz -d, and pipe the result to /bin/bash. That shell script in turn extracts a prebuilt object file from the second test file and links it into liblzma. NVD's one-paragraph description says exactly this: the build "extracts a prebuilt object file from a disguised test file existing in the source code, which is then used to modify specific functions in the liblzma code".

Box five is the running process. The object replaces the resolvers that glibc's ifunc mechanism calls at load time to choose a CRC implementation. From there, when the host process is /usr/sbin/sshd, it redirects the RSA_public_decrypt entry in the program's linkage table to its own code. Freund's trace shows that code being invoked during a public-key login, "in a pre-authentication context".

Why did this reach sshd at all? Because of an edge nobody drew. "openssh does not directly use liblzma", Freund wrote. "However debian and several other distributions patch openssh to support systemd notification, and libsystemd does depend on lzma". A distribution patch, not upstream OpenSSH, put a compression library into the address space of a daemon that commonly faces the internet, and put it there before any login is checked.

Why is it easy to write and hard to see? Three reasons the record makes concrete. Generated files are treated as noise: a diff of one tarball against the previous one is dominated by regenerated autoconf output, and a review culture built on git never sees the tarball at all. Binary test fixtures are opaque by nature, and a compression project must have them. The larger payload file was 35,430 bytes of "compressed data" in a directory full of compressed data. And the preparation was spread across a dozen commits with plausible titles: the eight Collin later reverted, the .gitignore change, two ifunc "fixes", and the Landlock "fix" that disabled a sandbox with a single character that reads as a typo.

Collin, reviewing everything afterward, wrote: "While the commits weren't signed, I didn't spot any signs of committer fraud". That is an absence of detected fraud, not a proof; what the record establishes is that the commits carry the account's name and that Collin found nothing to suggest otherwise. OWASP's prevention list says Prefer signed packages to reduce the chance of including a modified, malicious component. The xz tarballs were signed, and the signatures were valid: the project's release key had signed those bytes, and the project had let the account holding that key cut every release since 5.4.2. A valid signature shows that a particular key signed a particular file. It does not show who operated the key, who built the file, or whether the file matches any source a reviewer has read.

The defense

Start with what was in place and why it did not fire. The tarball's signature would have passed any check against the project's published key. Collin's statement says the 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 tarballs "were created and signed by Jia Tan". The modified macro file sat among the generated .m4 files that autoreconf would have produced fresh in a git checkout. In the tarball it arrived ready-made, and Freund's post points to line 63 of the file as Debian had imported it into its packaging repository. Nothing in the record collected here shows anyone comparing the tarball to the tag before Freund did. The one check that did fire was a human noticing a half-second. Freund's own post describes how contingent that was: the slowdown did not appear at first outside systemd, depended on which environment variables were set, and was observed on Debian sid.

Now the defense the contract asks about. Reproducible builds is the practice of making a package build bit-for-bit identically from the same inputs, so that an independent rebuild can show the shipped binary came from the declared source. Would it have caught xz? The record says: not as the practice stood, and the practitioners' own posts show what it was used for instead. Within a day of the disclosure, Debian's Vagrant Cascadian was asked to rebuild recent security updates "to gain confidence about Debian's buildd infrastructure". Those builders ran "in sid chroots which may have used or built or run a vulnerable xz-utils". He rebuilt eleven bookworm security updates and the reverting xz-utils package and "was able to get to build bit-for-bit identical with what is in the Debian archive".

That is a real result, and it is the right shape, but read it as narrowly as its author did. He stated the caveats himself. His own machine had pulled packages from sid for months and "may have at some point run the vulnerable xz-utils code". The check, he wrote, was "not absolutely cleanest" and "at least some sort of data point". What the rebuild showed is that the sampled outputs matched, bit for bit, what the archive held, despite those caveats. Matching outputs are evidence about the artefacts; they are not proof about what the builders had run or had not done. And it is not detection of the backdoor. A build of the 5.6.1 tarball reproduces perfectly, backdoor included, because the tarball is the declared input.

The strongest recent claim to the contrary is Julien Malka's 2025 essay "How NixOS and reproducible builds could have detected the xz backdoor". His method is to build xz twice, once from the maintainer's tarball and once from the GitHub source archive, and diff the outputs. His own footnote concedes the limit: "it only detects modifications of the tarball that have an impact on the final result. In the case of the xz backdoor, NixOS executables did not contain the backdoor and as such without any modification we would not have discovered the backdoor. So yes, the title is a little bit catchy".

He also notes that where xz sits in the Nix bootstrap "the GitHub source archive cannot be used and we have to rely on the maintainer provided tarball". What his method really is, stripped of the title, is a comparison of two sources. That is the check that would have worked.

The record of that check being adopted is thin but not empty. On 3 April 2024 Arch's kpcyrd wrote to the reproducible-builds list that "a distribution that claims to build from source should operate on VCS snapshots instead of tarballs with 25k lines of pre-generated shell-script". He added that "Some packages have been actively converted to VCS snapshots by Arch Linux staff in response to the xz incident". On 13 April Simon Josefsson announced that libntlm 1.8 would ship, beside the usual tarball, a signed "minimal source-only tarball" made with git archive. His reason: "tarballs with files that are not included in the git archive offer an opportunity to disguise malicious backdoors". That is one upstream project and one distribution. No one has published a count of how many projects followed, and the chapter will not invent one.

The better-documented defense is the removal of the edge. Solar Designer's update to oss-security on 17 April records two changes. OpenSSH implemented systemd readiness notification without libsystemd, committed for the 9.8 release. Upstream systemd changed libsystemd to load its compression libraries with dlopen() on demand rather than link them. After both changes, a backdoored liblzma is still a backdoored liblzma, but it is no longer in sshd's process before a login is checked. That is the least-privilege answer to a supply-chain problem: shrink what a compromised dependency can reach.

Finally the threat model the contract asks for, stated as a model and not a lament. OWASP's own list says you are likely vulnerable if your supply chain systems do not have any separation of duty. No single person should be able to write code and promote it all the way to production without oversight from another human being. In June 2022 xz had one maintainer, who described the work as "an unpaid hobby project". Within the month he called a second account "practically a co-maintainer already", on the strength of help he described as off-list; nine months later that account was tagging and signing releases. Nothing in the record says he was wrong to at the time: Collin's own review marks a commit of January 2024 as "the first commit preparing for the backdoor" and flags nothing earlier.

The model is simply this. A dependency with a single unfunded maintainer is a system with one administrator, and the credential that matters is that administrator's trust. Here the record shows it extended within months of a first patch, and the payload arriving two years in. OWASP's advice to Monitor for libraries and components that are unmaintained or do not create security patches for older versions is aimed at abandonment. xz was the opposite case, a project that suddenly had more maintainer than it could review. A defender's inventory should flag both.

What you can now see

Pick one dependency your build takes as a release tarball rather than a git checkout; a C library with a configure script is the classic case. Download the tarball your pipeline uses. Then run git archive on the matching tag from the project's repository and diff the two trees. You will see a list of files that exist only in the tarball: configure, Makefile.in, a directory of .m4 files, maybe documentation. A code review built on git never sees those files, because they are not in git, and in the 5.6.x tarballs one of them was not what the tool produces.

You do not have to read them either. You have to decide who is allowed to produce them. Either your build regenerates them from the tree and discards the tarball's copies, or you build from the tag and skip the tarball. Both close the gap; neither needs a scanner, an SBOM tool or a budget. Be exact about what each comparison shows. Tarball against tag lists the undeclared generated inputs; in March 2024, build-to-host.m4 would have been on that list as a file with no counterpart in git, not as a visibly modified one. Only a tarball compared against a release you regenerated yourself from the tag would have isolated the altered macro. Neither comparison shows the payload, which was committed to git as test data. A history diff shows binary fixtures being added and replaced, not what they mean. That is the other half of the lesson.
