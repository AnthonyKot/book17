Chapter 4 · A04:2025 Cryptographic Failures

The Backup That Outlived the Fix

On 3 October 2013 Adobe's chief security officer wrote that attackers had "accessed Adobe customer IDs and encrypted passwords on our systems". The dump that followed became the most famous crossword in security: passwords encrypted so that equal passwords looked equal, with each user's plain-text hint printed beside them. The headline version says Adobe never hashed or salted its passwords. The regulators' reports say something more useful. Adobe introduced a replacement authentication system in April 2010. What the attackers copied was a backup database of the old system, kept "as a backup measure in case of issues with the new system". The fix had shipped. The data it replaced was still retained.

The break

The folk story has three tellers. Have I Been Pwned says 153 million accounts were breached in October 2013, "each containing an internal ID, username, email, encrypted password and a password hint in plain text". The same page: "The password cryptography was poorly done and many were quickly resolved back to plain text". Wikipedia, citing a password-manager company: Adobe "failed to use best practices for securing the passwords and has not salted them". And, for the mirror this chapter needs, WIRED on LinkedIn a year earlier: "6.5 million hashed (encrypted) passwords, as many as 300,000 of which had been decrypted and posted online". Every sentence describes the data that leaked. None describes the system that was supposed to have replaced it.

The record is three regulators' reports, from Ireland, Canada and Australia, and Adobe's own announcement. The Australian report has the timeline. The access ran "Between 30 August 2013 and 17 September 2013", when "an unauthorised third party illegally accessed certain customer order information". Adobe "became aware of the unauthorised access on 17 September 2013". What it noticed was "an attempt by the attacker to decrypt card numbers that were a part of the customer order information". On 3 October it "reported on its website that it had been the target of a cyber-attack". Adobe described it to the Canadian Commissioner as "a sophisticated, long-term intrusion of its computer systems". The Australian report identifies the entry point: a compromised public-facing web server used to reach other Adobe servers. It does not identify the vulnerability or technique used to compromise that server. The discovery came from the payment side, not the passwords.

What they took is described the same way in Dublin and in Sydney. The Irish Data Protection Commissioner: "Attackers identified and removed data from a backup server that stored the compromised data". The Australian Commissioner: "The attacker took a copy of a backup database containing the personal information of customers". The contents, in the report's list: "customer usernames (Adobe IDs), email addresses, encrypted passwords", "plain text password hints, names, addresses and telephone numbers of some users, encrypted payment card numbers and payment card expiration dates". A small number of unencrypted passwords, "held in a separate database, may also have been compromised". The Canadian report lists the same data types and adds that Adobe "estimated that over one million Canadian customers were affected".

The Australian numbers show what "current" meant. The database held "1,787,100 Australian active and inactive users whose current password data was involved". It also held "218,750 Australian active and inactive users whose obsolete password data was involved", "36 Australian users who may have had plain text passwords exposed", and payment information for 135,288. A current password, the report explains, is "a password that was valid against Adobe's production authentication system". For most of the Australian users counted in the report, the password held in reversible form still opened their account.

How the passwords were stored is in the same report. The system "used Triple DES encryption to protect all password information stored". "The choice of a block cipher encryption algorithm meant that common passwords shared by different users had the same ciphertext representation." Adobe "also stored customer 'password hints' in the backup system in plain text rather than in an encrypted format". A Cambridge paper that used the breach as its motivating story names the mode: "Reversibly encrypted with 3DES, not salted and hashed as they should have been. To add the insult to the injury, the encryption was performed by treating each block independently, in ECB mode."

The Canadian report explains what that combination allowed, without publishing anything. "The plain text hints sometimes contained the password itself (or a very obvious hint)", and "the encryption format used by Adobe scrambled identical passwords in the same way". So it "would be relatively easy to obtain access to several different accounts where the same password was used by starting with the hint/password obtained from one account".

The Irish report records the result: "A number of research articles have demonstrated that some passwords have been deciphered by reference to password hints and repeated passwords". The Australian one: the encryption "together with plaintext password hints" let "security experts with access to the database" identify "the 100 most common passwords and customer accounts associated with those passwords". The database, it notes, "became widely available on the internet after the breach". No report says the encryption key was recovered, and none says the hints belonged to everyone; the Irish report puts them at "a small percentage of users".

Then the part the folk story skips. The Irish Office "queried why passwords were stored in one system in an encrypted manner rather that hashed and salted", and recorded the answer. Adobe "was actually hashing and salting passwords within a new system for a number of years prior to the discovery of the security breach". It had "decided to also keep the database in the old system as a backup measure in case of issues with the new system". "Passwords in the old system's database had been encrypted."

The Australian report dates it. Adobe "introduced a new system in April 2010 as a more secure means of authenticating users than the encrypted passwords stored in the backup system". For more than a year before the report, that system had "cryptographically hashed customer passwords using the SHA-256 algorithm, including salting the passwords and iterating the hash more than 1,000 times".

The Canadian investigators, working with the Irish, listed their concerns in one sentence: "password management, outdated software, redundant systems containing personal information, insecure and inadequate logging". The list continues: "retention of data for longer than necessary, and a failure to isolate corporate computer systems from the engineering network". Some of the systems, they noted, "had been inherited as a result of absorbing other companies", and "Aspects of the inherited systems and operations appear to have contributed to the security safeguard problems".

The findings. Ireland: the transition "from the use of encrypted passwords in the old system to the use of hashed and salted passwords in the new system" was one that "could have been achieved more effectively and expeditiously than was the case". Canada: the safeguards "were not appropriate for protecting the personal information at issue"; the complaint "well-founded and resolved".

Australia: Adobe "breached NPP 4 by failing to take reasonable steps to protect all of the personal information it held". The report adds a sentence written for this case. NPP 4 "will not be satisfied if an organisation has adequate security measures in place to protect personal information stored in one area of its systems". The failure is when it "does not implement these measures in relation to all of the personal information that it holds". Adobe reset passwords on 3 and 4 October for users whose current password data was in the database. Its announcement asked customers to change the same password anywhere else they had used it.

The mechanism

OWASP's category page says this weakness focuses on failures related to the lack of cryptography, insufficiently strong cryptography, leaking of cryptographic keys, and related errors. Among the questions it tells a reviewer to ask: Are any old or weak cryptographic algorithms or protocols used either by default or in older code? And: Is an insecure mode of operation such as ECB in use? Adobe's backup store answers both. But the failure that mattered is the one the first question hides in its last four words: older code.

Start with the whiteboard. A password store has one job: to let the server check a password without being able to produce it. Encryption cannot do that job, because encryption is reversible by design. Whoever holds the key can read every password, and the server must hold the key to check anything. A hash can. The server stores H(password), recomputes it at login, and compares. Nobody, including the server, can walk back from the stored value to the password except by guessing.

Adobe's old system encrypted. That alone is the first line of the finding. The second line is the mode. Triple DES is a block cipher: it turns each fixed-size block of input into a block of output under the key. In ECB mode every block is transformed independently, so the same input block under the same key gives the same output block every time. The Australian report says exactly this without the acronym: "common passwords shared by different users had the same ciphertext representation". Drawn on the board, the store looked like this:

user            ciphertext (3DES, ECB, one key)     hint (plain text)
alice@…         2fca9b003de39278                    "my dog"
bob@…           2fca9b003de39278                    "the usual"
carol@…         2fca9b003de39278                    "rex plus year"
dave@…          e2a311d0ff1c5a17                    (none)

Nothing above is real data; it is the shape. Three people with the same password share a row of ciphertext. Whoever reads the table can group users by ciphertext without knowing the key, and then read every hint in a group. One careless hint anywhere in the group gives the password for the whole group. That is the crossword: the clues were never encrypted, and the grid told you which clues shared an answer. The Canadian report describes precisely this, and the Irish report's "deciphered by reference to password hints and repeated passwords" is the same sentence from the other side. No decryption was needed and, on the record, none happened.

The fix, as a data structure, is two changes. The first is a salt: a random value stored per user and mixed into the hash, so that equal passwords no longer produce equal stored values. NIST's standard gives the reason in one clause: "to prevent multiple subscriber accounts from having the same hashed password". The second is a cost factor, so that each guess is slow. The same standard says the purpose is "to make each password guess more expensive for an attacker who has obtained a hashed password file", so that the cost of a guessing attack is "high or prohibitive".

OWASP's instruction is the same in one line: Store passwords using strong adaptive and salted hashing functions with a work factor (delay factor), such as Argon2, yescrypt, scrypt or PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-512. Adobe's new system had two relevant structural properties: salt and repeated work. Adobe described SHA-256 with more than a thousand iterations, but the report does not give enough construction detail to assess it against today's recommended schemes. The Cambridge paper adds the limit that survives every fix: with salting, "the attackers can't tell that thousands of people share that same password, nor which ones of them do. It is true, however, that the attackers can still try the most likely guesses that fit the policy."

Why is this easy to write and hard to see? Because encryption feels like protection, and for data that must be read back it is. Card numbers must be recovered to charge a card; passwords never need to be. A team that has encrypted one table will encrypt the next with the same library, and a store that decrypts correctly passes every test a store can pass. The hints were a product feature. The Adobe record does not say the old authentication code still served logins. It says credential data remained on a redundant backup system. That data was still reachable more than three years after the replacement system was introduced.

The defense

The defense in this chapter was in place before the attack. Adobe introduced the replacement authentication system in April 2010. By the later period described to the Australian Commissioner, it hashed and salted passwords and applied repeated work. On the available record, no report says the hashed store was taken. The Irish and Australian reports identify the compromised data as coming from the backup system. What failed was the decision to retain a database of reversibly encrypted, current passwords "in case of issues with the new system".

The Australian Commissioner's sentence is the rule: security "stored in one area of its systems" does not count if the same data sits unprotected in another. OWASP's prevention list has the same idea in plainer words: Don't store sensitive data unnecessarily. Discard it as soon as possible or use PCI DSS compliant tokenization or even truncation. Data that is not retained cannot be stolen. A migration is finished when the old store is gone, not when the new one works. The Irish finding, that the transition "could have been achieved more effectively and expeditiously", is a regulator saying the same thing in the register of a report.

What Adobe did afterward is on the record in the form regulators use. Australia lists the steps: "network monitoring, the storage of payment card information and passwords, two-factor authentication, decommissioning the affected server and abolishing the use of password hints". Canada lists "enhancements to both its user-password management process and its server authentication system". It also lists "the decommissioning of the server affected by the Data Breach and a reduction in the retention period for customers' payment data". Many other measures "are of a commercially sensitive nature and thus cannot be mentioned in this report". Ireland is "satisfied that it now has appropriate procedures in place".

Two of those steps are the whole defense against this attack: the server is gone, and the hints are gone. NIST now forbids the second outright: "Verifiers SHALL NOT permit the subscriber to store a hint (e.g., a reminder of how the password was created) that is accessible to an unauthenticated claimant."

Here the defense record is thin in the usual way. The regulators record that the affected server was decommissioned and password hints were abolished. None published a check that no other copy of the reversible database existed, and the Canadian report says many details are confidential. Whether another copy remained is open. Whether Adobe's later systems have held is not on the record either way.

The mirror is LinkedIn, sixteen months earlier, and it fell a different way. On 6 June 2012 LinkedIn "became aware" of "the theft of approximately 6.5 million passwords". Those were not encrypted. A later paper identifies them as "unsalted SHA-1 password hashes" and adds that "LinkedIn was not using key-stretching tools such as PBKDF2 or Argon2 at the time of the breach", so "the passwords were significantly easier to crack". LinkedIn's own statement of 12 June says it had "completed a long-planned transition from a password database system that hashed passwords, i.e. provided one layer of encoding, to a system that both hashes and salts the passwords".

In May 2016 the same 2012 data resurfaced, and LinkedIn's notice described it as "email addresses, hashed passwords, and LinkedIn member IDs" from 2012, "not a new security breach", now guarded by salted hashes and optional two-factor authentication. The paper puts the resurfaced count at "over 177.5+ million".

Set the two side by side. Adobe stored passwords reversibly, in a mode that leaked equality, beside plain-text hints, in a backup that should not have existed. LinkedIn stored them irreversibly but fast and unsalted, so equal passwords still matched and every guess was cheap. OWASP's own scenario covers the second case: The password database uses unsalted or simple hashes to store everyone's passwords. and Hashes generated by simple or fast hash functions may be cracked by GPUs, even if they were salted. Neither company lacked the knowledge. Adobe had built the right system and LinkedIn had planned the right transition. Both were caught holding the old store.

What you can now see

Find one migration in a system you run that everyone calls finished. A password store moved to a proper hash, a secrets system replaced, a card-number table tokenised. Then ask a single question of it: where is the old copy? Not the old code, the old data. A backup taken before the cut-over, a replica that was never torn down, a nightly export that still runs, a dump on a share "in case of issues with the new system".

If you can name the copy and the date it was destroyed, the migration is finished. If you can only name the new system, you have Adobe's finding: more than three years after the replacement arrived, a retained database still held current passwords in reversible form. That database is what was taken. Then look for the hint column, or its modern equivalent: any field stored in the clear that helps a reader guess the protected one. The record shows that the clue beside the ciphertext, not the cipher, is what turned a dump into a crossword.
