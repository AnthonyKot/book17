Chapter 0 · How the list is made

A Ranking of Tested Applications

Radware's 2025 explainer says the list highlights the most common and impactful vulnerabilities, enabling organizations to focus their security work. The edition itself does something narrower. Contributors supplied application-testing data. OWASP calculated incidence and testing coverage for individual weaknesses, then ranked categories with a five-term risk score. Eight places came from that data; two came from a practitioner survey. This chapter is written last because its job is to report what the other ten found: ten incidents, one per category, each set against a familiar retelling.

The break

Start with what the list says about itself. The 2025 edition is the 8th installment of the OWASP Top Ten. Its data came from organizations that, in its words, kindly donated data for over 2.8 millions applications to make this the largest and most comprehensive application security data set. Twelve contributors are named, among them Veracode, Sonar, Semgrep, Contrast Security and Bugcrowd, plus anonymous donors. The published source names the donors and total. It does not enumerate the applications, identify customers, or say that every donor tested each entry with its own tools.

The incidence calculation starts with applications, but it is performed for each CWE. For the incidence rate, we calculated the percentage of applications vulnerable to each CWE from the population tested by an org for a period of time. Frequency within one application is discarded: Whether an application has four instances of a CWE or 4,000 instances is not part of the calculation for the Top Ten. The reason given is honest about the tools: manual testers tend to list a weakness once, while automated frameworks list every instance. Counting instances would partly measure the tester rather than the application.

The vocabulary is CWE, the Common Weakness Enumeration. The dataset went from approximately 30 CWEs in 2017, to almost 400 CWEs in 2021, to 589 CWEs in this edition. The authors spent months grouping them, chose root causes over symptoms where they could, capped a category at 40 CWEs, and ended with 248 CWEs within the 10 categories out of 968 CWEs in the downloadable dictionary from MITRE. They also say why they stopped: We spent several months grouping and categorizing CWEs and could have continued for additional months. We had to stop at some point.

The ranking uses five terms, not a prevalence half and a severity half. Incidence is the share of a contributor's tested applications found vulnerable to one CWE. Coverage is the share tested for that CWE across all contributors. OWASP then applies this formula: (Max Incidence Rate % * 1000) + (Max Coverage % * 100) + (Avg Exploit * 10) + (Avg Impact * 20) + (Sum Occurrences / 10000) = Risk Score. The category score therefore uses maximum incidence, maximum coverage, two CVE-derived averages and total occurrences.

The two severity terms come from the CVE record. There are approximately 175k records (up from 125k in 2021) of CVEs mapped to CWEs in the National Vulnerability Database (NVD). Exploit and impact scores are averaged per CWE from CVSS versions 2 and 3. Version 4 no longer easily provides the Exploit or Impact scores.

Then the part most readers never hear. Eight of the ten seats come from the data. Two do not: We ranked 12 categories based on the data contributed, and allowed two to be promoted or highlighted by responses from the community survey. The reason is stated plainly: examining the contributed data is essentially looking into the past, and There are also important risks that we may never be able to reliably test and be present in the data. The 2025 text names which categories those are. Supply chain was overwhelmingly voted a top concern in the community survey and has the fewest occurrences in the data, but also the highest average exploit and impact scores from CVEs. Logging and alerting will always be underrepresented in the data, and was again voted into a position in the list from the community survey participants.

What moved in 2025 is on the record too. Misconfiguration moved up from #5 in 2021 to #2 in 2025. Cryptographic Failures falls two spots from #2 to #4; Injection falls two spots from #3 to #5; Insecure Design slides two spots from #4 to #6. Server-side request forgery, a category of its own in 2021, has been rolled into Broken Access Control. Supply Chain Failures is an expansion of A06:2021-Vulnerable and Outdated Components, and Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions is a new category for 2025. Radware's wording makes the rank sound like a work queue. OWASP instead says organizational exposure, threat actors and business impact are specific to each organization.

The mechanism

Here is the distinction the reader is meant to draw. The Introduction reports average CWE-incidence figures of 3.73% for Broken Access Control, 3.00% for Security Misconfiguration and 3.80% for Cryptographic Failures. Those are category averages, not the formula's maximum-incidence input. The Security Misconfiguration page makes the distinction visible: 100% of the applications tested were found to have some form of misconfiguration, with an average incidence rate of 3.00%. A category can touch every tested application while its average per-CWE incidence remains low. Rank is not prevalence.

The second thing to draw is the population. The public source gives its volume and contributors, not its composition. Its own words for one limit are: The results in the data are largely limited to what the industry can test for in an automated fashion. That is a constraint, not a claim that all input came from scanners; the methodology also discusses manual testing. The two survey seats address risks underrepresented in testing data. They do not make the tested population representative of your system.

Even the denominator is unsettled. OWASP calls the definition of an "application" a growing challenge as architectures and code repositories change the unit being counted. The published corpus cannot show whether a pipeline network, a compression library's release process, or a fleet of kernel sensors appeared in it. Those incidents can illuminate a category, but the dataset cannot supply their base rates.

A category also hides a range. The Introduction says of Injection that it includes a range of issues from Cross-site Scripting (high frequency/low impact) to SQL Injection (low frequency/high impact) vulnerabilities. One rank, two very different bugs: the common one that rarely matters and the rare one that emptied MOVEit. The category's prevalence figure is mostly the first; the category's place in the news is mostly the second. The authors describe their own stance as data-informed, but not blindly data-driven, and a reader should hold the list the same way.

The third thing is the category boundary. The authors call the overlap unavoidable: With the complexity of software engineering and software security, it’s basically impossible to create ten categories without some level of overlap. The chapters found the same. The widely repeated Capital One reconstruction routes through SSRF, but the primary record confirms only a firewall misconfiguration. MOVEit is injection, but the input arrived through session state. CrowdStrike is exceptional-condition handling, but the fault was an out-of-bounds read the category does not list. A category is a lens. Pointed at a real incident it shows one plane of the failure sharply and the others blurred.

So what can a ranking of tested applications say about your application? It can tell you which classes of weakness the tools found most often in the kinds of applications they test, weighted by how badly published bugs of that class have gone. It can tell you where the practitioners think the tools are blind. It cannot tell you your odds, your order of work, or whether the category you fear is the one that will get you. For that, the record of what happened to named systems is the better instrument, which is the reason this book exists.

The defense

The defense in this chapter is a method, and its record is the other ten chapters. Each took one incident in one category and did two things: set the folk story against the primary documents, row by row, and set the attack against a real defense with a record. The list itself makes one such argument in its own text. The logging category was renamed to emphasize the importance of the alerting functionality needed to induce appropriate action on relevant logging events. The reason given: Great logging with no alerting is of minimal value in identifying security incidents. Chapter 9 found the step after alerting was the one the record could not show.

Every chapter's ledger uses the same five verdicts. HOLDS: the record agrees. OVERSTATED: true in outline, wrong in a number, a date or a cause. WRONG: the record contradicts it. MISSING: the record shows something a defender needed that the retelling leaves out. OPEN: no public document settles it.

Across the ten chapters the ledgers hold 75 rows. Twenty-two hold. Twenty-five are overstated. Ten are missing something a defender needed. Eleven are open. Seven are wrong. Read as a distribution, the folk stories are mostly right in outline and mostly wrong in the detail that would have helped. The WRONG rows are rarely a wrong story; they are almost always one wrong word. "Encrypted" for hashed. "Ransomware" for a wiper. "Inactive" for an account that accepted a login. "Invalid certificates" for an unchecked signature. "Nine months" for nineteen. A reader who trusts the headline will not be misled about what happened. They will be misled about why.

Five MISSING rows expose a second layer hidden by the headline. Capital One's WAF misconfiguration sits beside regulator findings about controls, audit and governance. xz's version label hides that the trigger lived only in release tarballs. Adobe's salted replacement system sits beside the retained reversible backup. MOVEit's first CVE sits before four more injection CVEs patched over five weeks. CrowdStrike's triggering configuration update sits beside a five-month-old sensor defect and a control that could pin one but not the other. These rows do not name one weakness. They show why reviewing only the object named in the headline misses its operational twin.

The OPEN rows mark causal gaps at different stages, not one repeated first step. Capital One's SSRF route and Colonial's credential origin concern ingress. Apple's duplicated line concerns defect provenance. Target's gap comes after detection: the public record does not show receipt, disposition and follow-up for the reported FireEye alerts. Different documents would close them: trial exhibits, Mandiant's final findings, Apple's version history, and Target's SOC records.

The defense record divides five and five. Chapters 2, 3, 7, 8 and 9 contain a measured observation: exposed MongoDB data, sampled reproducible builds, account-hijacking attempts, Maersk continuity and Mandiant dwell time. Each has a stated scope, and none alone proves that the featured control caused the result. The measurements range from a bit-for-bit rebuild to an incident-response population.

Chapters 1, 4, 5, 6 and 10 instead document a design, adoption or code change without a before-and-after outcome tied to it. Those records cover IMDSv2, Adobe's decommissioned server, MOVEit's bound parameters, Strava's threshold and CrowdStrike's narrower checks. Some vendors assert results; none of these five chapters has the adoption measure or independent causal test needed to rank the fix. A ranking of defenses cannot be built by treating these two evidence types as equivalent.

The list's authors say the data looks into the past. The defenses' records mostly do not look anywhere. A ranking of weaknesses exists. A ranking of what fixed them does not, and this book could not build one from the public record.

What a reader should conclude from the list, then, is narrow and useful. Use the ten categories as ten questions to ask of a system you run, in the order that system's exposure suggests, not in the order of the list. Where a category's data is thin, the authors have told you; treat their survey seats as warnings that the tools are blind there. And when you meet a folk story about a famous breach in your category, expect it to be true in outline and to have dropped the twin: the backup, the tarball, the second default, the step after the alert. That is the finding of ten ledgers, and it is the only sense in which this chapter summarizes anything.

What you can now see

Take the list and find the category your own system's folk story lives in: the breach your team cites when it argues for a control. Then open that chapter's ledger and read its MISSING rows. Look for the second layer: wider controls beside one misconfiguration, a tarball beside a tree, a retained backup beside its replacement, later patches beside the first CVE, or trigger content beside dormant code.

Now ask which object in your system has an operational twin. The list's data is largely constrained by automatable testing, and two categories come from a survey. Its five-term score ranks categories; it does not inventory your copies, release artefacts or control paths. The list tells you where to begin looking. The record shows why you should look beside the named thing.
