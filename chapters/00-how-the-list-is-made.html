Chapter 0 · How the list is made

A Ranking of Tested Applications

The OWASP Top 10 is the most quoted document in application security, and it is quoted as a list of the ten most common vulnerabilities. It is not that. Its 2025 edition ranks ten categories of weakness across applications that twelve named contributors and several anonymous ones tested. The measure is the share of applications with at least one finding, weighted by exploit and impact scores from the CVE database. Two of the ten seats are filled by a practitioner survey instead of the data. This chapter is written last because its job is to report what the other ten found: ten incidents, one per category, each set against the story everyone tells. The pattern is that the stories are nearly always true and almost never enough.

The break

Start with what the list says about itself. The 2025 edition is the 8th installment of the OWASP Top Ten. Its data came from organisations that, in its words, kindly donated data for over 2.8 millions applications to make this the largest and most comprehensive application security data set. Twelve contributors are named, among them Veracode, Sonar, Semgrep, Contrast Security and Bugcrowd, plus anonymous donors. That sentence tells you the population. These are applications that tool vendors and testing firms scanned or tested for their customers. Your application is in the set only if one of them tested it.

The unit of measurement is the application, not the finding. We asked for the number of applications tested for a given year (starting in 2021), and the number of applications with at least one instance of a CWE found in testing. Frequency is discarded on purpose: Whether an application has four instances of a CWE or 4,000 instances is not part of the calculation for the Top Ten. The reason given is honest about the tools: manual testers list a weakness once, automated frameworks list every instance, and counting instances would measure the tester rather than the application.

The vocabulary is CWE, the Common Weakness Enumeration. The dataset went from approximately 30 CWEs in 2017, to almost 400 CWEs in 2021, to 589 CWEs in this edition. The authors spent months grouping them, chose root causes over symptoms where they could, capped a category at 40 CWEs, and ended with 248 CWEs within the 10 categories out of 968 CWEs in the downloadable dictionary from MITRE. They also say why they stopped: We spent several months grouping and categorizing CWEs and could have continued for additional months. We had to stop at some point.

Prevalence is half the ranking. The other half is severity, taken from the CVE record. There are approximately 175k records (up from 125k in 2021) of CVEs mapped to CWEs in the National Vulnerability Database (NVD). Exploit and impact scores are averaged per CWE from CVSS versions 2 and 3, because version 4 no longer easily provides the Exploit or Impact scores. So a category's rank combines how often tested applications had a weakness of that kind with how bad the published vulnerabilities of that kind have been.

Then the part most readers never hear. Eight of the ten seats come from the data. Two do not: We ranked 12 categories based on the data contributed, and allowed two to be promoted or highlighted by responses from the community survey. The reason is stated plainly: examining the contributed data is essentially looking into the past, and There are also important risks that we may never be able to reliably test and be present in the data. The 2025 text names which categories those are. Supply chain was overwhelmingly voted a top concern in the community survey and has the fewest occurrences in the data, but also the highest average exploit and impact scores from CVEs. Logging and alerting will always be underrepresented in the data, and was again voted into a position in the list from the community survey participants.

What moved in 2025 is on the record too. Misconfiguration moved up from #5 in 2021 to #2 in 2025. Cryptographic Failures falls two spots from #2 to #4; Injection falls two spots from #3 to #5; Insecure Design slides two spots from #4 to #6. Server-side request forgery, a category of its own in 2021, has been rolled into Broken Access Control. Supply Chain Failures is an expansion of A06:2021-Vulnerable and Outdated Components, and Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions is a new category for 2025. The folk story of the list is a league table: the top entry is the biggest problem, the bottom entry the smallest. The document describes something else, and says so.

The mechanism

Here is the arithmetic the reader is meant to draw. The Introduction gives three prevalence figures. Broken Access Control, ranked first: on average, 3.73% of applications tested had one or more of the 40 Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs) in this category. Security Misconfiguration, ranked second: 3.00% of the applications tested had one or more of the 16 CWEs in this category. Cryptographic Failures, ranked fourth: on average, 3.80% of applications have one or more of the 32 CWEs in this category. The category in fourth place is more prevalent than the category in first. Rank is not prevalence. It is prevalence weighted by severity, adjusted by a survey, across a population you did not choose.

The second thing to draw is the population. Every application in the 2.8 million was tested by a contributor, with the contributor's tools, for the contributor's customers. The document's own words for the limit: The results in the data are largely limited to what the industry can test for in an automated fashion. A weakness that no scanner finds is absent from the data however common it is. The two survey seats exist to patch that, and the authors say which seats they are. What they cannot patch is the base rate: a percentage of tested applications is not a probability for yours. If your system is a pipeline control network, a compression library's release process, or a fleet of kernel sensors, it was not in the population, and this book's incidents say so where it matters.

A category also hides a range. The Introduction says of Injection that it includes a range of issues from Cross-site Scripting (high frequency/low impact) to SQL Injection (low frequency/high impact) vulnerabilities. One rank, two very different bugs: the common one that rarely matters and the rare one that emptied MOVEit. The category's prevalence figure is mostly the first; the category's place in the news is mostly the second. The authors describe their own stance as data-informed, but not blindly data-driven, and a reader should hold the list the same way.

The third thing is the category boundary. The authors call the overlap unavoidable: With the complexity of software engineering and software security, it’s basically impossible to create ten categories without some level of overlap. The chapters found the same. Capital One is access control, but the route was a request forgery. MOVEit is injection, but the input arrived through session state. CrowdStrike is exceptional-condition handling, but the fault was an out-of-bounds read the category does not list. A category is a lens. Pointed at a real incident it shows one plane of the failure sharply and the others blurred.

So what can a ranking of tested applications say about your application? It can tell you which classes of weakness the tools found most often in the kinds of applications they test, weighted by how badly published bugs of that class have gone. It can tell you where the practitioners think the tools are blind. It cannot tell you your odds, your order of work, or whether the category you fear is the one that will get you. For that, the record of what happened to named systems is the better instrument, which is the reason this book exists.

The defense

The defense in this chapter is a method, and its record is the other ten chapters. Each took one incident in one category and did two things: set the folk story against the primary documents, row by row, and set the attack against a real defense with a record. The list itself makes one such argument in its own text. The logging category was renamed to emphasize the importance of the alerting functionality needed to induce appropriate action on relevant logging events. The reason given: Great logging with no alerting is of minimal value in identifying security incidents. Chapter 9 found the step after alerting was the one the record could not show.

Every chapter's ledger uses the same five verdicts. HOLDS: the record agrees. OVERSTATED: true in outline, wrong in a number, a date or a cause. WRONG: the record contradicts it. MISSING: the record shows something a defender needed that the retelling leaves out. OPEN: no public document settles it.

Across the ten chapters the ledgers hold 75 rows. Twenty-two hold. Twenty-five are overstated. Ten are missing something a defender needed. Eleven are open. Seven are wrong. Read as a distribution, the folk stories are mostly right in outline and mostly wrong in the detail that would have helped. The WRONG rows are rarely a wrong story; they are almost always one wrong word. "Encrypted" for hashed. "Ransomware" for a wiper. "Inactive" for an account that accepted a login. "Invalid certificates" for an unchecked signature. "Nine months" for nineteen. A reader who trusts the headline will not be misled about what happened. They will be misled about why.

The MISSING rows are where the defender's lesson lived, and they rhyme. The thing the folk story left out was, five times in ten, a second copy or a second place. Adobe's replacement system was running; the old reversible store was kept as a backup. Maersk's controllers were synchronised peers; none was a time-separated copy. xz's trigger was never in git; it was in the release tarball. MongoDB's safe default lived in a package file; the binary said otherwise. Target's own account has intruder activity detected, logged, surfaced and evaluated; the retelling stops at the vendor's door and at "ignored". In each case the reviewed thing was fine and the unreviewed twin was the failure. No category on the list names that, because it is not a weakness in code. It is a weakness in inventory.

The OPEN rows rhyme too. What the record could not settle was, repeatedly, the first step: how the Capital One firewall was made to fetch credentials, where the Colonial password came from, why the line entered Apple's tree, what Target's evaluators decided. Court documents, regulators and post-mortems describe what a system did and what it held. They describe how an attacker began only when a trial forced it into the record. The most repeated sentence in security writing, the one that begins "the attackers got in through", is the one the primary documents most often do not contain.

The defense side of the ten chapters has its own pattern, and it is the least comfortable. Every chapter found a real defense: IMDSv2, a localhost default, source-versus-tarball comparison, decommissioning a backup, bound parameters, a threshold on an aggregate, hardware keys, an offline copy, alerting that reaches a person, a fail-closed exit. In almost every chapter the defense's record is a design and a rationale with no published outcome. Amazon published the mechanism of IMDSv2 and no count of accounts that adopted it. MongoDB published a default and no count of installs that kept it. CrowdStrike published fixes and commissioned reviews whose findings were not public on the research date. Strava published a threshold and no before-and-after map.

The list's authors say the data looks into the past. The defenses' records mostly do not look anywhere. A ranking of weaknesses exists. A ranking of what fixed them does not, and this book could not build one from the public record.

What a reader should conclude from the list, then, is narrow and useful. Use the ten categories as ten questions to ask of a system you run, in the order that system's exposure suggests, not in the order of the list. Where a category's data is thin, the authors have told you; treat their survey seats as warnings that the tools are blind there. And when you meet a folk story about a famous breach in your category, expect it to be true in outline and to have dropped the twin: the backup, the tarball, the second default, the step after the alert. That is the finding of ten ledgers, and it is the only sense in which this chapter summarises anything.

What you can now see

Take the list and find the category your own system's folk story lives in: the breach your team cites when it argues for a control. Then open that chapter's ledger and read only the MISSING rows. Each names a thing the famous story left out that a defender needed. A backup that still held the secret. A tarball that was not the tree. A default that lived in one install path. An account that was believed disabled. An alert that was evaluated and then went nowhere.

Now ask which of those your system has a twin of. Not the weakness the category names; the unreviewed copy beside the reviewed thing. That question is not on the OWASP list, because the list ranks weaknesses in code and configuration that tools can find. Ten ledgers say the failure was as often in what nobody counted. The list tells you where to look. The record tells you what to look for.
