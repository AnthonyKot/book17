# Chapter 06 — pitches (standard mode: the user picks; nothing is drafted yet)

Sources for every quoted phrase below are indexed in `resources/sources/06/SOURCES.md` and saved beside it. §6 contract: no bug anywhere; the heatmap as designed, what it revealed, Strava’s response, threat modeling as the defense, and why no scanner could find the flaw. Two caveats carry into every pitch: the senators’ letter states concerns and asks questions rather than finding facts, and Strava and DOD document adopted heatmap/geolocation changes but publish no outcome measure for them; the USENIX paper measures an adjacent Privacy Zone defense, not proof that sensitive bases stopped appearing.

## A. The map did exactly what it was built to do

Anchor: Strava Engineering’s November 2017 design account—“the heatmap only contains public activities and respects all privacy settings”, draws each activity as a “pixel-perfect path”, and uses normalization that “causes the heatmap to convey maximum information about relative heat values”—read beside Strava’s January response that sparse regions “inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations.” Folk claim tested: the Guardian headline, “Fitness tracking app Strava gives away location of secret US army bases”; the record supports disclosure of sensitive locations but the headline omits the mechanism that matters to a defender: individually public, correctly processed runs became a conspicuous site-level signal when aggregated and maximally contrasted, without a bypass, exploit, or broken implementation. Defense: OWASP’s “Use threat modeling for critical parts of the application” set against Strava’s documented low-activity threshold and monthly privacy refresh, and DOD’s later requirement for a “threat-based comprehensive Operations Security (OPSEC) survey” before authorizing non-government devices; those records prove adoption, not outcome, so the chapter would say the defense record is thin. Takeaway: before publishing an aggregate, ask what a hostile reader can infer from absence, sparsity, and contrast—questions a scanner cannot answer because every line of code may be working correctly.

## B. Anonymized people, identified places

Anchor: Hassan et al.’s USENIX paper separates two claims the folk story fuses: “the identities of specific athletes were not directly leaked in the heatmap”, yet “as a result of the sparsity of background noise in some regions” the aggregate exposed sensitive locations; Strava’s engineering post supplies the mechanism in its local, maximum-contrast normalization. Folk claim tested: Wikipedia says “this map had mapped military bases”; that HOLDS at the place layer but is MISSING the consequential distinction between identifying a site from an aggregate and identifying a person through a related Strava surface. Defense: OWASP’s threat-modeling line set against Strava’s post-incident rule that low-activity roads and trails do not show heat until several athletes contribute, plus the paper’s narrower evidence that tested privacy mechanisms were put into production; the paper does not measure the heatmap rule, so no success claim transfers across. Takeaway: anonymization can protect the row and still expose the group or place—the design review must name every inference worth protecting, while a scanner can only inspect the implementation it was given.

## C. “Strava did nothing wrong”

Anchor: GAO-17-668, published before the heatmap update, says DOD’s own 2016 work had already described how aggregated IoT information could inform adversaries about deployments, while the “threat-based comprehensive operations security surveys” that could examine such risks “are not being conducted”; Strava later said it understood “our responsibility related to the data you share with us” and would review the motivating features themselves. Folk claim tested: *Data Science in Context* says, “Strava did nothing wrong; they displayed anonymized tracks for opted-in users”; anonymized tracks HOLDS, but “opted-in users” is OVERSTATED as a description of consent because Strava describes an “ability to opt out of heatmaps altogether” and the senators describe “opt-out provisions” that consumers may not understand, while the absolution omits the design responsibility Strava accepted and DOD’s unperformed survey. Defense: OWASP’s instruction to “look for changes in data flows” set against the August DOD memo, which made a threat-based survey the condition for authorizing non-government geolocation in operational areas, while Strava simplified controls and reviewed feature abuse; implementation is documented, effectiveness is not. Takeaway: threat models belong at the seam between systems—a default-valid user choice in one product can still disclose an employer’s asset—so neither a code scanner nor a privacy toggle can settle the design question alone.

## D. The Pentagon did not ban the watch — CHOSEN

Anchor: the August 2018 DOD memorandum prohibits “geolocation features and functionality” in designated operational areas, permits non-government use after a threat-based OPSEC survey, permits government-issued use for mission necessity with OPSEC risk considered, and calls for risk-based treatment elsewhere. Folk claim tested: Ars Technica compresses that design into “Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones”; the shorthand HOLDS on the immediate behavior but is MISSING the memo’s boundaries, exceptions, and explicit risk decision, just as the common Strava-leaked-bases shorthand hides Strava’s documented response and the map’s aggregation mechanism. Defense: this is OWASP’s “Use threat modeling for critical parts of the application” translated into an operational control, with GAO documenting that threat-based surveys were not being conducted and the memo showing what DOD adopted; neither document reports whether the control reduced disclosure. Takeaway: secure design is not always a product patch—sometimes it is deciding where a useful capability is forbidden, where it is allowed, and who must model the threat before making the exception.

## Recommendation

Pick **A**. It is the only pitch anchored in the pre-incident design document itself, so it can prove §6’s no-bug-anywhere premise, put the aggregation-and-contrast mechanism on a whiteboard, then carry Strava’s response and the threat-model defense without changing subjects. It also supports an honest ledger: the opted-in-users claim is OVERSTATED as consent against Strava’s opt-out wording and the senators’ stated concern, while MISSING rows cover the headline’s omitted public inputs and design amplification, the site-versus-person distinction, DOD’s pre-existing warning, and the later policy’s scope and exceptions. **B** offers the sharpest lesson about anonymization, but it would cost the clean no-bug line by pulling the adjacent Privacy Zone vulnerability toward center stage; its site-versus-person distinction folds into A as a ledger row. **C** gives the strongest institutional-warning row, but it would cost Strava’s design account the narrative center and turn much of the chapter into DOD governance; GAO’s aggregation warning and unperformed surveys fold into A as one MISSING row and the defense setup. **D** gives threat modeling its most concrete operational form, but it would cost the product mechanism and compress Strava’s response; the memo’s feature-versus-device scope and exceptions fold into A as a defense paragraph and ledger row. No alternative supplies a measured heatmap-specific success, so changing pitches does not remove the defense-evidence caveat.
