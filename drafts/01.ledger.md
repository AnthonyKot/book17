| 1 | "The type of vulnerability exploited … is a well-known method called a 'Server Side Request Forgery' (SSRF) attack … trick the firewall into relaying requests to … the 'metadata' service" (Krebs, 2 Aug 2019, from "a source with direct knowledge of the breach investigation"). | Complaint ¶10 (p. 6): "A firewall misconfiguration permitted commands to reach and be executed by that server"; ¶13 (p. 7): "unusual communications through the misconfigured firewall to the server." The trial exhibits (2:19-cr-00159, June 2022) would settle it. |
| 2 | An ex-Amazon engineer used insider knowledge or access: "who had previously worked as a software engineer for Amazon Web Services" (Wikipedia lede); "what is often referred to as insider threats" (researcher quoted by Krebs, 30 Jul 2019). | Complaint ¶15 (p. 9): resume says systems engineer at the Cloud Computing Company 2015–16. Ninth Circuit p. 5: "Over two years after her employment at Amazon ended, Thompson began hacking AWS customers' accounts." The credentials came from Capital One's own server via the misconfigured firewall (¶¶10–11), not from any employer access. Krebs himself, 2 Aug: "the methods she deployed have been well understood for years." | OVERSTATED — ex-employee, yes; insider attack, no document says so. |
| 3 | "The Capital One hack" — a targeted attack on one bank (headline framing; Wikipedia section title "July 2019 data breach"). | DOJ indictment release: "created scanning software that allowed her to identify customers of a cloud computing company who had misconfigured their firewalls"; "more than 30 other entities." Ninth Circuit p. 5–6: "scanned millions of publicly available IP addresses associated with AWS"; "credentials from at least 200 entities and stole data from at least 30 of them." | MISSING — Capital One was found by a scan for a class of misconfiguration, not chosen. A defender needs to know the scan is running against them too. |
| 4 | "some of its leaked data was being stored out in the open on the software development platform Github" (Krebs, 30 Jul 2019). | Complaint ¶¶10–12 (pp. 6–7): the April 21 File "contained the IP address for a specific server", "code for three commands, as well as a list of more than 700 folders or buckets of data." The customer data is placed on Thompson's devices (¶27) and in her own Slack claims (¶18), not in the gist. The tipster's subject line was "Leaked s3 data" (¶9). | OVERSTATED — what was on GitHub was the recipe and the inventory, not the records. That difference is why Capital One could confirm the intrusion in two days: it re-ran the commands (¶12). |
| 5 | The WAF role "was allowed to list all of the files in any buckets of data, and to read the contents of each of those files" (Krebs, 2 Aug 2019). | Complaint ¶11 (p. 6): List Buckets listed "the names of folders or buckets of data in Capital One's storage space" (700+, ¶12); the Sync Command copied "from those folders or buckets … for which the *****-WAF-Role account had the requisite permissions." ¶13: the role "does not, in the ordinary course of business, invoke the List Buckets Command." | OVERSTATED — list-all is what List Buckets does for any principal allowed to call it; read-all is not what the complaint says. The record does not say how many of the 700 the role could read, only that they included credit card application data. |
| 6 | "The intrusion occurred through a misconfigured web application firewall that enabled access to the data" (DOJ arrest release, 29 Jul 2019; repeated as the whole cause in Wikipedia and most retellings). | OCC Consent Order #2020-036, Art. II ¶1: "In or around 2015, the Bank failed to establish effective risk assessment processes prior to migrating its information technology operations to the cloud operating environment … including appropriate design and implementation of certain network security controls, adequate data loss prevention controls, and effective dispositioning of alerts"; ¶2 internal audit "failed to identify numerous control weaknesses"; ¶3 the Board "failed to take effective actions to hold management accountable." Complaint ¶13: the role ran List Buckets on 22 March outside "the ordinary course of business"; notice came from a stranger's e-mail on 17 July (¶9). | MISSING — one firewall setting is the proximate cause; the regulator's findings are about a role that could read application data, alerts that were not dispositioned, and an audit that did not report gaps. The four-month gap between the anomalous call and the tip is in the complaint itself. |
| 7 | "about 140,000 Social Security numbers … 80,000 bank account numbers" and "106 million people" (Wikipedia; Capital One statement). | Complaint ¶14 (p. 8), sworn 27 Jul: "approximately 120,000 Social Security Numbers and approximately 77,000 bank account numbers." Ninth Circuit p. 6: "PII of 98 million Americans." | HOLDS — as an order of magnitude. The count moved three times inside the record, which is what counts do; nothing turns on it. |
| 8 | "It was ultimately determined there was no evidence the data was shared by Thompson … She was sentenced to time served and five years of probation" (Wikipedia). | DOJ indictment release: "Investigators have found no evidence that THOMPSON sold or disseminated any of the information she accessed."
