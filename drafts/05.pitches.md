# Chapter 05 — pitches (standard mode: user picks; nothing drafted)

Sources for every number below are in `resources/sources/05/SOURCES.md`. Nothing here rides on

## A. One function, three patches — CHOSEN
Anchor: Rapid7's patch diff of CVE-2023-34362 (`UserGetUsersWithEmailAddress()` rebuilt from
`ConcatenateObject` string-building to `SQLBasicBuilder` with bound parameters; the header-to-session
function `SetAllSessionVarsFromHeaders()` deleted), read against Progress's 8-K of 5 June 2023 and
its own update log (9 June: third-party code review finds a second SQLi; 15 June: a third; 5 July:
"three new CVEs"). Folk claim tested: "a zero-day SQL injection in a file-transfer tool" — HOLDS as a
label and hides the two things a defender needs: the injected value did not arrive in a form field but
in a session variable the attacker could set through a header-parsing mismatch, and the pre-patch code
*did* escape the address for one clause and passed it raw for another (Rapid7: "in every case the
application correctly escapes the arguments" — until it didn't). Defense: parameterised queries as the
diff shows them being adopted, then the CISA/FBI Secure-by-Design alert of March 2024 (prepared
statements since 2004, "unforgivable" since 2007, "a simple code review would reveal") as the honest
answer to why a 2023 product shipped without them — and the fact that one commissioned review found
two more within a fortnight. Takeaway: escaping is a per-call-site promise and one missed call site is
the whole breach; a query builder is a per-codebase promise, and the fix was exactly that swap.

## B. The 60 million who never ran MOVEit
Anchor: the Emsisoft and KonBriefing tallies read *with* their own method notes (KonBriefing: "not
the total number of people affected, but the total number of people records"; Emsisoft: overlap "we
have no way to account for"), TechTarget's "1,690 of the 2,098 known victim organizations were
compromised via third parties", and the Genworth 8-K ("the Company does not itself use the MOVEit
file transfer system") beside the Maximus 8-K ("at least 8 to 11 million"). Folk claim tested: "Cl0p
hacked 2,000 organisations and 60 million people" — OVERSTATED on people (records, double-counted)
and MISSING on structure: roughly four in five named victims were downstream of a vendor such as PBI,
and Rapid7/Huntress put the directly exposed population at ~2,500 servers. Defense: thin on injection;
the real lesson is data minimisation at the interface, which KonBriefing itself says. Takeaway: a
victim count is a count of notifications, and most of the notified never had the vulnerable product.

## C. Alleged, subpoenaed, not found
Anchor: the JPML transfer order (4 Oct 2023: 91 actions in 22 districts, "over 55 million people"
estimated), Progress's 10-Q and 10-K (58 → ~118 class actions; SEC subpoena 2 Oct 2023; FY2023 cost
$1.5M net of $3.7M insurance against Maximus's $15M), MDL Order 22 of 31 July 2025 ("the allegation of
a persistent SQL injection vulnerability… clears that hurdle at the pleading stage"; Ch. 93A count
survives) and the 8-K of 7 Aug 2024 (SEC declines enforcement). Folk claim tested: "Progress shipped
an unforgivable bug and walked away" — half WRONG (118 suits survive dismissal; DC AG investigation
open) and half OPEN (no court has found a fact; the document that would settle fault is a judgment
that does not yet exist). Defense: the only defense with an institutional owner in the record is
CISA's demand that "senior executives… mount a formal review of their code". Takeaway: how to read a
breach through filings in the order they were made, and what "survived a motion to dismiss" does and
does not mean. Cost: like 01-D, a chapter about courts, and the mechanism section would be thin.

## D. Four days, and the day before
Anchor: CISA AA23-158A and Mandiant ("Beginning on May 27, 2023"; "data theft… within minutes of
the deployment of web shells"; "seemingly opportunistic"), Progress's 8-K (support call evening of
28 May, patch 31 May, "all within 48 hours") and its 10-Q ("does not have any on-going telemetry…
whether the customer has applied any security patches"). Folk claim tested: "zero-day — nothing could
have been done" — HOLDS on the timeline and is therefore the point: most of the data was gone before
the advisory existed, so patch speed, firewalling and detection were not defenses in this incident;
only the absence of the bug class was. Defense: parameterised queries as the one defense that works
before day zero, with the OWASP A05 numbers (SQLi "low frequency/high impact", >14k CVEs) and CWE-89's
standing Top-25 place as the measure of how often that defense is skipped. Takeaway: for an
internet-facing product you did not write, the question is not "how fast do we patch" but "which
classes of bug does the vendor's build system make impossible".

## Recommendation
Pick **A**. It is the only pitch that meets the whole §6 contract on its own: the CVE and the vendor
advisory (from the record, once the advisory text is saved by hand), the Cl0p campaign as the
regulators reported it (AA23-158A folded into the break section), parameterised queries as a defense
with a *visible* record — the patch diff is the nearest thing this chapter has to a commit — and the
"why did it ship" question answered from a regulator's document and from Progress's own admission that
one outside review found two more. It also carries the strongest MISSING rows (session-variable input
route; escape-one-clause-not-the-other) and passes the whiteboard test in a way no other pitch does.
B and D fold into A as ledger rows (victim-count method; the 27–31 May timeline) and a paragraph each.
Choosing **B** instead would cost the mechanism: it is a chapter about supply chains, which is chapter
3's job, and its defense record is one analyst's aside. Choosing **C** would cost the same plus fairness
risk: every fact about Progress's code in Order 22 is a plaintiff's allegation, so the ledger would be
mostly OPEN. Choosing **D** would cost the diff — it argues the defense from the timeline rather than
from the code, so the reader learns *that* parameterisation matters but not what the missing
parameterisation looked like.
