| 1 | "Hackers Breached Colonial Pipeline Using Compromised Password" (Bloomberg headline, 4 Jun 2021, as reported by CNN the same day); Senator Peters's opening: "Recent news reports indicate that hackers accessed the Colonial system through a compromised password" (Senate transcript l. 228–246) | Carmakal, House Q&A (l. 1420–1452): "let me, you know, clarify it as a misconfiguration ... there was a legacy VPN profile that was in place that wasn't believed to be active, and that enabled an attacker to leverage both the user and the password to login." | 2 | "suffered a ransomware cyberattack that afflicted computerized equipment managing the pipeline" (Wikipedia lede) | CISA/FBI AA21-131A (Summary, Technical Details): ransomware was deployed "against the pipeline company's information technology (IT) network"; "no indication that the entity's operational technology (OT) networks have been directly affected"; the company "proactively disconnected certain OT systems". Blount, Senate oral (l. 364–376): "that attack encrypted our IT systems". GAO-21-105263 (p. 1): ransomware "against Colonial Pipeline's business systems. The record places the malware on the IT side and the shutdown on Colonial's decision. | WRONG |
| 3 | "an inactive virtual private network (VPN) account" (Wikipedia §Background); "an unprotected VPN account that was no longer in use" (Chairman Thompson, House l. 134–142); "a disused virtual private networking account" (CNN) | Carmakal, House (l. 1668–1712): MFA "was not required for the specific VPN profile that was used for this specific account. It is because the account and the VPN profile wasn't believed to actually be enabled." Carmakal, prepared statement (l. 1192–1215): on 29 April 2021 "the threat actor had logged into a virtual private network (VPN) appliance using a legacy VPN profile". Blount, Senate (l. 1118–1140): a VPN "that we could not see and it did not show up in any pen testing". The profile was not inactive; it was believed inactive, and it accepted a login. The folk story leaves out that "inactive" was an unverified belief, which is the defender's whole problem. | MISSING |
| 4 | "started from a breached employee personal password likely found on the dark web" (Wikipedia §Background); "part of a batch of leaked passwords found on the dark web, according to Bloomberg's interview with Carmakal" (CNN) | Carmakal, House (l. 1420–1452): "We did not figure out exactly how the attacker was able to get access to the username, but it is a possibility that the attacker was able to leverage credentials that the employee may have used on another website"; (l. 1668–1712): "we did find that it was one of the passwords that was stolen from another website. But we don't know exactly where it came from." Blount, written statement §V: "We are still trying to determine how the attackers gained the needed credentials". Would settle it: Mandiant's final report, which Blount offered to share with the committee (Senate l. 1692–1702); not in the public record fetched September 2026. | OPEN |
| 5 | Colonial "did not have two-step authentication in place" (Senator Hassan's summary to Blount, Senate l. 1118–1140); Senator Peters: "This account did not use multifactor authentication ... We will talk more about that and why they did not" (l. 228–246) | Blount, Senate (l. 674–700): "in the case of this particular legacy VPN, it did only have single-factor authentication ... But in our normal operation, we use an RSA token allowance in order to create authentication difficulties for remote access." Carmakal (House l. 1668–1712): MFA "was not required for the specific VPN profile". True of one profile; the company's normal remote access carried a token. | OVERSTATED |
| 6 | "For nearly a week, 5,500 miles of pipeline ... was shut down" (Chairman Thompson, House l. 134–142); Wikipedia lede: "halted all pipeline operations to contain the attack" | Blount, written statement §II: ransom note found "just before 5:00 AM Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) on Friday, May 7th"; the Operations Supervisor "put in the stop work order"; "At approximately 5:55 AM EDT, employees began the shutdown process. By 6:10 AM EDT, they confirmed that all 5,500 miles of pipelines had been shut down"; restart "Wednesday evening, May 12" (§IV). Five and a half days, by Colonial's own order, "to help ensure the malware did not spread to the Operational Technology (OT) network". | HOLDS |
| 7 | "the FBI has since recovered most of it" (Chairman Thompson, House l. 134–142); "recovered 63.7 of the bitcoins (about 84% of the original payment)" (Wikipedia lede) | DOJ press release, 7 Jun 2021: "seized 63.7 bitcoins currently valued at approximately $2.3 million" of a ransom "for approximately 75 bitcoins"; "As alleged in the supporting affidavit ... a specific address, for which the FBI has the 'private key'". Peters, Senate (l. 534–546): "you have affirmed today that you made the decision to pay the ransom of $4.4 million". Most of the coins by count and about half the dollars ($2.3 million against $4.4 million), under a seizure warrant whose affidavit is not public; the release does not say how the key was obtained. | OVERSTATED |
| 8 | "Critical infrastructure and voluntary compliance has been the approach" (Senator Portman, Senate l. 674–700); pitch C's folk form: pipeline cybersecurity was unregulated until Colonial | GAO-21-105263 (p. 1): "Prior to issuing a cybersecurity directive in May 2021, TSA's efforts included issuing voluntary security guidelines and security reviews"; 15 recommendations from 2018–2019, 12 addressed. 86 FR 52953 §I.A–B: SD Pipeline-2021-01 issued 26 May 2021 (report within 12 hours, coordinator, self-assessment); SD-02 issued 19 July 2021 (mitigation measures, contingency plan, annual third-party review). SD-02E (2024 renewal) §III.C.2: "Multi-factor authentication, or other logical and physical security controls that supplement password authentication to provide risk mitigation commensurate to multi-factor authentication". Blount on the pre-attack TSA review (Senate l. 1508–1520): "It is a questionnaire format type thing"; it "probably would not have resulted in finding that legacy VPN". Voluntary before, mandated after, with a compensating-controls clause. | HOLDS |
| 9 | "Google has not had any of its 85,000+ employees successfully phished on their work-related accounts since early 2017" (Krebs, 23 Jul 2018, lede; headline "Security Keys Neutralized Employee Phishing") | Same article, the spokesperson's words: "We have had no reported or confirmed account takeovers since implementing security keys at Google" — a statement to a journalist, with the qualifier "reported or confirmed", and no Google publication behind it. The published measurement is Doerfler et al., WWW '19, Table 3: Security Key 100% ± 25% (bots), 100% ± 28% (phishing), no figure for targeted attacks; Google's blog (17 May 2019): "zero users that exclusively use security keys fell victim to targeted phishing during our investigation" — consumer accounts, not staff. Lang et al. 2016 report the design and a two-year deployment "which began within Google", not a takeover count. | OVERSTATED |
| 10 | "even if thieves manage to phish or steal your password, they still cannot log in to your account unless they also hack or possess that second factor" (Krebs, 2018, explaining 2FA) | Uber, "Security update", 16/19 Sep 2022: the attacker "purchased the contractor's Uber corporate password on the dark web", then "repeatedly tried to log in ... Each time, the contractor received a two-factor login approval request, which initially blocked access. Eventually, however, the contractor accepted one, and the attacker successfully logged in." The second factor was possessed by the right person and used for the wrong login. Doerfler et al. §Table 1 discussion: OTP apps "are nevertheless vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks such as inline phishing ... something U2F security keys solve". | OVERSTATED |
