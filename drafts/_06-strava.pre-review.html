Chapter 6 · A06:2025 Insecure Design

The Pentagon Did Not Ban the Watch

In January 2018, an Australian security analyst looking at an online fitness map noticed something strange in the Syrian desert. Where satellite photography showed bare sand, a bright geometric circuit traced the perimeter of an undisclosed military outpost. The app had not suffered a SQL injection, an authentication bypass, or a data breach. Every server had executed its code precisely as instructed. The folk story said Strava gave away secret army bases, and that the Pentagon responded by banning fitness trackers from combat zones. Both stories are clean, dramatic, and wrong. The Pentagon did not ban the watch, and Strava did not have a software bug. The vulnerability was in the architecture itself.

The break

On 1 November 2017, Strava Engineering published an account of what it called the largest and richest dataset of its kind: an updated Global Heatmap visualizing two years of trailing exercise data from its worldwide user base. Writing on the company engineering blog, Drew Robb described the technical scale of the accomplishment. The dataset comprised 700 million activities, 1.4 trillion latitude and longitude points, 7.7 trillion rasterized pixels, and 5 terabytes of raw input data. The total recorded distance spanned 16 billion kilometers, capturing more than 100,000 years of human physical exertion. The pipeline was designed to show athletes where their peers were running and cycling, turning individual GPS tracks into a collective map of human recreation across the planet.

From an engineering perspective, the data pipeline had been constructed with careful hygiene. Robb documented that raw activity streams were drawn from a warehouse and processed through automated cleanup filters. Most importantly, Robb noted, the heatmap only contained public activities and respected all user privacy settings. Speeds were filtered to remove anomalies: running activities at implausible speeds were excluded from the running heat layer, and cycling tracks were subjected to a ceiling to discard records recorded by cars and airplanes. The software contained no leaked credentials, no unsecured cloud buckets, and no broken access controls. Every single GPS point accepted into the rendering pipeline came from a user account configured to share public activities.

The system functioned quietly for nearly three months until 27 January 2018, when Nathan Ruser, a twenty-year-old Australian student studying international security, noticed the visualization while reviewing open-source intelligence. In major cities, Strava's map glowed like a solid sheet of neon. In western Europe and North America, millions of commutes and weekend runs blended together into a dense topography of everyday life. But when Ruser panned over sparsely populated combat theaters, the visual context vanished. In the deserts of eastern Syria, the mountains of Afghanistan, and remote stretches of the Sahel, commercial fitness trackers were practically nonexistent among the local population.

Against that dark canvas, small clusters of foreign military personnel wearing Fitbits, Garmin watches, and smartphones burned brightly. By zooming into isolated coordinates, researchers and journalists could trace the exact perimeters of known and undisclosed forward operating bases. The glowing loops mapped out supply roads, jogging paths around barbed-wire fences, watchtower routes, and internal living quarters. A runner circling a compound every morning at dawn produced a clean, bright oval in the empty desert. The map even illuminated patrol patterns along supply corridors outside base walls, showing where convoys traveled and how frequently troops ventured along specific dirt roads.

The headlines were immediate and sensational. On 28 January 2018, The Guardian ran the story under the headline "Fitness tracking app Strava gives away location of secret US army bases". Wikipedia quickly recorded the consensus folk summary. It reported that an Australian student discovered the map had charted military bases in Syria, Afghanistan, and the naval base at HMNB Clyde. In popular retellings, the incident was frequently dismissed as an unforced user error. Authors David Baumer et al. later wrote in Data Science in Context that "Strava did nothing wrong; they displayed anonymized tracks for opted-in users who hadn't realized this compromised military security."

That folk absolution was too simple. Strava had not used an affirmative opt-in model. In an open letter to the community on 29 January 2018, Strava CEO James Quarles acknowledged the gravity of the exposure. Quarles wrote that while the company respected privacy selections, including an existing ability to opt out of heatmaps altogether, Strava learned that members in the military and humanitarian workers living abroad had shared locations in areas without other activity density. In doing so, Quarles admitted, they had "inadvertently increased awareness of sensitive locations". He rejected the idea that the platform bore no responsibility. He pledged to review motivating features to ensure they could not be abused by people with bad intent.

The regulatory and political reaction pushed deeper into the architecture. On 14 February 2018, United States Senators Chris Coons and Jeff Flake sent a formal inquiry to Quarles pressing the company on consumer confusion. The senators wrote that while the heatmap contained aggregated and anonymized location data, the information could be easily cross-referenced with other publicly available data. They argued that many consumers were confused by Strava's opt-out provisions or unaware of what information was tracked, noting that privacy protections must be clear and understandable to be meaningful.

Strava moved to alter its platform rules. On 13 March 2018, the company announced significant updates to the Global Heatmap. Strava shifted the visualization to a monthly refresh schedule, ensuring that when an athlete opted out, their historical contributions would be removed at the next monthly cycle. To protect low-density regions, Strava implemented a new threshold rule: roads and trails with very little activity would not show any heat until several different athletes uploaded activities in that area. Finally, the company restricted detailed street-level zoom functionality on the public map to registered, authenticated users.

The mechanism

OWASP defines the category as Insecure design is a broad category representing different weaknesses, expressed as “missing or ineffective control design.”

The framework separates conceptual design errors from everyday programming bugs. It explains: We differentiate between design flaws and implementation defects for a reason, they have different root causes, take place at different times in the development process, and have different remediations. A secure design can still have implementation defects leading to vulnerabilities that may be exploited. An insecure design cannot be fixed by a perfect implementation as needed security controls were never created to defend against specific attacks.

Strava's heatmap represents a textbook example of insecure design with zero implementation defects. Not a single line of software failed to execute its intended specification. To understand why sensitive military outposts were unveiled to the world, one must examine two explicit mathematical decisions detailed by Strava engineer Drew Robb: rasterization geometry and color normalization.

In Strava's original heatmap, each GPS coordinate recorded by a user device was rasterized as a single isolated pixel. Because fitness trackers record at most one coordinate per second, runners moving quickly created discontinuous dotted lines separated by multi-pixel gaps. When Strava rebuilt the system in late 2017, it increased resolution to two meters per pixel and replaced point plotting with geometric interpolation. The new engine drew each activity as a continuous, pixel-perfect path connecting sequential GPS coordinates. This change produced elegant trails in suburban parks. But in combat zones, it meant that a solitary perimeter patrol or a soldier jogging laps around a wire fence was rendered as an unbroken, razor-sharp perimeter polygon.

The second decision amplified that geometry through statistical contrast. Mapping raw activity counts from an unbounded range onto a fixed display colormap presents a fundamental visualization challenge. If a platform applies a linear scale, high-density areas like Central Park saturate the color palette while rural trails remain invisible. Strava solved this by applying histogram equalization, using the cumulative distribution function of the raw heat values. Under this algorithm, the normalized value of any given pixel represented the exact percentage of pixels with a lower heat value, guaranteeing a perfectly uniform distribution of colors across the display map.

As Robb acknowledged, this normalization caused the map to convey maximum information about relative heat values, but it destroyed absolute quantitative meaning. The same bright color represented vastly different amounts of physical activity in different regions. In Manhattan, thousands of workouts were required to reach maximum heat. In rural Syria or southern Afghanistan, where background activity was essentially zero, twenty workouts from an overseas deployment occupied the top percentile of the local distribution. The mathematical function faithfully stretched those few dozen runs across the entire spectrum, rendering an obscure airstrip in glowing white and incandescent red.

Furthermore, the platform conflated individual privacy with geographic confidentiality. In their 2018 USENIX Security study, researchers led by Wajih Ul Hassan pointed out a vital architectural distinction: the identities of specific athletes were not directly leaked within the heatmap. The heatmap produced aggregate graphical tiles without user identifiers. Discovering an individual soldier's profile required separate attacks against adjacent platform features, such as uploading spoofed GPS activities to competitive segment leaderboards. The heatmap did not leak who was running; it leaked where anyone was running.

No automated code scanner could ever have detected this exposure. A static analysis tool examines source code for known defect patterns: buffer overflows, format string vulnerabilities, unescaped SQL queries, or improper authorization checks. A dynamic scanner tests running endpoints for injection flaws and malformed inputs. In Strava's pipeline, every database query was properly structured, every API request was authenticated, and every mathematical transformation executed without error. The flaw lay entirely in the business logic: the unverified architectural assumption that aggregating public data across geography is inherently safe, regardless of baseline population density.

The defense

When the military establishment responded to the heatmap disclosures, the popular press seized upon a simple narrative. On 7 August 2018, Ars Technica summarized the government's action with the headline "Pentagon tells troops: Turn off fitness tracker GPS when you head to warzones". News outlets described a sweeping Pentagon ban on wearable fitness devices. But the primary record reveals a far more structured and nuanced operational policy. The Pentagon did not ban the watch. It designed an operational threat model to manage the interface between commercial technology and military deployments.

On 3 August 2018, Deputy Secretary of Defense Patrick Shanahan signed a two-page memorandum titled "Use of Geolocation-Capable Devices, Applications, and Services." The policy established an immediate prohibition, but it drew precise boundaries. It prohibited DoD personnel from using "geolocation features and functionality on both non-government and government-issued devices, applications, and services while in locations designated as operational areas." Personnel could wear their digital watches, count their steps, and monitor their heart rates. What was forbidden in combat theaters was the continuous digital recording and external transmission of geographic coordinates.

Crucially, the memorandum built formal threat modeling directly into its operational exceptions. The policy recognized that operational realities vary across units and environments. Rather than imposing an inflexible, unenforceable blanket ban, the directive empowered Combatant Commanders or their designees to authorize non-government geolocation capabilities in operational areas after conducting a "threat-based comprehensive Operations Security (OPSEC) survey" under DoD Directive 5205.02E. For government-issued devices, commanders could grant authorization based on mission necessity after weighing potential OPSEC risks.

For military installations outside active operational areas, including domestic bases and major overseas hubs, the Pentagon rejected a single global mandate. Instead, the memorandum ordered component heads to consider inherent risks and apply a tiered structure for categorizing location and operations sensitivity. This operational zoning mirrored sound secure design principles: segregating environments based on exposure and protection requirements, rather than pretending that an infantry outpost in Helmand province and the Pentagon concourse in Arlington face identical threat environments.

The irony of the military directive was that defense planners had diagnosed the problem long before Strava's code went live. In July 2017, the Government Accountability Office published report GAO-17-668 on Internet of Things security in the military. GAO revealed that DoD's own 2016 IoT policy recommendations had warned that data collected from IoT devices and subsequently aggregated could inform adversaries about unit capabilities, troop deployments, or overtime work patterns. Yet GAO found that the comprehensive OPSEC surveys mandated by DoD Directive 5205.02E were simply not being conducted for IoT devices. Although DoD concurred with GAO's recommendations to conduct those assessments, eighteen months elapsed before the public heatmap scandal forced policy adoption.

Strava complemented the military's operational controls with architectural changes on the server side. As noted, the company established a multi-athlete threshold for sparse roads and trails, instituted monthly privacy update cycles, and restricted street-level zoom to authenticated users. When academic researchers discovered vulnerabilities in Strava's Privacy Zones—where users set an excluded radius around their homes—Strava adopted randomized spatial cloaking to prevent reverse-engineering of endpoints.

Yet an honest examination of the defense record reveals a profound evidentiary gap. While the Department of Defense documented its policy rules and Strava announced its platform updates, neither entity ever published empirical follow-up audits measuring their real-world impact. The public record does not contain data showing whether sensitive military installations actually vanished from subsequent open-source aggregations, or whether personnel compliance with the August 2018 geolocation directive was achieved. In secure design, as in warfare, publishing a policy is not the same as verifying its defense.

What you can now see

Tomorrow morning, look at the features in your application that aggregate user data to display summaries, leaderboards, or geographic distributions. Do not look for SQL injection, authentication flaws, or authorization bugs; assume your code executes flawlessly. Ask instead what a hostile observer can infer when your aggregate output is rendered across a non-uniform background. If your application computes relative metrics, normalizes scores across uneven groups, or displays activity density on a map, test what happens in the empty spaces. Where baseline activity is near zero, a handful of mundane actions can outline a private facility, unmask a discreet corporate transaction, or spotlight a user who believed they were invisible in a crowd. If your system assumes aggregation is the same thing as anonymity, you have an insecure design.
